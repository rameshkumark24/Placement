# Day 325

**L-325** · Load balancing — L4 vs L7, and the health-check death spiral

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> **⭐ A load balancer is the thing that decides which of your servers gets to fail.**
>
> Most of what matters is not the algorithm. It is what happens when a backend is *sick but not
> dead*, what happens during a deploy, and why a naive health check can convert one struggling node
> into a total outage.

---

# Part 1 — ⭐ L4 vs L7

| | ⭐ L4 (transport) | ⭐ L7 (application) |
|---|---|---|
| Decides on | ⭐ IP and port | ⭐ **URL, headers, cookies, method** |
| ⭐ **Sees the request?** | ⭐ **no — it forwards bytes** | ⭐ **yes — it terminates and re-issues** |
| ⭐ Throughput | ⭐ **very high — near line rate** | lower; it parses HTTP |
| ⭐ **Can it retry?** | ⭐ **no — the connection is the unit** | ⭐ **yes — idempotent retries on another backend** |
| TLS | ⭐ passes through (end-to-end) | ⭐ **terminates — enables inspection, needs re-encryption** |
| ⭐ Content routing | ⭐ **no** | ⭐ `/api/*` → A, `/img/*` → B; canary by header |
| ⭐ Sees per-request health | no — only connections | ⭐ **yes — status codes and latency** |
| Examples | ⭐ AWS NLB, IPVS | ⭐ ALB, NGINX, Envoy, HAProxy in HTTP mode |

> **⭐ The decision in one line: use L7 unless you need raw throughput or true end-to-end TLS.**
> Retries, path routing, canaries, per-request metrics and circuit breaking all require seeing the
> request — **and those are the features you actually want.**

⚠️ **L4 with long-lived connections balances connections, not load.** Ten clients each opening one
gRPC or WebSocket connection to five backends **pins the traffic**: a chatty client and an idle one
count the same. **This is the classic gRPC-behind-an-NLB problem, and the fix is L7 or client-side
balancing.**

---

# Part 2 — ⭐ Algorithms, and the one worth knowing

| Algorithm | ⭐ Behaviour | ⭐ When |
|---|---|---|
| Round robin | ⭐ ignores load entirely | uniform, short requests |
| ⭐ **Least connections** | ⭐ **tracks in-flight work — a decent default** | ⭐ variable request cost |
| Weighted | proportional to capacity | heterogeneous machines |
| ⭐ Least response time | fastest-observed backend | ⭐ **beware: a fast-failing node looks fastest** |
| ⭐ **Power of two choices** | ⭐⭐ **pick 2 at random, send to the less loaded** | ⭐⭐ **near-optimal, O(1), no global state** |
| ⭐ Consistent hashing | same key → same backend | ⭐ **cache affinity, sticky sharding** (Day 326) |

> **⭐ Power of two choices is the one to name.** Randomly sampling two backends and choosing the less
> loaded gets you almost all the benefit of full least-connections **with no coordination**, and it
> avoids the herd problem where every balancer simultaneously discovers the same "least loaded" node
> and stampedes it.

⚠️ **The least-response-time trap is worth saying:** a backend returning instant 500s has the best
response time, so a naive latency-based balancer **routes everything at the broken node.** Latency
must be combined with a success rate.

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Health checks and the death spiral

## ⭐ Passive vs active

| | ⭐ Active (the LB probes) | ⭐ Passive (observe real traffic) |
|---|---|---|
| Detects | ⭐ a dead process | ⭐ **a sick one — errors, slowness** |
| Cost | constant background load | free |
| ⭐ **Blind spot** | ⭐⭐ **`/health` returns 200 while every real request fails** | needs traffic to notice |
| ⭐ Best | ⭐ **both** | ⭐ **both** |

**⭐ The `/health` design decision, and it is the one that matters:**

| Check style | ⭐ Consequence |
|---|---|
| ⭐ "Am I alive?" (shallow) | ⭐ **cannot detect a broken dependency** |
| ⭐ **"Are my dependencies up?" (deep)** | ⭐⭐ **the database blips → EVERY instance fails its check → the entire fleet is removed → total outage** |
| ⭐⭐ **Liveness shallow, readiness considers dependencies, and the LB never removes 100%** | ⭐⭐ **the correct combination** |

> **⭐ This is Day 143 and Day 286's probe split, seen from the load balancer's side — and the extra
> rule is the important one: a load balancer must refuse to remove the last healthy backends.**
> If every node fails its check, the correct behaviour is to **send traffic anyway** ("fail open"),
> because a degraded service beats no service. Envoy calls this *panic mode*, and naming it is a
> strong detail.

## ⭐⭐ The death spiral

```
⭐ 5 backends at 70% utilisation. One gets slow and fails its health check.
⭐ → removed. 4 backends now carry the same load: ⭐ 87% utilisation.
⭐ → Day 322's curve: latency ~2.5× worse. A second node exceeds its timeout.
⭐ → removed. 3 backends: ⭐ 117% — over capacity.
⭐ → ⭐⭐ CASCADE. All five are eventually removed, and none of them was broken.
```

| ⭐ Defence | ⭐ Why |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Never remove more than X% of the pool** | ⭐⭐ **the single most important rule — breaks the cascade** |
| ⭐ Health checks must be cheap and not depend on shared resources | ⭐ or they fail together |
| ⭐ **Headroom** (Day 322) | ⭐ at 70%, losing one of five is survivable |
| ⭐ Load shedding at the backend | ⭐ **reject excess fast rather than dying slowly** — a fast 503 is better than a timeout |
| Slow start on re-entry | ⭐ a cold JVM given full traffic immediately just fails again |

# Part 4 — ⭐ The operational details that come up

## ⭐ Timeouts must be ordered

```
⭐ client timeout  >  LB timeout  >  application timeout  >  downstream timeout
⭐        30 s          25 s              20 s                    5 s
```

⚠️ **If the LB times out before the app does, the app keeps working on a request nobody is waiting
for** — burning a thread and a connection, and possibly retried on top. **Getting this order wrong
is a real and common source of load amplification** (Day 154's budget, at the infrastructure layer).

## ⭐ Retries, and why they are dangerous

| ⭐ Rule | ⭐ Why |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Only idempotent requests** | ⭐ a retried POST charges twice (Day 122) |
| ⭐ **Only on connection failure or 503** | ⭐ **never on a timeout — the work may still be running** |
| ⭐⭐ **Cap the retry budget fleet-wide** | ⭐⭐ **retries during an incident triple the load on a system already failing** |
| Jitter | or every client retries in lockstep |

> **⭐ "Retries turn a partial outage into a total one" is the sentence to have ready.** The fix is a
> retry *budget* — for example, retries may not exceed 10% of total requests — not per-request retry
> counts.

## ⭐ Draining, DNS, and where balancing happens

| Topic | ⭐ Point |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Connection draining** | ⭐ on deploy: stop new traffic, finish in-flight, then stop (Day 286) |
| ⭐ **DNS round robin** | ⭐ **poor balancing — clients cache beyond the TTL, and failover is slow** |
| ⭐ **Anycast / GSLB** | route to the nearest region — this is what solves geography, not the LB |
| ⭐ **Client-side balancing** | ⭐ the client knows all backends; **no extra hop, but every client needs discovery logic** — this is what a service mesh provides (Day 342) |
| ⭐ Sidecar/mesh | ⭐ L7 balancing, retries and breakers **without changing the application** |

⚠️ **DNS is not a load balancer.** TTLs are ignored by some resolvers and cached by others, so
removing a failed IP can take many minutes. **It is for coarse geographic routing, not for failover.**

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts |
|---|---|
| ⭐ L4 in front of gRPC/WebSockets | ⭐ **balances connections, not load — traffic pins** |
| ⭐ Least-response-time with no success rate | ⭐ **routes everything at the fastest-failing node** |
| ⭐⭐ **Deep dependency checks in the health endpoint** | ⭐⭐ **one database blip removes the whole fleet** |
| ⭐ No cap on removals | ⭐ **the death spiral** |
| Running at 90% with no headroom | losing one node cascades |
| ⭐ No slow start on re-entry | a cold instance fails immediately |
| ⭐ **LB timeout shorter than the app timeout** | ⭐ orphaned work, amplified load |
| ⭐ Retrying non-idempotent requests | duplicate side effects |
| ⭐ Retrying on timeouts | ⭐ the original may still be executing |
| ⭐ **No fleet-wide retry budget** | ⭐⭐ **retries convert a partial outage into a total one** |
| No connection draining | every deploy drops requests |
| DNS as the failover mechanism | minutes of downtime, by design |

## Interview questions

**Q: L4 or L7?**
L7 unless I need raw throughput or genuinely end-to-end TLS, because everything useful requires
seeing the request — path routing, canary by header, retries on another backend, per-request latency
and status metrics, circuit breaking. The specific trap is L4 in front of long-lived connections:
gRPC or WebSockets balance per connection, so a chatty client and an idle one count the same and
traffic pins to whichever backend they landed on.

**Q: Which balancing algorithm?**
Power of two choices — sample two backends at random and send to the less loaded. It gets nearly all
the benefit of least-connections with no global state, and it avoids every balancer simultaneously
discovering the same least-loaded node and stampeding it. I avoid pure least-response-time unless
it is combined with success rate, because a backend returning instant errors looks like the fastest
one.

**Q: What should a health check check?**
Liveness stays shallow — am I running — because a deep check makes a dependency blip remove every
instance simultaneously and turns a slow database into a total outage. Readiness may consider
dependencies, but the load balancer must refuse to remove the whole pool; if everything is failing,
sending traffic to degraded backends beats sending it nowhere. Envoy calls that panic mode.

**Q: Describe the health-check death spiral.**
Five backends at seventy percent. One slows and gets removed, so the remaining four carry the same
load at eighty-seven percent, where queueing makes latency two and a half times worse. A second
exceeds its timeout and is removed. Three now carry more than capacity, and the whole fleet is
removed even though nothing was actually broken. The defences are a cap on how much of the pool can
be removed, real headroom, cheap health checks, load shedding so a backend fails fast rather than
slowly, and slow start when a node returns.

**Q: How do you handle retries safely?**
Only idempotent requests, only on connection failures or explicit 503s — never on timeouts, because
the original work may still be running — with jitter, and with a fleet-wide retry budget rather than
a per-request count. Retries are what turn a partial outage into a total one: the system is already
failing, and every client is now sending three times the traffic.

## Mini task

1. Put an L7 balancer in front of two instances. **Route `/api` and `/static` to different backends
   and canary by header.**
2. ⭐ Put an L4 balancer in front of a gRPC or WebSocket service with two clients of very different
   volume. **Measure the imbalance.** Switch to L7 and measure again.
3. Implement **power of two choices** in a small simulation against round robin and least
   connections, with variable request costs. **Compare p99.**
4. ⭐⭐ **Reproduce the death spiral**: five simulated backends at 70%, a health check that fails on
   latency, and no removal cap. Then add the cap and re-run.
5. Make `/health` check the database. **Stop the database and watch every instance leave the pool.**
   Split into liveness and readiness and repeat.
6. ⭐ Set the LB timeout **below** the application timeout and observe orphaned work under load. Fix
   the ordering.
7. ⭐ Enable retries with no budget, fail 30% of requests, and **measure total load amplification.**
   Add a 10% budget and measure again.
8. Deploy under load with and without **connection draining**; count dropped requests both times.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Three minutes:** *"One backend gets slow. Walk me through what your infrastructure does — and how
that becomes an outage."*

**⭐ The marker: you tell the death-spiral story with numbers**, then give the defences in priority
order — cap the removals first, then headroom, then load shedding, then slow start. **And you add the
retry amplification on top**: as the fleet degrades, clients retry, tripling the load on the nodes
still standing.

**⭐ Anyone can say "the load balancer removes the unhealthy node". Explaining how that correct
behaviour destroys the system is the answer that demonstrates you have watched it happen.**
