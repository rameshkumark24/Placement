# Day 466

**L-466** · Communication under evaluation — thinking aloud, "I don't know", and recovery

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> **⭐⭐ The interviewer is not grading your solution. They are filling in a scorecard, from memory,
> twenty minutes after you leave — and they can only write down what you said out loud.**
>
> Today: what an interviewer actually records, **thinking aloud as a skill rather than a personality
> trait**, how to say "I don't know" so it scores positively, recovering from a wrong turn without
> losing the room, and clock management.

---

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ What the interviewer actually writes down

```
⭐⭐ A typical scorecard, filled in AFTER you leave, from a page of scrappy notes:
⭐   ⭐ Problem solving   — did they find a path, or did I have to give it?
⭐   ⭐⭐ COMMUNICATION    — ⭐⭐ could I follow their thinking WITHOUT ASKING?
⭐   ⭐ Coding            — does it run, is it clean, did they test it
⭐   ⭐ Depth             — did they know why, not just what
⭐   ⭐⭐ SIGNAL/NO SIGNAL — ⭐⭐ "I couldn't tell" scores the same as "no" in most companies
```

| ⭐⭐ The consequence people miss | |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Silent correct work scores WORSE than narrated imperfect work** | ⭐⭐ they cannot write down what they did not hear |
| ⭐ A brilliant insight you had at minute 12 and mentioned at minute 40 | ⭐ was not observed |
| ⭐⭐ **"Strong hire" requires evidence, not absence of failure** | ⭐⭐ neutral rounds are rejections |
| ⭐ Your job | ⭐⭐ **make it easy for a tired person to write good things about you** |

> **⭐ Reframe the whole hour with one sentence: you are not solving a problem, you are producing
> evidence.** The solution is the *by-product*. **Everything below follows from taking that literally.**

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ Thinking aloud, as a technique

```
⭐⭐ NARRATION HAS THREE MODES. ⭐ Most people only use the third, and only at the end.
⭐   1. ⭐⭐ INTENT      "I'm going to start by clarifying the input constraints."
⭐   2. ⭐⭐ REASONING   "A hash map gets me O(1) lookups; the cost is O(n) memory — I'll take that."
⭐   3. ⭐ MECHANICS   "...now I loop over the array..."   ⭐⭐ ← the least valuable one
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ Narrate INTENT and REASONING. ⭐ Nobody needs you to read your own code aloud.
```

| ⭐ Phrase | ⭐ What it buys |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **"Let me restate the problem to check I have it."** | ⭐⭐ catches a misread before it costs 20 minutes |
| ⭐⭐ **"Two approaches — X is O(n log n), Y is O(n) with O(n) space. I'll take Y unless memory is tight."** | ⭐⭐ **shows the trade, invites a steer** |
| ⭐ "I'm going to write the brute force first so we have something correct." | ⭐ signals pragmatism |
| ⭐⭐ **"I'm not sure about that — let me reason it out."** | ⭐⭐ honest, and it keeps you talking |
| ⭐ "That's a good catch, let me fix it." | ⭐ receiving correction well is scored |
| ⭐⭐ **"Before I code, does this approach look reasonable to you?"** | ⭐⭐ **the highest-value sentence in the hour** |

⚠️ **That last one is worth naming separately.** Checking the approach before coding **costs fifteen
seconds and can save twenty-five minutes**, and interviewers almost always answer honestly — they want
you to succeed, and they are usually not allowed to volunteer a steer you did not ask for.

## ⭐ Clarifying questions, and how many

| ⭐ Ask about | ⭐ Do not ask |
|---|---|
| ⭐ Input size and ranges | ⭐ things you can reasonably assume |
| ⭐⭐ **Edge cases: empty, null, duplicates, negatives** | ⭐ five minutes of trivia |
| ⭐ Whether the input can be modified | ⭐ questions designed to look thorough |
| ⭐⭐ **What "optimal" means here — time or memory** | |

**⭐ Two to four questions, in the first two minutes, then move.** More than that reads as stalling;
**zero reads as someone who will build the wrong thing.**

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ "I don't know" — and how to say it well

```
⭐⭐ THE THREE VERSIONS, in scoring order:

⭐ ❌ ⭐⭐ BLUFFING — inventing a plausible-sounding answer
⭐    ⇒ ⭐⭐ the single most damaging thing you can do, because it is DETECTABLE
⭐      and it retroactively devalues everything you said confidently earlier.

⭐ ⚠️ FLAT — "I don't know."
⭐    ⇒ honest, but it ends the exchange and produces no signal.

⭐ ✅ ⭐⭐ THE GOOD VERSION — three parts:
⭐    ⭐ 1. name the boundary   "I haven't used Cassandra in production."
⭐    ⭐⭐ 2. reason from what you DO know
⭐         "But it's a Dynamo-style store, so I'd expect tunable quorums and
⭐          last-write-wins conflict resolution, which means clock skew matters."
⭐    ⭐ 3. say how you'd close it   "I'd check the consistency-level defaults first."
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ You just scored on reasoning, honesty AND judgement — from a knowledge gap.
```

> **⭐⭐ Interviewers respect the boundary far more than the bluff, and it is not close.** A candidate who
> says "I don't know, but here is how I would reason about it" is describing exactly how they will behave
> on the job — **which is the entire point of the exercise.**

| ⭐ Also true and worth internalising | |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **You do not need to solve everything to pass** | ⭐⭐ many strong hires did not finish the optimal solution |
| ⭐ A brute force plus a stated optimisation path | ⭐ frequently enough |
| ⭐⭐ **Nobody expects perfection under a 45-minute clock** | ⭐ they expect *legible* thinking |

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ Recovery, and the clock

```
⭐⭐ YOU REALISE AT MINUTE 25 THAT YOUR APPROACH IS WRONG.
⭐ ❌ ⭐⭐ push on hoping it works out         ⇒ ⭐⭐ the worst option; you lose the round silently
⭐ ❌ go quiet and stare                      ⇒ no signal
⭐ ✅ ⭐⭐ SAY IT: "This doesn't handle the case where the interval is nested. That breaks my
⭐      approach — let me back up. Sorting by start time and merging handles it; that's O(n log n)."
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ Catching your OWN error and recovering cleanly is a POSITIVE signal.
⭐   ⭐⭐ It is close to the strongest thing you can demonstrate, because it is what the job is.
```

| ⭐ Situation | ⭐ Move |
|---|---|
| ⭐ The interviewer gives a hint | ⭐⭐ **take it immediately and gratefully.** Resisting a hint is scored badly |
| ⭐ You are stuck for 90 seconds | ⭐⭐ **say where you are stuck** — that is a hint request, and it is legitimate |
| ⭐ You made a typo they point out | ⭐ fix it, say thanks, move on — do not apologise three times |
| ⭐⭐ **You genuinely cannot solve it** | ⭐⭐ get the brute force down, state the optimal direction, **finish the hour well** |
| ⭐ They go quiet | ⭐ that is usually note-taking, not disapproval — keep going |

## ⭐⭐ The 45-minute clock

```
⭐ 0–3    ⭐ clarify. 2–4 questions. restate the problem.
⭐ 3–8    ⭐⭐ approach OUT LOUD: brute force, then the better one, with complexity.
⭐        ⭐⭐ "Does this look reasonable before I code?"
⭐ 8–30   ⭐ code, narrating INTENT not mechanics.
⭐ 30–38  ⭐⭐ TEST IT YOURSELF — walk a real example, then edge cases. ⭐⭐ Before they ask.
⭐ 38–43  ⭐ complexity, and what you would improve with more time.
⭐ 43–45  ⭐ your questions.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE MOST COMMONLY SKIPPED BLOCK IS 30–38, and it is the cheapest signal in the hour.
```

⚠️ **Testing your own code before being asked is disproportionately valuable.** It converts "wrote
something plausible" into "verified it", **it is what a professional does**, and it very often finds the
off-by-one that would otherwise be found for you.

| ⭐ Remote mechanics that quietly cost points | |
|---|---|
| ⭐ Camera on, at eye level, decent light | ⭐ you are being remembered as a person |
| ⭐⭐ **Test the shared editor beforehand** | ⭐⭐ ten minutes lost to setup is ten minutes of your round |
| ⭐ Wired connection or good wifi; a phone as backup | ⭐ and say so if it drops |
| ⭐ A glass of water, a pen, no notifications | ⭐⭐ a notification sound is heard |
| ⭐ Do not read from notes | ⭐⭐ it is visible in your eye movement |

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Solving silently** | ⭐⭐ they cannot write down what they did not hear |
| ⭐ Narrating mechanics instead of reasoning | ⭐ reading your code aloud adds nothing |
| ⭐ Zero clarifying questions | ⭐ you may build the wrong thing |
| ⭐ Ten clarifying questions | ⭐ reads as stalling |
| ⭐⭐ **Not checking the approach before coding** | ⭐⭐ fifteen seconds that can save twenty-five minutes |
| ⭐⭐ **Bluffing** | ⭐⭐ detectable, and it devalues everything you said before |
| ⭐ Flat "I don't know" with no reasoning | ⭐ honest but produces no signal |
| ⭐⭐ **Pushing on with a broken approach** | ⭐⭐ you lose the round silently |
| ⭐ Going quiet when stuck | ⭐⭐ say where you are stuck instead |
| ⭐ Resisting a hint | ⭐⭐ scored badly, always |
| ⭐ Over-apologising | it costs time and confidence |
| ⭐⭐ **Skipping the test block** | ⭐⭐ the cheapest signal in the hour |
| ⭐ Untested screen-share setup | ten minutes of your own round |
| Assuming silence means disapproval | it is note-taking |

## Interview questions

**Q: Why does thinking aloud matter so much?**
Because the interviewer fills in a scorecard from memory after I leave, and they can only write down what
they heard. Silent correct work scores worse than narrated imperfect work — an insight I had at minute
twelve and mentioned at minute forty was not observed. And in most companies "I couldn't tell" is scored
the same as no, so a neutral round is a rejection. The useful reframe is that I am not solving a problem,
I am producing evidence, and the solution is the by-product.

**Q: What should you narrate?**
Intent and reasoning, not mechanics. Saying "now I loop over the array" adds nothing, because they can
read the code. Saying "a hash map gets me constant-time lookups at the cost of linear memory, and I will
take that trade unless memory is constrained" is the thing being scored. The single highest-value
sentence in the hour is asking whether the approach looks reasonable before I start coding — it costs
fifteen seconds, interviewers almost always answer honestly, and it can save twenty-five minutes of
building the wrong thing.

**Q: How do you handle a question you cannot answer?**
Never by bluffing, because it is detectable and it retroactively devalues everything I said confidently
earlier. But a flat "I don't know" produces no signal either. The version that works has three parts:
name the boundary, reason from what I do know, and say how I would close the gap. "I haven't run
Cassandra in production, but it is Dynamo-style, so I would expect tunable quorums and last-write-wins,
which means clock skew matters — I would check the consistency level defaults first." That scores on
reasoning, honesty and judgement, out of a knowledge gap.

**Q: You realise at minute twenty-five that your approach is wrong. What do you do?**
Say so immediately and back up. Pushing on hoping it works is the worst option because the round is then
lost silently, and going quiet produces no signal at all. Naming the specific case that breaks it and
proposing the alternative with its complexity is close to the strongest thing I can demonstrate, because
catching my own error and recovering cleanly is literally what the job is. Related: if the interviewer
offers a hint I take it immediately and gratefully — resisting a hint is scored badly every time.

**Q: How do you use the forty-five minutes?**
Roughly three minutes clarifying and restating, five on approach out loud with complexity and an explicit
check before coding, about twenty coding while narrating intent, then — the block most people skip —
eight minutes testing it myself with a real example and the edge cases, before being asked. Then
complexity and what I would improve, then my questions. Testing my own code is disproportionately
valuable: it converts "wrote something plausible" into "verified it", and it usually finds the off-by-one
that would otherwise be found for me.

## Mini task

1. ⭐⭐ Solve a medium problem while **recording yourself talking.** Watch it back with the sound only —
   could you follow the thinking?
2. ⭐ Count your **narration mode**: how much was mechanics? Redo it narrating intent and reasoning.
3. ⭐⭐ Practise the sentence **"Before I code, does this approach look reasonable?"** until it is
   automatic.
4. ⭐ Do a problem with **exactly three clarifying questions.** Then one with zero, and feel the
   difference.
5. ⭐⭐ Have someone ask you **five things you genuinely do not know.** Answer all five with the
   **three-part** structure.
6. ⭐⭐ Deliberately start a problem with the **wrong approach**, then practise **announcing the failure and
   backing up** cleanly.
7. ⭐ Have someone give you a **hint you do not need** and practise taking it gracefully.
8. ⭐⭐ Run a full **45-minute clock** with a timer visible, hitting every block — especially **30–38**.
9. ⭐ Test your **remote setup** end to end: editor, camera, audio, backup connection.
10. ⭐⭐ Ask a friend to **score you as an interviewer would** — signal or no signal, per category, with
    one sentence each.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes:** narrate the *first five minutes* of a problem you have never seen, out loud, recorded.

**⭐ You are not solving it. You are practising the opening: restate the problem in your own words, ask two
or three real constraint questions, name the brute force with its complexity, name a better approach with
its complexity and the trade between them — and then ask whether that looks reasonable before writing
anything.**

**⭐ Play it back and ask the only question that matters: could a tired person, taking notes, follow what
you were thinking without asking you a single question? If not, that is the gap — and it is a bigger one
than any algorithm you have not yet learned.**
