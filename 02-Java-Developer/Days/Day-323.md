# Day 323 ⭐

**L-323** · ⭐ Back-of-the-envelope estimation — the numbers that decide the architecture

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> **⭐ Estimation is not a maths test. It is the step that turns "design a URL shortener" into a
> specific design, because the answer at 100 requests per second is a completely different system
> from the answer at 100,000.**
>
> Five minutes of arithmetic, done out loud, eliminates half the architecture space — **and skipping
> it is why candidates end up defending a Kafka cluster for a workload that fits on a laptop.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐ The numbers you must know without thinking

```
⭐ SECONDS
⭐   1 day          = 86,400 s   ⭐ ≈ 100,000 — use this
⭐   1 month        ≈ 2.5 M s
⭐   1 year         ≈ 30 M s

⭐ THE SHORTCUT THAT DOES 80% OF THE WORK
⭐   1 million requests/day  ≈ 12 rps      ⭐ (1M ÷ 86,400)
⭐   100 M requests/day      ≈ 1,200 rps
⭐   1 billion/day           ≈ 12,000 rps

⭐ POWERS OF TWO
⭐   2^10 = 1 K   2^20 = 1 M   2^30 = 1 G   2^40 = 1 T
⭐   ⇒ a 4-byte int × 1 billion rows ≈ 4 GB

⭐ TYPICAL SIZES
⭐   UUID 16 B · timestamp 8 B · a tweet ~300 B · a JSON row ~1 KB
⭐   a photo ~2 MB · a minute of 1080p video ~50 MB
```

| ⭐ Also memorise | |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **One commodity server** | ⭐ **~10k QPS for simple work; 1k–5k for real work** |
| ⭐ **One PostgreSQL instance** | ⭐ **a few thousand writes/s, tens of thousands of cached reads/s** |
| ⭐ **One Redis instance** | ⭐ **~100k ops/s** (Day 203) |
| ⭐ **Peak ÷ average** | ⭐ **2× to 3× — always apply it** |
| ⭐ Read:write ratio | ⭐ **the first thing to ask** — 100:1 changes everything |

---

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ The method: seven steps, five minutes

```
⭐ 1. USERS        DAU, and actions per user per day
⭐ 2. QPS          actions/day ÷ 86,400   →  ⭐ × 2–3 for PEAK
⭐ 3. READ:WRITE   ⭐ ask; assume 100:1 for social, 10:1 for commerce
⭐ 4. STORAGE      bytes/record × records/day × retention  ⭐ × replication × overhead
⭐ 5. BANDWIDTH    bytes/request × QPS  (in and out separately)
⭐ 6. MEMORY       ⭐ the 80/20 rule — cache 20% of data for 80% of reads
⭐ 7. MACHINES     QPS ÷ per-server capacity ÷ 0.7 utilisation (Day 322)
```

**⭐ Then — and this is the step that scores — say what the numbers decided:**

> *"1,200 writes per second and 4 TB a year: that is comfortably one PostgreSQL primary with read
> replicas, so I will not shard. 120,000 reads per second is not, so there is a cache in front, and
> at a 95% hit rate the database sees 6,000 reads per second, which is fine."*

## ⭐ Worked example 1 — a URL shortener

```
⭐ 100 M new URLs/day, read:write 100:1

⭐ WRITES   100 M ÷ 86,400 ≈ 1,200/s        ⭐ peak ≈ 3,000/s
⭐ READS    100× → 120,000/s                 ⭐ peak ≈ 300,000/s
⭐ STORAGE  500 B/record × 100 M = 50 GB/day
⭐          × 365 × 5 years ≈ 90 TB          ⭐ × 3 replicas ≈ 270 TB
⭐ CACHE    20% of a day's hot URLs ≈ 10 GB  ⭐ ← fits in ONE Redis node
⭐ KEYSPACE 100 M/day × 5 yr = 180 B URLs    ⭐ base62^7 = 3.5 T  ⇒ ⭐ 7 chars is enough
```

| ⭐ Decided | ⭐ Because |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Cache is mandatory** | ⭐ 300k reads/s cannot hit a database |
| ⭐ **The write path is easy** | 3,000/s is one node |
| ⭐ **Storage forces sharding** | ⭐ **90 TB is not one machine** |
| ⭐ **Short code = 7 base62 chars** | ⭐ **the arithmetic answered a design question directly** |

> **⭐ That last row is the point of the whole exercise.** "How long is the short code?" is not a taste
> question — **it is `log_62(total URLs over the lifetime)`, and you can compute it in ten seconds.**

## ⭐ Worked example 2 — a Twitter-style feed

```
⭐ 300 M DAU · 2 tweets/day avg · 100 feed views/day

⭐ WRITES  600 M ÷ 86,400 ≈ 7,000/s          ⭐ peak ≈ 20,000/s
⭐ READS   30 B ÷ 86,400 ≈ 350,000/s         ⭐ peak ≈ 1 M/s   ⭐⭐ read:write = 50:1
⭐ STORAGE 300 B × 600 M = 180 GB/day ≈ 65 TB/yr (text only)
⭐ FAN-OUT avg 200 followers × 7,000 tweets/s = ⭐⭐ 1.4 M feed writes/s
⭐          ⭐ and one celebrity with 100 M followers = 100 M writes for ONE tweet
```

| ⭐ Decided | |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Fan-out on write** for normal users | reads become one lookup |
| ⭐⭐ **Fan-out on read for celebrities** | ⭐⭐ **the hybrid — and the estimate is what forced it** |
| Feeds live in memory | 1 M reads/s is not a database |
| Text is small; media dominates | ⭐ **object storage + CDN, not the database** |

> **⭐ The celebrity number is the whole design.** You did not need a diagram to discover the hybrid
> fan-out model — **one multiplication produced it**, and saying "100 million writes for one tweet is
> why the celebrity path must differ" is the strongest possible justification.

# Part 3 — ⭐ Storage arithmetic people get wrong

**⭐ Raw data size is not the number you need. Four multipliers:**

| Multiplier | ⭐ Typical | ⭐ Why |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Replication** | ⭐ **×3** | you keep copies |
| ⭐ **Indexes** | ⭐ **×1.3–2** | ⭐ **routinely forgotten and often as big as the table** |
| ⭐ Row/page overhead | ×1.2 | headers, alignment, fill factor (Day 186) |
| ⭐ **Backups & headroom** | ⭐ **×1.5–2** | ⭐ you cannot run a disk at 95% |
| ⭐ **Combined** | ⭐⭐ **≈ ×6 to ×10** | ⭐⭐ **the realistic factor** |

```
⭐ 90 TB of raw records is really 500 TB–900 TB of provisioned storage.
⭐ Saying "×3 for replicas, plus indexes and headroom, call it half a petabyte"
⭐ is the answer of someone who has provisioned storage.
```

⚠️ **And ask what retention actually is.** Five years of raw events is often a made-up requirement;
**90 days hot plus cold archive in object storage changes the number by an order of magnitude** —
and asking is worth more than computing.

## ⭐ Bandwidth, and the number that surprises people

```
⭐ 300,000 reads/s × 500 B = 150 MB/s  ⭐ ≈ 1.2 Gbps — fine
⭐ 10,000 video views/s × 5 Mbps       ⭐ = 50 Gbps  ⭐⭐ ⇒ CDN is not optional, it is the system
```

> **⭐ For any media system, egress bandwidth is the dominant cost and the dominant constraint.** The
> estimate is what tells you the architecture is "a CDN with an origin", not "a service that returns
> files".

# Part 4 — ⭐ How to do this in an interview

| ⭐ Do | ⭐ Don't |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Round aggressively** — 86,400 → 100,000 | compute 86,400 exactly |
| ⭐ **State assumptions out loud** | invent numbers silently |
| ⭐ **Ask for DAU and read:write** | assume |
| ⭐ **Work in orders of magnitude** | ⭐ **precision theatre — "1,157 QPS"** |
| ⭐⭐ **Say what each number DECIDED** | ⭐⭐ **compute, then ignore the results** |
| ⭐ Apply peak multipliers | design for the average and fall over at 8pm |
| Sanity-check against reality | ⭐ produce a number implying more storage than exists |

> **⭐ The single most common failure is doing the arithmetic and then designing as if you hadn't.**
> Every number should eliminate an option: **"6,000 database reads per second after caching — that is
> one primary, so no sharding on the read path."** That sentence is the deliverable, not the 6,000.

⚠️ **Second most common: forgetting peak.** Traffic is not uniform. **A day's average of 1,200 rps
means an evening peak near 3,000, and a launch or a flash sale is another order of magnitude.**

---

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts |
|---|---|
| ⭐ Skipping estimation entirely | ⭐ **you design for an unknown scale and defend the wrong system** |
| ⭐ Precision to four digits | ⭐ signals inexperience; the inputs were guesses |
| ⭐ Designing for the average | ⭐ the evening peak is 2–3× |
| ⭐ Raw storage with no multipliers | ⭐ **out by 6–10×** |
| ⭐ Forgetting index size | often as large as the data |
| ⭐ **Computing then not concluding** | ⭐⭐ **the numbers were the point** |
| Not asking the read:write ratio | the single most architecture-changing input |
| Ignoring the celebrity/hotspot case | the average hides the design problem |
| ⭐ Media systems sized on requests, not bandwidth | ⭐ egress is the real constraint |
| Accepting a retention requirement unquestioned | an order of magnitude, for free |

## Interview questions

**Q: Why estimate at all?**
Because the scale decides the architecture. A thousand requests a second is one server and a
database; a hundred thousand is caching, sharding and a CDN, and defending the second design for the
first workload is how you fail the round. Five minutes of arithmetic eliminates most of the option
space, and every number should rule something out.

**Q: How do you get from daily users to QPS?**
Actions per user per day times users, divided by roughly a hundred thousand seconds in a day, then
multiplied by two or three for peak. A million requests a day is about twelve a second — that ratio
alone answers a surprising number of questions before any real work.

**Q: How much storage for a hundred million records a day at half a kilobyte each?**
Fifty gigabytes a day raw, about eighteen terabytes a year, ninety over five. Then multiply: three
for replication, another thirty to a hundred percent for indexes, plus overhead and headroom — call
it six to ten times raw, so roughly half a petabyte provisioned. And before accepting that, I would
ask whether five years must be hot, because ninety days hot plus cold archive changes it by an order
of magnitude.

**Q: What does the estimate tell you about a Twitter feed?**
That fan-out is the problem. Seven thousand tweets a second times two hundred average followers is
about 1.4 million feed writes a second, which is workable — but a celebrity with a hundred million
followers is a hundred million writes for one tweet, which is not. That single multiplication is what
forces the hybrid model: fan-out on write for normal accounts, fan-out on read for large ones. The
arithmetic produced the design.

**Q: What is the most common estimation mistake?**
Doing it and then ignoring it. The value is in the conclusion — "six thousand database reads a second
after caching, so one primary, so no sharding" — not the number. After that, forgetting peak, and
forgetting that raw data size is not provisioned storage.

## Mini task

1. **Memorise the numbers block.** Then, with no calculator, answer: 500 M requests/day is how many
   rps? A billion 1 KB rows is how much storage, provisioned?
2. ⭐ **Estimate your flagship**: DAU, QPS, peak, storage per year with multipliers, cache size.
   **Compare with what you actually provisioned.** Explain any gap over 3×.
3. Do the **URL shortener** estimate from scratch in five minutes, timed, out loud. **Derive the
   short-code length from the keyspace.**
4. Do the **Twitter feed** estimate and **derive the hybrid fan-out** from the celebrity number.
5. ⭐ Estimate a **video platform**: 1 M daily viewers, 30 minutes each at 5 Mbps. **Compute egress
   and conclude what the architecture must be.**
6. Take three estimates and **write the one sentence each number decided.** Any number with no
   sentence was wasted work.
7. ⭐ Measure your real per-server capacity with a load test, then **redo your machine count using
   70% utilisation.**
8. Estimate one system **twice** — once at 10× the users — and **list what changed.** That difference
   is what scale means.

# 🚪 Exit questions

1. Give seconds per day, per month, per year, rounded.
2. What is a million requests a day in rps?
3. Give the seven estimation steps in order.
4. Why is read:write the first question?
5. Give the four storage multipliers and the combined factor.
6. Why is the peak multiplier not optional?
7. Derive a short code length from a keyspace requirement.
8. Show how the celebrity number forces hybrid fan-out.
9. Why is bandwidth the constraint for media systems?
10. Give the five interview rules for doing this out loud.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Five minutes, recorded:** *"Design a photo-sharing app for 50 million daily users."* — **do only
the estimate.** Users, QPS, peak, storage with multipliers, bandwidth, cache size. Stop there.

**⭐ The marker: after every number, one sentence saying what it ruled out.** *"Two hundred terabytes
a year of media — that is object storage plus a CDN, not a database, and it means the metadata
service and the media path are separate systems."*

**⭐ Someone who produces numbers is doing arithmetic. Someone who produces eliminations is
designing.**
