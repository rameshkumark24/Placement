# Day 350

**L-350** · Design 6 — a notification system at scale

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** BUILD

> **⭐ Day 318 designed the classes. At scale the problem changes shape entirely: a single product
> decision — "notify everyone" — becomes ten million sends in a window you do not control, against
> providers who rate-limit you.**
>
> The deep dive is the broadcast, and **the constraint that governs everything is that you do not own
> the delivery infrastructure.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐ Requirements and estimates

| ⭐ Functional | ⭐ Non-functional |
|---|---|
| Multi-channel: push, email, SMS, in-app | ⭐⭐ **transactional notifications: < 30 s end to end** |
| ⭐ **User preferences per category** | ⭐ broadcasts: minutes are fine |
| ⭐ Templates per (event, channel, locale) | ⭐⭐ **no duplicates for critical messages** |
| ⭐ **Scheduling and quiet hours** | at-least-once delivery, deduped |
| ⭐ **Broadcasts to millions** | ⭐ a failing channel must not block the others |
| Delivery tracking, bounces, unsubscribes | availability: **degrade, never drop** |

```
⭐ ESTIMATE
⭐   Steady state: 10M notifications/day ⇒ ⭐ ~120/s — trivial
⭐   ⭐⭐ BROADCAST: 20M users × 1 push, to be delivered in ~10 minutes
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ 20M ÷ 600 s = 33,000/s — ⭐ 275× the steady-state rate
⭐   Storage: 10M × 500 B × 90 days ⇒ ~450 GB of delivery records
```

> **⭐ The single most important number: the peak is nearly three hundred times the average**, and it
> arrives because a human clicked "send". **That ratio is the design** — you cannot provision for it
> continuously, and you cannot let it starve the transactional path.

# Part 2 — ⭐ The pipeline

```
⭐ EVENT ──► ROUTER ──► [preferences · quiet hours · dedup · rate limits] ──► ⭐ PER-CHANNEL QUEUES
⭐                                                                                    │
⭐   ⭐ SEPARATE QUEUES BY PRIORITY, not a priority field (Day 318)                     ▼
⭐   ⭐ transactional-push · transactional-email · bulk-push · bulk-email          WORKERS ──► PROVIDERS
⭐                                                                                    │
⭐                                                                         delivery records ◄─ webhooks
```

| ⭐ Decision | ⭐ Reason |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **A queue per (channel × priority)** | ⭐⭐ **a 20M broadcast must not delay a password reset** |
| ⭐ Workers scale per queue | bulk workers scale out for the broadcast, then back in |
| ⭐ **Delivery status per (notification, channel)** | Day 318 — "email delivered, SMS retrying" is the normal case |
| Router is stateless | ⭐ all state is in the queue and the store |

⚠️ **A priority *field* in one queue does not work at this ratio.** With twenty million bulk messages
enqueued, **a high-priority flag still leaves the password reset behind whatever the workers are
currently processing** — and with a batch of 500, that could be minutes. **Separate queues with
separate worker pools is the only thing that delivers on the promise.**

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ The deep dive: the broadcast

## ⭐ Step 1 — do not build the recipient list in one place

```
⭐ NAIVE: SELECT user_id FROM users WHERE ... → 20M rows into memory → enqueue 20M messages
⭐   ✗ ⭐ one machine holds 20M ids   ✗ it takes minutes before ANY message is sent
⭐   ✗ ⭐⭐ a crash halfway leaves you with no idea what was sent

⭐⭐ CHUNKED FAN-OUT:
⭐   1. Create a broadcast record: { id, template, audienceQuery, status: PENDING }
⭐   2. ⭐ Split the audience into ranges (by user id) → enqueue ~20,000 CHUNK jobs of 1,000 users
⭐   3. ⭐ Each chunk worker resolves its users, applies preferences, and enqueues sends
⭐   4. ⭐⭐ Each chunk is idempotent and independently retryable — a crash loses one chunk, not the job
```

| ⭐ Property | |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **First message goes out in seconds** | not after a twenty-minute query |
| ⭐⭐ **Progress is observable** | ⭐ "14,200 of 20,000 chunks complete" — **the operator can see it** |
| ⭐ Restartable | completed chunks are marked; a retry skips them |
| ⭐ **Cancellable** | ⭐⭐ a marketing mistake must be stoppable mid-flight — check a kill flag per chunk |

> **⭐ Cancellation is the detail that shows product sense.** Broadcasts are sent by humans, and humans
> send the wrong one. **A design where "stop" is impossible after the first second is a design that
> will one day email twenty million people the wrong thing.**

## ⭐⭐ Step 2 — provider rate limits govern everything

```
⭐ You do not own delivery. ⭐ Push providers, SMTP relays and SMS gateways all impose quotas.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ Your send rate is capped by the PROVIDER, not by your workers.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐ Adding workers past that cap produces 429s, retries, and MORE load.
```

| ⭐ Requirement | ⭐ Design |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Rate-limit yourself, per provider** | ⭐⭐ **Day 336's outbound limiting — the case people forget** |
| ⭐ Batch where the API allows it | ⭐ push APIs take hundreds of tokens per call — **a 100× reduction in requests** |
| ⭐ **Multiple providers with weights** | ⭐ failover, and more aggregate capacity |
| ⭐ Circuit breaker per provider | Day 339 — stop hammering a failing gateway |
| ⭐ **Backpressure into the queue** | ⭐ workers slow down; the queue absorbs it — **which is what the queue is for** |

> **⭐ This is where the design differs most from a naive one: the bottleneck is external and fixed.**
> Autoscaling workers does not increase throughput — **it converts a queue into a stream of 429s.**
> Saying that unprompted is the strongest moment available in this problem.

## ⭐ Step 3 — quiet hours across a global audience

```
⭐ A 09:00 broadcast is 09:00 WHERE? ⭐ 20M users across 24 timezones.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ Either send at 09:00 local per user — which spreads the send over 24 HOURS —
⭐    or send once globally and suppress recipients currently in quiet hours.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐ THE PRODUCT MUST DECIDE. Ask.
```

**⭐ If it is local-time delivery**, the send becomes a **scheduled** problem: bucket users by
timezone offset and enqueue 24 (or 38, with half-hour offsets) waves. **That completely changes the
capacity profile — from one 33,000/s spike to a gentle rolling load** — and it is usually the better
product answer too.

# Part 4 — ⭐ Dedup, tracking, failure

| Concern | ⭐ Design |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Deduplication** | ⭐⭐ **an idempotency key on (eventId, userId, channel)**, stored with a TTL longer than the max retry window (Day 335) |
| ⭐ Retries | exponential backoff **with jitter**; a per-channel DLQ with an owner |
| ⭐ **Delivery tracking** | provider webhooks: delivered, bounced, opened — **and hard bounces must suppress the address permanently** |
| ⭐ Unsubscribes | ⭐ honoured **before** the send, at chunk resolution — and legally required for email |
| Digests | ⭐ a windowed buffer per (user, category), flushed on a timer — **with an urgent bypass** |
| ⭐ **Preference checks** | at chunk time, not at broadcast creation — **preferences change between the two** |

⚠️ **Hard bounces are not a retry case.** Retrying a permanently invalid address damages your sender
reputation, which degrades deliverability for *everyone* — **an email system's most valuable and
most fragile asset.** Suppress on hard bounce; retry only on soft.

```
⭐ MONITOR: ⭐ end-to-end latency by priority class · per-provider success rate and 429s
⭐          ⭐⭐ transactional queue depth DURING a broadcast (must stay near zero)
⭐          bounce rate · unsubscribe rate · ⭐ chunks completed / total
⭐ CUT:     rich analytics, A/B testing, in-app inbox UI, per-user send-time optimisation
⭐ AT 10×:  ⭐ more chunk workers and more providers — ⭐ the pipeline shape does not change
```

## ⭐ Traps

| Trap | Consequence |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ A priority field in one queue | ⭐⭐ **the password reset waits behind 20M marketing messages** |
| ⭐ Materialising the whole audience in memory | slow to start, unrestartable, one machine's problem |
| ⭐ **No chunk-level idempotency** | a retry re-sends to millions |
| ⭐ **No cancellation** | ⭐⭐ a mistaken broadcast cannot be stopped |
| ⭐ Autoscaling workers past the provider cap | ⭐ 429s, retries, and more load |
| ⭐ **Not rate-limiting outbound** | you are the abusive client (Day 336) |
| ⭐ Sending one API call per recipient | 100× more requests than needed |
| ⭐ Checking preferences at creation time | they change before the send lands |
| Quiet hours in server time | 3am messages (Day 318) |
| ⭐ **Retrying hard bounces** | ⭐ sender reputation destroyed for everyone |
| Delivery status on the notification, not the channel | cannot express partial success |
| No DLQ owner | silent loss |

## Interview questions

**Q: What is the defining characteristic of this system?**
The peak-to-average ratio. Steady state is a hundred and twenty notifications a second; one product
decision — notify everyone — is thirty-three thousand a second for ten minutes. You cannot provision
for that continuously, and the whole design is about absorbing it without letting it touch the
transactional path.

**Q: How do you stop a broadcast delaying a password reset?**
Separate queues with separate worker pools per channel and priority class, not a priority field.
With twenty million messages already enqueued, a flag still leaves the urgent message behind whatever
the workers are currently processing, which at a batch size of five hundred can be minutes. Separate
queues are the only structure that delivers on the promise, and I monitor transactional queue depth
during a broadcast specifically to prove it.

**Q: How do you fan out to twenty million users?**
In chunks. A broadcast record plus about twenty thousand chunk jobs of a thousand users each, where
each chunk resolves its own users, applies current preferences and enqueues sends. The first message
goes out in seconds rather than after a twenty-minute query, progress is observable, a crash loses
one chunk rather than the job, and — importantly — it is cancellable, because broadcasts are sent by
humans and humans send the wrong one.

**Q: What actually limits your throughput?**
The providers. You do not own delivery — push services, SMTP relays and SMS gateways all impose
quotas — so adding workers past the cap produces rate-limit errors, retries and more load rather than
more throughput. The correct design rate-limits itself outbound per provider, batches where the API
allows it, runs several providers with weights, and lets the queue absorb the difference. That is
exactly what a queue is for.

**Q: How do you handle quiet hours for a global audience?**
By asking the product what nine in the morning means. Either the broadcast goes out once and
recipients currently in quiet hours are suppressed, or it is delivered at nine local time, which
turns a single spike into twenty-four waves bucketed by timezone offset. The second is usually better
for both engagement and capacity, because it converts a thirty-three-thousand-a-second spike into a
gentle rolling load.

2. ⭐ Implement **chunked fan-out** for 1M simulated users. **Measure time to first send** against the
   naive materialise-everything version.

   sent to twice.

   version.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Three minutes:** *"Marketing wants to send a push to all twenty million users. Walk me through what
happens."*

**⭐ Four beats.** Chunked fan-out so the first send is in seconds and the job is restartable and
cancellable · **separate bulk queues so nothing transactional is delayed** · **outbound rate limiting
per provider, because the bottleneck is theirs and adding workers just produces 429s** · and
timezone waves if the product wants local-time delivery.

**⭐ The sentence that most distinguishes the answer: "I don't control my own throughput here — the
providers do, so scaling workers past their quota makes things worse, not better."** It is the
opposite of the instinct every other design in this stage rewards, and noticing that is the point.
