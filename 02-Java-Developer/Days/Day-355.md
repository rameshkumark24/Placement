# Day 355

**L-355** · Design 11 — ride hailing

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** BUILD

> **⭐ Day 320 designed the matching. At scale a different number dominates: a million drivers each
> reporting position every four seconds is a quarter of a million writes per second of data that is
> worthless in five seconds.**
>
> **Nothing about that belongs in your transactional database** — and separating ephemeral location
> from durable trips is the design.

---

# Part 1 — ⭐ Requirements, and the number nobody expects

| ⭐ Functional | ⭐ Non-functional |
|---|---|
| Request a ride; match a driver | ⭐⭐ **match within ~5 s** |
| ⭐ **Live driver tracking** | ⭐ location freshness < 5 s |
| Fare quote, trip lifecycle, payment | ⭐⭐ **a trip must never be lost** — durability |
| Surge pricing | availability: **matching 99.99%** |
| Ratings, history | ⭐ regional isolation — a city outage is not a global one |

```
⭐ ESTIMATE
⭐   1M active drivers · a position every 4 s ⇒ ⭐⭐ 250,000 location writes/s
⭐   Rides: 20M/day ⇒ ~230/s ⇒ peak ~700/s   ⭐ ← trivial by comparison
⭐   ⭐⭐ Location data is 1,000× the volume of trip data — AND IT EXPIRES IN SECONDS
⭐   Trip storage: 20M × 2 KB × 365 ⇒ ~15 TB/year — small
```

> **⭐ Say the ratio out loud: location writes outnumber business events by three orders of
> magnitude, and they have a four-second useful life.** That is what forces two completely separate
> systems, **and it is the insight the design turns on.**

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ The deep dive: location ingestion and the geo index

```
⭐ driver apps ──250k/s──► ⭐ LOCATION SERVICE (in-memory, sharded by GEO CELL)
⭐                                │
⭐                                ├──► ⭐ nearby(point, radius) for matching
⭐                                └──► ⭐ sampled writes → durable store (for trip replay only)
```

| ⭐ Decision | ⭐ Reason |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Never write raw locations to the primary database** | ⭐⭐ **250k writes/s of 4-second-lived data destroys any OLTP store** |
| ⭐ **Latest-wins, in memory** | ⭐ no history needed for matching — **only the current position matters** |
| ⭐ **Sharded by geo cell, not by driver** | ⭐⭐ **queries are "who is near here", so locality must match the query** |
| ⭐ Downsample for durability | ⭐ store one point per 30 s **for trip replay and disputes**, not every point |
| ⭐ **Regional deployment** | ⭐ a driver in London is never relevant to Tokyo — **shard by city** |

## ⭐ The index — and why a grid wins here

| Structure | ⭐ Verdict at 250k updates/s |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Geohash / H3 cells** | ⭐⭐ **O(1) remove-and-insert — updates dominate, so this wins** |
| Quadtree | ⭐ adapts to density; **rebalancing under constant movement is the cost** |
| k-d tree | ⭐ **bad — rebalancing on every update** |
| ⭐ Redis `GEOADD`/`GEOSEARCH` | ⭐ the pragmatic production answer (Day 204) |

**⭐ The query: search the containing cell plus its neighbours, then filter by true distance**
(Day 320). **Cell size is a real tuning parameter** — too small and you scan many cells, too large
and you get too many candidates.

⚠️ **Hotspots are geographic here, which is unusual.** A stadium at full time puts thousands of
drivers in one cell — **so cells must subdivide dynamically, or you use a hierarchical scheme (H3
resolutions) and drop to a finer level where density demands it.** Uniform grids are wrong for a
non-uniform world.

## ⭐ The connection question

**⭐ A million drivers holding open connections is [Day 352](Day-352.md)'s problem again.** But the
requirement is weaker: **location is a fire-and-forget upstream write**, so **UDP-like semantics are
acceptable** — a lost update costs four seconds of staleness. **Only during an active trip does the
rider need a push channel**, and that is a far smaller set.

> **⭐ Recognising that only in-trip users need a persistent connection reduces the connection
> problem by an order of magnitude**, and it is a nice example of scoping a hard requirement down
> before solving it.

# Part 3 — ⭐ Matching, at scale

**⭐ Day 320's protocol is unchanged and correct: an offer with a timeout, not an assignment**, with
a compare-and-set on the driver's state so two riders cannot be offered the same driver.

| ⭐ At scale, what is added | |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Matching is sharded by geo cell** | ⭐⭐ a matcher owns a region, so candidate lookup and the CAS are local |
| ⭐ **The index may be stale** | ⭐ it only produces candidates; **the CAS is the arbiter** (Day 311) |
| ⭐ Batch matching | ⭐ **collect requests over ~1–2 s and solve the assignment globally** — better outcomes than greedy |
| ⭐ Cross-cell requests | a rider at a cell boundary searches neighbours |
| ⭐ **No drivers found** | a real terminal state, with radius expansion first |

> **⭐ Batch matching is the scale-specific upgrade worth naming.** Greedy per-request matching gives
> each rider the nearest available driver; **solving a small assignment problem over a two-second
> window reduces total wait time across all riders**, because it can avoid sending the one nearby
> driver to the rider who had three alternatives.

**⭐ The cost, stated honestly: a 1–2 second delay before matching starts** — which is acceptable
against a five-second match SLA, and is a genuine latency-for-quality trade.

# Part 4 — ⭐ Trips, surge, and what breaks

## ⭐ The trip is the durable object

```
⭐ REQUESTED → MATCHING → ASSIGNED → ARRIVED → IN_PROGRESS → COMPLETED → PAID
⭐                 │
⭐                 └──► NO_DRIVERS / CANCELLED
```

| ⭐ Rule | |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **A trip is an immutable event record once complete** | ⭐ disputes need what was true at the time (Day 290) |
| ⭐ **Fare inputs frozen at completion** | ⭐⭐ distance, duration, surge multiplier, rate card version — **all stored on the trip** |
| ⭐ Payment is a saga (Day 342) | ⭐ the trip completes; the charge may fail and compensate |
| ⭐ **The trip survives app failures** | ⭐ a driver whose phone dies mid-trip must not lose the ride |

⚠️ **Fare disputes are the reason for freezing.** *"Why was I charged £24?"* must be answerable six
months later, **which is impossible if surge is recomputed or the rate card has changed.** This is
the third appearance of the frozen-inputs rule ([Day 290](Day-290.md), [Day 315](Day-315.md),
[Day 320](Day-320.md)) — **and naming the repetition is worth a sentence.**

## ⭐ Surge

```
⭐ Per cell, per short window: surge = f(open requests ÷ available drivers)
⭐ ⭐ Computed on a schedule (every ~1 min), cached, and ⭐⭐ LOCKED INTO THE QUOTE.
```

| ⭐ Detail | |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Quote, lock, charge the locked value** | a multiplier changing mid-trip is indefensible |
| ⭐ Smooth over time | ⭐ a multiplier oscillating between 1.0 and 2.4 every minute is a product failure |
| ⭐ Cell-level, with neighbour blending | hard boundaries create absurd price cliffs across a street |

## ⭐ What breaks

| ⭐ Breaks first | ⭐ Mitigation |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Location ingest** | ⭐ shard by cell; **drop rather than queue** — a stale point is worthless (Day 336) |
| ⭐ Stadium / event hotspots | ⭐ dynamic cell subdivision; surge is itself a demand-shedding mechanism |
| ⭐ **Matcher for one hot cell** | ⭐ split the cell; the matcher is per-region so blast radius is one area |
| Trip store | tiny by comparison |
| ⭐ **A regional outage** | ⭐ regional isolation means one city fails, not the platform |

```
⭐ MONITOR: ⭐ match time p99 · driver-offer acceptance rate · ⭐ location staleness
⭐          ⭐ rides completed/min (business metric) · surge multiplier distribution
⭐ CUT:     pooling, scheduled rides, multi-stop, driver incentives
⭐ AT 10×:  ⭐ more cells and more regions — ⭐ the design is already partitioned by geography
```

> **⭐ Geographic sharding is the quiet strength of this design: it is naturally partitionable,
> because a ride never spans two cities.** Very few systems have a partition key that obvious — **say
> so, because it is why this scales gracefully.**

---

## ⭐ Traps

| Trap | Consequence |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ Location writes to the primary database | ⭐⭐ **250k writes/s of 4-second-lived data** |
| ⭐ Storing every location point durably | enormous cost for data nobody reads |
| ⭐ Sharding location by driver id | ⭐ **the query is geographic; locality must match it** |
| ⭐ A k-d tree or unbalanced structure | rebalancing on every update |
| ⭐ **A uniform grid** | ⭐ stadium hotspots; use hierarchical cells |
| ⭐ Assigning rather than offering | Day 320 — riders stranded on absent drivers |
| ⭐ Locking the index for matching | ⭐ the index is a candidate source; **the CAS decides** |
| ⭐ **Recomputing surge at completion** | ⭐⭐ indefensible fare disputes |
| Surge with no smoothing or boundary blending | oscillation and price cliffs |
| Persistent connections for all drivers | ⭐ only in-trip users need one |
| Global matching instead of regional | needless coupling; a city outage becomes global |

## Interview questions

**Q: What dominates this design?**
Location volume. A million drivers reporting every four seconds is a quarter of a million writes per
second — three orders of magnitude more than the business events — and it is worthless after about
five seconds. So location lives in a sharded in-memory store with latest-wins semantics, downsampled
for trip replay, and never touches the transactional database that holds trips.

**Q: How do you index positions?**
Geohash or H3 cells, because updates dominate reads and a grid is an O(1) remove-and-insert while a
k-d tree needs rebalancing on every move. A query searches the containing cell and its neighbours,
then filters by true distance. The catch is that the world is not uniform — a stadium at full time
overloads one cell — so I use hierarchical cells and drop to a finer resolution where density
demands it.

**Q: How is matching different from the single-server version?**
It is sharded by geographic region, so candidate lookup and the compare-and-set on driver state are
both local and a hot city cannot affect another. The offer-with-timeout protocol is unchanged, and
the index is still allowed to be stale because it only produces candidates while the CAS decides. The
scale-specific addition is batch matching — collecting requests over a second or two and solving the
assignment globally reduces total wait time compared with greedy, at the cost of a small deliberate
delay.

**Q: Why freeze the fare inputs?**
Because "why was I charged twenty-four pounds?" must be answerable six months later, and it cannot be
if surge is recomputed or the rate card has since changed. So distance, duration, the surge
multiplier and the rate card version are all stored on the completed trip, which is then immutable.
It is the same rule as a risk score computed from a versioned ruleset and an expense recorded with
that day's exchange rate.

**Q: What happens when a stadium empties?**
Thousands of drivers and riders concentrate in one cell, which is a geographic hotspot rather than a
key hotspot. Cells subdivide to a finer resolution so the index stays balanced, matching for that
region can be split across matchers, and surge itself acts as demand shedding. The reassuring part is
that the blast radius is one area, because the whole system is partitioned by geography — a partition
key most designs would envy.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Three minutes:** *"A million drivers are sending their position every four seconds. Where does that
data go?"*

**⭐ The marker: you refuse the obvious destination immediately.** *"Not the database — that is a
quarter of a million writes a second of data with a four-second useful life, so it goes to an
in-memory store sharded by geographic cell with latest-wins semantics, and only a downsampled track
is persisted for trip replay."*

**⭐ Then the second half that most candidates miss: shard by cell rather than by driver, because the
query is 'who is near here'.** Choosing a partition key that matches the query, not the entity, is
the same lesson as [Day 329](Day-329.md) — **and this is the design where it is most visible.**
