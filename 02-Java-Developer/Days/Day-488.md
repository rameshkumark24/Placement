# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ Do not name the number first

```
⭐⭐ THE RECRUITER ASKS, IN THE FIRST CALL: "What are your compensation expectations?"
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ Anything you say becomes the CEILING. ⭐ Nobody has ever been offered more than they asked for.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ And you do not yet know the level, the scope, or the band. ⭐ You are guessing.

⭐⭐ THE DEFERRAL, said warmly and without evasion:
⭐   ⭐ "I'd rather understand the role and the level first — I'm sure we can make the numbers work
⭐     if it's the right fit. ⭐⭐ What range is budgeted for this level?"
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ You deferred AND asked. ⭐ Many recruiters will tell you the band; that is their job.

⭐⭐ IF PUSHED (and in some regions they must ask): give a RANGE anchored on RESEARCH, not on hope.
⭐   ⭐ "Based on what I've seen for this level in this market, I'd expect somewhere in X–Y,
⭐     but it depends on the level and the equity structure."
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ Never state your CURRENT salary. ⭐⭐ In many places they may not ask; where they may,
⭐     ⭐ "I'd rather anchor on the market rate for the role" is a complete answer.
```

⚠️ **Your current salary is the most damaging number you can volunteer**, because it converts the
conversation from "what is this role worth" to "what is the smallest raise you will accept". **If you are
underpaid now, disclosing it guarantees you stay underpaid.**

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ What is actually negotiable

| ⭐ Component | ⭐ Flexibility |
|---|---|
| ⭐ Base salary | ⭐⭐ **some** — usually banded by level, so **the level is the bigger lever** (Day 487) |
| ⭐⭐ **Signing bonus** | ⭐⭐ **the most flexible component** — it is one-off, so it does not distort the band |
| ⭐⭐ **Equity** | ⭐⭐ often more flexible than base, and the number people under-negotiate most |
| ⭐ Level | ⭐⭐ **the largest single lever, and the hardest** — needs evidence, not persuasion |
| ⭐ Start date | ⭐ almost always flexible; **worth using to keep other loops alive** |
| ⭐ Remote / location | ⭐ increasingly negotiable, and sometimes worth more than money |
| ⭐ Learning budget, hardware, title | ⭐ small, easy wins; ask together, not separately |

```
⭐⭐ THE ASK, in four sentences — enthusiastic, specific, justified, and NOT an ultimatum:
⭐   ⭐ "I'm genuinely excited about this — the [specific thing] is what I want to work on."
⭐   ⭐⭐ "Based on the market for this level and the other conversations I'm in, I was hoping
⭐     for something closer to [specific number]."
⭐   ⭐⭐ "Is there flexibility on base, or would a signing bonus be easier on your side?"
⭐   ⭐ "Either way, I'd like to make this work."
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ Note: ⭐ ONE number, not a range (⭐⭐ they will take the bottom of a range) ·
⭐   ⭐⭐ a reason that is not "I need it" · ⭐⭐ and you offered them a mechanism.
```

| ⭐ Rules | |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Always negotiate.** | ⭐⭐ the offer is rarely the maximum, and asking politely has **never** cost anyone an offer |
| ⭐⭐ **Get it in writing before deciding** | ⭐ verbal numbers move |
| ⭐ Ask once, maybe twice — never four times | ⭐⭐ persistence past two rounds costs goodwill you will need later |
| ⭐⭐ **Never bluff a competing offer** | ⭐⭐ it is occasionally checked, and it is unrecoverable |
| ⭐ Do not accept on the call | ⭐⭐ **"thank you — can I come back to you tomorrow?"** is always fine |
| ⭐ Be someone they still want on Monday | ⭐⭐ you will work with these people |

> **⭐ On competing offers: real ones are the strongest leverage that exists, and they only work if they
> are concurrent** (Day 486). **"I have another offer at X and I'd rather come to you" is a fact, not a
> threat** — and stated that way, it is neither rude nor risky.

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Comparing offers, when salary is the least of it

| ⭐ Dimension | ⭐ The question that reveals it |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **What you will learn** | ⭐⭐ **who is on the team who is better than you?** — the largest factor at the start of a career |
| ⭐⭐ **Scope** | ⭐ will you own something, or work on someone else's design? |
| ⭐ Manager | ⭐⭐ **the single biggest determinant of a good year** — ask about their team's last promotion |
| ⭐ On-call reality | ⭐ pages last month (Day 465) |
| ⭐ Deploy frequency | ⭐⭐ merge-to-production time is a proxy for engineering health (Day 370) |
| ⭐ Equity | ⭐⭐ **strike price, current valuation, vesting cliff, and what happens if you leave** |
| ⭐ Stability | ⭐ funding stage, runway, profitability |
| ⭐⭐ **Optionality** | ⭐⭐ **what does this role make possible in two years?** |

```
⭐⭐ THE HONEST WEIGHTING EARLY IN A CAREER:
⭐   ⭐⭐ LEARNING RATE > SCOPE > MANAGER > COMPENSATION
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ a 15% higher salary at a place where nobody reviews your code seriously
⭐     is ⭐⭐ a bad trade — you will earn it back within two years and lose the two years.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ Later, that ordering inverts. ⭐ Know which phase you are in and choose accordingly.
```

⚠️ **Equity requires arithmetic, not enthusiasm.** Ask for the strike price, the current preferred
valuation, the total shares outstanding, the vesting schedule and the cliff, **and what happens to vested
options if you leave** — many companies give you 90 days to exercise, **which can mean a tax bill you
cannot pay.** Treat unlisted equity as a **lottery ticket with a positive expected value**, not as salary.

## ⭐ Resigning, and the counter-offer

| | |
|---|---|
| ⭐ Give proper notice, hand over well | ⭐⭐ the industry is smaller than it looks |
| ⭐⭐ **Counter-offers** | ⭐⭐ mostly retention tactics — **the reasons you were leaving are usually still there** |
| ⭐ If money was genuinely the only reason | ⭐ it may be legitimate; **be honest with yourself about that** |
| ⭐ Do not use an offer purely to extract a raise | ⭐⭐ it works once and it costs a relationship |

# Part 4 — ⭐ The first ninety days

| ⭐ Weeks 1–2 | ⭐ Weeks 3–8 | ⭐ Weeks 9–12 |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Ship something tiny on day 2** | ⭐⭐ **own one small thing end to end** | ⭐ take on-call with a buddy |
| ⭐ Read the on-call runbook | ⭐ fix a paper cut everyone complains about | ⭐⭐ propose one improvement **with a measurement** |
| ⭐⭐ **Ask "why" about one thing per day** | ⭐ write down what confuses you | ⭐ write the doc nobody wrote |
| ⭐ Meet people, not just your team | ⭐⭐ **do not propose a rewrite** | ⭐ have an honest check-in with your manager |

> **⭐⭐ The most valuable thing a new engineer has is a fresh pair of eyes, and it expires in about six
> weeks.** Write down everything confusing in week one — **that list is the most useful onboarding
> document the team will ever get, and you can only write it once.**

⚠️ **And the one to avoid: arriving with opinions about their architecture.** You have Stages 8–12 in your
head and it will be tempting. **Ask why it is that way first — there is usually a reason, and if there is
not, you will have earned the right to say so by month three.**

## ⭐⭐ The through-lines — the things that kept reappearing

```
⭐⭐ 1. A LIMITED RESOURCE, CLAIMED BY AN ATOMIC CONDITIONAL UPDATE, HELD WITH AN EXPIRY.
⭐    ⭐ the seat hold · the parking spot · the SQS visibility timeout · the distributed lock ·
⭐    ⭐ the job lease · the rate limiter token. ⭐⭐ Six problems, one mechanism. (Day 320A)

⭐⭐ 2. ANYTHING CHARGED OR JUDGED USES FROZEN INPUTS.
⭐    ⭐ the price at purchase · the address at order · the rate at entry · the rules at evaluation.

⭐⭐ 3. EXACTLY-ONCE DELIVERY DOES NOT EXIST. EFFECTIVELY-ONCE PROCESSING DOES.
⭐    ⭐ at-least-once + an idempotent consumer, with the dedup and the effect in ONE transaction.

⭐⭐ 4. YOU CANNOT TELL A DEAD NODE FROM A SLOW ONE.
⭐    ⭐ every timeout is a guess, so the system must stay correct after the guess is wrong.

⭐⭐ 5. NAME THE COST OF EVERY CHOICE.
⭐    ⭐ a design presented as pure upside was never evaluated — it was adopted.

⭐⭐ 6. MEASURE IT, OR IT IS AN OPINION.
⭐    ⭐ "I load-tested it and it broke here" outranks any correct guess, every time.
```

## Interview questions

**Q: The recruiter asks your expectations on the first call. What do you say?**
I defer warmly and turn it around: I would rather understand the role and the level first, and what range
is budgeted for this level. Many recruiters will tell you the band, because it is their job to avoid
wasting everyone's time. If pushed I give a researched range and make clear it depends on level and
equity structure — but I never give my current salary, because that converts the conversation from what
the role is worth into what the smallest acceptable raise is. If I am underpaid now, disclosing it
guarantees I stay underpaid.

**Q: How do you actually ask for more?**
Four sentences: genuine enthusiasm about something specific, one number rather than a range because a
range gets read as its bottom, a reason based on market and other conversations rather than on need, and
an offer of a mechanism — is there flexibility on base, or would a signing bonus be easier on your side.
Then close with wanting to make it work. It is not an ultimatum, and I ask once or possibly twice, never
four times. Signing bonus is usually the most flexible component because it is one-off and does not
distort the band, and the biggest lever of all is the level rather than the number.

**Q: How do you compare two offers?**
Early in a career, weighted as learning rate, then scope, then manager, then compensation — because
fifteen percent more money somewhere nobody reviews your code seriously is a bad trade you earn back in
two years while losing the two years. The questions that reveal those are: who on this team is better than
me, will I own something or implement someone else's design, how many times were you paged last month,
and how long from merge to production. For equity I do arithmetic rather than enthusiasm — strike price,
valuation, shares outstanding, cliff, and specifically what happens to vested options if I leave, because
a ninety-day exercise window can mean a tax bill you cannot pay.

**Q: What do you do in your first ninety days?**
Ship something tiny on day two, because the first commit is more about the pipeline than the code. Own
one small thing end to end within the first two months, including its operations. Take on-call with a
buddy. And write down everything that confuses me in week one — that list is the best onboarding document
the team will ever get and it can only be written once, because a fresh pair of eyes expires in about six
weeks. What I would not do is arrive with opinions about their architecture; there is usually a reason,
and if there is not, I will have earned the right to say so by month three.

**Q: What do you think you learned from all this?**
Six things that kept reappearing across completely different problems. That a limited resource is claimed
by an atomic conditional update held with an expiry — a seat hold, a parking spot, a visibility timeout
and a job lease are one mechanism. That anything charged or judged uses frozen inputs. That exactly-once
delivery does not exist but effectively-once processing does. That you cannot distinguish a dead node from
a slow one, so every timeout is a guess you have to stay correct after. That every choice has a cost and
a design presented as pure upside was adopted rather than evaluated. And that a measurement outranks a
correct guess every single time.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**The last one. Two parts, both recorded.**

**⭐ First, two minutes: the negotiation, played out with someone pushing back twice. Practise the
deferral, the four-sentence ask, and — the hardest part — the silence after you say the number. Do not
fill it. The silence is not a rejection; it is someone doing arithmetic.**

**⭐ Then, five minutes: "Tell me about yourself" — the version you could not have given 488 days ago.**

**⭐ Not the story of a course. The story of a system: what you built, the decision in it you are proudest
of, the failure you reproduced on purpose, the number you measured, and the thing you deliberately chose
not to do. Say what it costs to run. Say where it breaks first. Say what you would change and what would
have to be true for you to change it.**

**⭐⭐ Then play it back next to the one you recorded on Day 1, if you kept it. That difference is the
whole roadmap, and nobody can take it off you.**
