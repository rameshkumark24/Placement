# Day 308 ⭐

**L-308** · ⭐ The LLD method — requirements to working code in forty-five minutes

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> **⭐ An LLD round is not a test of whether you know the design patterns. It is a test of whether you
> can be handed three vague sentences and produce a defensible model, out loud, under time
> pressure.**
>
> Today is the method — six steps, a clock, and the things to say at each one. **Days 311–321 apply
> it eleven times until it is automatic.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐ What is actually being scored

**⭐ Interviewers score four things, and code quality is not the first of them:**

| ⭐ What they score | ⭐ What it looks like when you have it |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Do you clarify before designing?** | ⭐ **three sharp questions, then a stated scope** |
| ⭐⭐ **Is your model coherent?** | ⭐ classes with clear responsibilities; **rules live inside the things they constrain** |
| ⭐⭐ **Do you narrate trade-offs?** | ⭐ **"I could do X or Y; I'm choosing X because…"** |
| ⭐ **Can you extend it?** | ⭐ their "now add Z" does not require a redesign |

**⭐ And the four ways candidates fail, in order of frequency:**

1. ⭐ **Coding immediately.** Ten minutes in, there is a `ParkingLot` class with sixteen fields and no
   requirements were agreed.
2. ⭐ **Designing everything shallowly.** Twelve classes, none finished, no flow works end to end.
3. ⭐ **Silence.** A correct design produced without narration scores below a mediocre one that was
   explained.
4. ⭐ **Pattern gold-plating.** A visitor, an abstract factory and an observer for a problem with two
   rules. **Naming a pattern is not a reason.**

> **⭐ The single highest-scoring behaviour: state a trade-off before being asked, then say what would
> change your decision.** It is the same marker as Stage 7's project defence, because it tests the
> same thing.

## ⭐ The clock

```
⭐  0–05  Clarify           ⭐ 3 questions, then SAY the scope out loud
⭐  5–10  Actors & flows    ⭐ who does what; pick ONE core flow
⭐ 10–18  Entities & rules  ⭐ nouns → classes, then prune; where does each rule live?
⭐ 18–25  Class diagram     ⭐ relationships, multiplicity, ⭐ THE AGGREGATE BOUNDARY
⭐ 25–40  Code the core     ⭐ ONE flow, end to end, compiling in your head
⭐ 40–45  Concurrency,
⭐         extensions,
⭐         what you cut     ⭐ ← say this WITHOUT being prompted
```

⚠️ **The 25-minute mark is the discipline that matters.** If you are still drawing at 30, you will
produce no code and the round is a fail regardless of how good the model was. **Set the internal
alarm and move.**

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ The six steps

## ⭐ Step 1 — Clarify (5 min): three questions, then declare scope

**⭐ Not ten questions. Three — the ones whose answers change the model.**

| Ask | ⭐ Because it changes |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **"What are the core operations?"** | ⭐ the whole design |
| ⭐ **"Single instance or distributed / multi-site?"** | ⭐ **in-process locks vs Day 207's problem** |
| ⭐ **"What varies?"** — pricing? rules? types? | ⭐ **where the strategies go** |
| (if relevant) "Scale?" | data structures; usually a red herring in LLD |

**⭐ Then say the scope out loud, and write it in a corner of the board:**

> *"So: in scope — park, unpark, price a ticket, multiple floors and vehicle sizes. Out of scope —
> reservations, payment gateways, user accounts. I'll design so pricing and spot-assignment are
> pluggable, since you said those change. Sound right?"*

**⭐ That sentence does four jobs at once**: it proves you listened, it fixes the scope so you cannot
be blamed for omissions, it pre-announces your extension points, and **it gets the interviewer to
agree**, which means the rest of the round is collaborative rather than adversarial.

## ⭐ Step 2 — Actors and the one core flow (5 min)

```
⭐ Actors:  Driver · Attendant · Admin
⭐ Flows:   park · unpark & pay · configure rates · report occupancy
⭐ CORE:    park → allocate spot → issue ticket → exit → price → pay → release
```

> **⭐ Pick one flow and commit to it. Depth beats breadth every time.** A finished parking flow with
> the double-allocation race handled scores far above six half-built subsystems.

## ⭐ Step 3 — Entities: nouns, then prune (8 min)

**⭐ Extract every noun from the problem statement. Then delete most of them.** The pruning is the
skill, not the extraction:

| ⭐ Prune rule | Example |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Is it a value or an entity?** (Day 304) | ⭐ `Money`, `LicensePlate`, `SpotId` → **value objects** |
| ⭐ **Does it have behaviour or just data?** | ⭐ pure data with no rules → a field, not a class |
| ⭐ **Would it ever be looked up by id?** | if never, it is probably a value |
| ⭐ **Is it a role, not a thing?** | `Driver` may just be a `User` in a context (Day 305) |
| ⭐ **Is it an enum?** | ⭐ `VehicleType`, `SpotSize`, `Status` — **not a class hierarchy** |

**⭐ Then, for each rule in the problem, name the class that owns it.** A rule with no owner is a rule
that will end up in a service and be bypassable (Day 302).

## ⭐ Step 4 — Relationships and the aggregate boundary (7 min)

```
⭐ ParkingLot 1 ──── * Floor 1 ──── * Spot         ⭐ composition — lifecycle bound
⭐ Ticket ──── SpotId (by identity)                ⭐ Day 305 rule 3
⭐ Ticket 1 ──── 0..1 Payment
```

| ⭐ Say out loud | ⭐ Why it scores |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **multiplicity on every line** | it forces the "can it be zero?" question |
| ⭐ **"This is the aggregate boundary"** | ⭐ you are naming the transaction and lock boundary (Day 305) |
| ⭐ **which references are by id** | shows you know what a boundary means |

## ⭐ Step 5 — Code the core (15 min)

**⭐ What to write, in order — and what to skip explicitly:**

```java
⭐ 1. The enums and value objects          — 60 seconds, and they make everything else read well
⭐ 2. The entity with the invariant         — the one method the whole problem is about
⭐ 3. The strategy interface(s)             — one implementation only
⭐ 4. The orchestrating service             — load, delegate, persist (Day 302)
⭐ 5. Say: "getters, equals, persistence — assume standard, happy to write any of them"
```

⚠️ **Write real Java, not pseudocode.** Method signatures, types, the actual `if`. **But say "I'll
skip getters and constructors unless you want them"** — that is a professional, not a lazy, move,
and interviewers almost always agree.

## ⭐ Step 6 — Concurrency, extensions, and what you cut (5 min)

**⭐ Volunteer all three. This is the section that separates candidates**, and it takes ninety
seconds:

```
⭐ "Concurrency: two drivers can be assigned the same spot. In-process I'd guard the
⭐  allocation with a per-floor lock, or use an atomic compare-and-set on the spot's
⭐  state so the loser retries. Distributed, that becomes an optimistic version column
⭐  or a lock with a fencing token."

⭐ "Extensions: pricing is already a strategy, so hourly→dynamic is one class. Reservations
⭐  would need a spot to have a future state, which changes the allocation query — that's
⭐  the one that isn't cheap."

⭐ "I cut: multiple entrances, EV charging, and the payment gateway integration."
```

# Part 3 — ⭐ Where the rules go: a decision table

**⭐ You will make these five decisions in every single problem. Decide them fast and say why.**

| Decision | ⭐ Default | ⭐ Switch when |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Inheritance or enum + field?** | ⭐ **enum + field** | ⭐ the subtypes have genuinely different *behaviour*, not just data |
| ⭐ **Strategy or `if`?** | ⭐ `if` for two fixed cases | ⭐ **the interviewer said it varies**, or a third case is coming |
| ⭐ **Where does this rule live?** | ⭐ **on the entity it constrains** (Day 302) | it spans two aggregates → domain service |
| ⭐ **Interface or class?** | class | ⭐ **there is a real second implementation, or you need a test double** |
| ⭐ **Singleton?** | ⭐ **no** (Day 093) | ⭐ almost never — inject it instead |

**⭐ The vehicle-type question is the canonical example, and it comes up in half the problems:**

```java
// ⭐ Usually WRONG — three classes with no behavioural difference
class Car extends Vehicle { }   class Truck extends Vehicle { }   class Bike extends Vehicle { }

// ⭐ Usually RIGHT — the difference is data
record Vehicle(LicensePlate plate, VehicleType type) { }
enum VehicleType { BIKE(1), CAR(2), TRUCK(3);  final int size; }
```

> **⭐ Say the reason out loud: "these differ in data, not behaviour, so I'll use an enum — I'd switch
> to subclasses if, say, trucks had a different pricing algorithm."** That single sentence
> demonstrates Day 092's composition-over-inheritance instinct better than any diagram.

# Part 4 — ⭐ The narration script

**⭐ Sentences that score, worth having ready:**

| Moment | ⭐ Say |
|---|---|
| After clarifying | ⭐ *"In scope… out of scope… I'll make X pluggable since you said it varies."* |
| Choosing a structure | ⭐ *"A list is O(n) for the lookup; I'll keep a map by type so allocation is O(1)."* |
| Placing a rule | ⭐ *"This goes on `Ticket` rather than the service, so nothing can bypass it."* |
| ⭐ **Any time you pick** | ⭐⭐ *"I'm choosing X over Y because ___. I'd switch if ___."* |
| ⭐ **Unprompted, at the end** | ⭐⭐ *"The concurrency risk here is ___, and here is how I'd handle it."* |
| When stuck | ⭐ *"Let me state the invariant I'm trying to protect"* — ⭐ **this un-sticks you almost every time** |

⚠️ **If the interviewer pushes back, do not fold immediately and do not dig in.** *"That's fair —
the cost of my approach is X. If Y matters more here, I'd switch."* **Changing your mind for a stated
reason scores; changing it because someone frowned does not.**

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts |
|---|---|
| ⭐ Coding before agreeing scope | ⭐ you solve a problem nobody asked for |
| ⭐ Asking ten clarifying questions | ⭐ you spend the round interviewing them |
| ⭐ Twelve classes, none finished | ⭐ **no flow works end to end** |
| ⭐ Silence while thinking | ⭐ an unnarrated correct answer loses to a narrated adequate one |
| ⭐ Patterns as decoration | a visitor for two rules signals inexperience |
| Class hierarchies for data differences | Day 092, unlearned |
| ⭐ Not mentioning concurrency | ⭐ **the most commonly skipped section, and it is free marks** |
| Not saying what you cut | it looks like you forgot rather than decided |
| ⭐ Still diagramming at minute 30 | ⭐ no code — usually an automatic fail |

## Interview questions

**Q: How do you approach an LLD problem?**
Five minutes clarifying — core operations, single-instance or distributed, and what varies — then I
state the scope out loud and get agreement. Actors and one core flow, nouns pruned to entities and
value objects, relationships with multiplicity and an explicit aggregate boundary, then fifteen
minutes coding that one flow end to end. I keep the last five for concurrency, extensions and what I
deliberately cut.

**Q: Why only one flow?**
Because depth is what is being assessed. Six half-built subsystems demonstrate nothing; one flow that
compiles, protects its invariant and handles two users doing it at once demonstrates everything —
and it gives the interviewer something concrete to push on, which is where the rest of the marks are.

**Q: How do you decide between an enum and a class hierarchy?**
Whether the subtypes differ in behaviour or only in data. Vehicle types that differ by size are an
enum with a field; if trucks priced differently by algorithm, that is a strategy or a subclass. I say
the reason out loud and name what would change it, because the decision matters less than the
awareness of the trade.

**Q: What do you do when you get stuck mid-design?**
Say the invariant I am trying to protect. Almost every stuck moment is an unstated rule, and naming
it usually produces the structure. It also keeps me narrating, which matters more than the pause
itself.

**Q: The interviewer disagrees with your design. What do you do?**
Name the cost of my approach, ask what they are optimising for, and switch if their priority makes
mine wrong. Changing a decision for a stated reason is a good signal; changing it because they
frowned is a bad one, and so is defending something I have already been shown a flaw in.

## Mini task

1. **Take any problem from Days 311–321 and run the clock strictly**, with a timer, alone, out loud.
   **Stop at 45 minutes wherever you are.** Note which phase overran.
2. Record yourself doing it. **Play it back with one question: how many seconds of silence?**
3. Write the **scope sentence** for three different problems. Twenty seconds each, no notes.
4. **Practise step 3's pruning**: take a problem statement, list twenty nouns, and defend deleting at
   least twelve.
5. For one problem, write **only** the enums and value objects. **Notice how much of the design that
   settles.**
6. ⭐ Write out your **five-decision table answers** for a problem before designing it. Then design it
   and see if you changed any — that is where the interesting judgement was.
7. ⭐ **Practise the ninety-second closing** — concurrency, extensions, cuts — until it is automatic.
   **It is the cheapest set of marks in the round.**

# 🚪 Exit questions

1. Give the four things being scored, in order.
2. Give the four failure modes and which is most common.
3. Give the clock, phase by phase, with the hard deadline.
4. What are the three clarifying questions, and why those three?
5. What four jobs does the scope sentence do?
6. Give the five pruning rules for turning nouns into classes.
7. What must you say out loud when drawing relationships?
8. What do you code, in what order, and what do you explicitly skip?
9. Give the five recurring decisions and their defaults.
10. When is a class hierarchy right for a type field?
11. What do you say when stuck, and why does it work?
12. What do you do when the interviewer disagrees?

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Five minutes, recorded:** take a problem you have never seen — *"design a hotel booking system"* —
and **do only steps 1 and 2**. Clarify, state scope, list actors, pick the core flow. Stop.

**⭐ The marker: your scope sentence names an extension point that was implied by their answer**
("you said rates change seasonally, so I'll make pricing pluggable"). **That single move sets up half
the round in your favour**, because when they later ask "now add dynamic pricing", you already built
for it and can say so.
