# Day 322

**L-322** · Scalability, latency vs throughput, and SLI/SLO/SLA

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> **⭐ "Scalable" is not "handles a lot of traffic". It is: when load doubles, what do I have to do,
> and what does it cost?**
>

> number in your monitoring.

# Part 1 — ⭐ Definitions that survive scrutiny

| Term | ⭐ Precise meaning |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Scalability** | ⭐ **the relationship between load and cost.** Linear is good; superlinear is a wall |
| ⭐ **Latency** | ⭐ time for **one** operation |
| ⭐ **Throughput** | ⭐ operations **per unit time** |
| ⭐ **Utilisation** | ⭐ **fraction of capacity in use — the hidden variable in every latency problem** |
| Capacity | maximum sustainable throughput at an acceptable latency |

> **⭐ Latency and throughput are not the same axis, and improving one often hurts the other.**
> Batching raises throughput and raises latency. Adding threads raises throughput until context
> switching and lock contention make latency collapse.

## ⭐⭐ Little's Law — the one formula worth memorising

```
⭐  L = λ × W

⭐  L = concurrent requests in the system
⭐  λ = arrival rate (requests/second)
⭐  W = average time in the system (seconds)
```

**⭐ It is exact, it needs no assumptions, and it answers questions people usually guess at:**

| Question | ⭐ Answer |
|---|---|
| ⭐ 2,000 rps, 50 ms average → how many in flight? | ⭐ **L = 2000 × 0.05 = 100 concurrent** |
| ⭐ **How big should the thread pool be?** | ⭐ **≥ 100, or you queue** (and Day 156's connection pool follows the same logic) |
| ⭐ Latency degrades to 200 ms — now? | ⭐ **400 concurrent. Your pool of 100 is now the bottleneck** |
| ⭐ **A dependency slows down — what happens?** | ⭐⭐ **W rises, L rises, you run out of threads, and you fail on requests that had nothing to do with it** |

> **⭐ That last row is the mechanism behind most cascading failures**, and being able to state it in
> one line is worth more than naming a dozen patterns.

## ⭐⭐ The utilisation curve — why 90% is dangerous

```
⭐ Latency
     │                                        ╱
     │                                      ╱
     │                                   ╱
     │                              ╱
     │                    ────────╱
     │  ──────────────────
     └────────────────────────────────────────► Utilisation
        30%      50%     70%   80%  90% 95%

⭐ Queueing theory (M/M/1): W ∝ 1 / (1 − utilisation)
⭐   50% → 2× the service time      ⭐ 90% → 10×      ⭐ 95% → 20×
```

| ⭐ Utilisation | ⭐ Latency multiplier | ⭐ Reality |
|---|---|---|
| 50% | 2× | comfortable |
| ⭐ **70%** | ⭐ 3.3× | ⭐ **a sensible target** |
| ⭐ **90%** | ⭐ **10×** | ⭐ **one traffic spike from an outage** |
| 95% | 20× | already degraded |

> **⭐ The consequence to say out loud: you cannot run a system at 100% utilisation, and headroom is
> not waste — it is the buffer that absorbs variance.** An interviewer asking "how many servers?"
> is often checking whether you divide by 0.7.

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ Percentiles, and why averages lie

```
⭐ 100 requests: 99 take 10 ms, one takes 5,000 ms.
⭐ Average = 59.9 ms   ⭐ ← describes NOBODY
⭐ p50 = 10 ms   p99 = 5,000 ms
```

| Metric | ⭐ What it tells you |
|---|---|
| ⭐ Average | ⭐ **almost nothing — one outlier moves it, and no user experiences it** |
| p50 | the typical experience |
| ⭐ **p99** | ⭐ **the experience of your most active users** — see below |
| p999 | where the systemic problems hide |
| ⭐ **max** | ⭐ your worst timeout, and often a bug |

## ⭐⭐ Tail amplification — the argument that lands

> **⭐ Your heaviest users hit the p99 most often, because they make the most requests.** The tail is
> not a rare edge case — **it is the routine experience of your best customers.**

**⭐ And it compounds through fan-out, which is the real point:**

```
⭐ One page = 100 backend calls, each with p99 = 10 ms.
⭐ P(no call hits its p99) = 0.99^100 ≈ 0.366
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ 63% of PAGE loads contain at least one p99 call.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ A per-service p99 becomes a per-page MEDIAN.
```

| ⭐ Consequence | ⭐ Fix |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Fan-out turns rare slowness into common slowness** | ⭐ reduce fan-out; parallelise; **hedged requests** |
| ⭐ Per-service SLOs are not enough | ⭐ **measure end-to-end** |
| ⭐ Hedging: send a duplicate at p95, take the first | ⭐ **~5% extra load, dramatically better tail** |

⚠️ **Never average percentiles across instances.** The mean of four p99s is not the p99 — you need
histogram merging, which is why Prometheus histograms exist and why `summary` quantiles cannot be
aggregated (Day 143).

# Part 3 — ⭐ SLI, SLO, SLA, and the error budget

| Term | ⭐ Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **SLI** — indicator | ⭐ **a measurement** | ⭐ % of requests < 300 ms |
| ⭐ **SLO** — objective | ⭐ **your internal target** | ⭐ 99.9% over 30 days |
| ⭐ **SLA** — agreement | ⭐ **a contract with financial penalties** | ⭐ 99.5%, or refunds |

> **⭐ The SLA is always looser than the SLO**, deliberately: you want to be alerted and fixing it
> long before a customer can claim money back.

## ⭐ The nines, in minutes you can feel

| Availability | ⭐ Downtime / month | ⭐ What it implies |
|---|---|---|
| 99% | ⭐ **7.2 hours** | one bad afternoon |
| 99.9% | ⭐ **43 minutes** | ⭐ a single-region system with good ops |
| 99.99% | ⭐ **4.3 minutes** | ⭐ **multi-AZ, automated failover, no manual steps** |
| 99.999% | ⭐ **26 seconds** | ⭐ **multi-region, and nobody can log in to fix it in time** |

> **⭐ The line that shows judgement: "each extra nine roughly multiplies the cost, and past 99.99%
> the constraint is that a human cannot respond fast enough — so everything must be automatic. I
> would ask what the business actually needs before designing for it."**

## ⭐⭐ The error budget

```
⭐ SLO 99.9% over 30 days  ⇒  ⭐ 43 minutes of failure you are ALLOWED to spend.
```

| ⭐ Consequence | ⭐ Why it matters |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Budget remaining → ship faster** | ⭐ reliability is a resource, not a virtue |
| ⭐ **Budget exhausted → freeze features, fix reliability** | ⭐ **an objective rule, not an argument** |
| ⭐ **A 100% target is wrong** | ⭐⭐ **it forbids all change, and your dependencies are not 100% anyway** |

**⭐ Choose SLIs users actually feel:** request success rate, latency at a threshold, freshness of
data. **Not CPU, not memory** — those are causes, not experiences, and an SLO on CPU means nothing to
anyone.

## ⭐ The latency numbers to know cold

```
⭐ L1 cache reference                    ~1 ns
⭐ Main memory reference                ~100 ns
⭐ SSD random read                      ~100 µs      ⭐ 1,000× memory
⭐ Round trip within a datacenter       ~500 µs
⭐ Disk (HDD) seek                       ~10 ms
⭐ Round trip US ↔ Europe               ~150 ms      ⭐⭐ THE speed of light. Not fixable.
```

> **⭐ The one that decides architectures: a cross-continent round trip is ~150 ms and no engineering
> removes it.** Three sequential cross-region calls is half a second before you have done any work —
> **which is the entire argument for regional deployments, CDNs, and not chatting across oceans.**

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts |
|---|---|
| ⭐ "Scalable" meaning "fast" | ⭐ they are unrelated properties |
| ⭐ Reporting averages | ⭐ **describes nobody; hides the tail entirely** |
| ⭐ Averaging percentiles across instances | ⭐ mathematically meaningless |
| ⭐ Planning for 100% utilisation | ⭐ **latency is 10× at 90%** |
| ⭐ Ignoring fan-out amplification | ⭐ **a p99 becomes a page median** |
| ⭐ Pool sizes not derived from Little's Law | ⭐ the pool becomes the bottleneck when a dependency slows |
| SLOs on CPU or memory | users do not experience CPU |
| ⭐ A 100% availability target | ⭐ forbids change; ignores your dependencies |
| SLA tighter than or equal to the SLO | no room to react before penalties |
| ⭐ Sequential cross-region calls | ⭐ 150 ms each, and physics does not negotiate |

## Interview questions

**Q: What does scalable mean?**
That the cost of serving load grows no worse than linearly with the load — so when traffic doubles I
know what I add and what it costs. It is not the same as fast: a batch system can be enormously
scalable and have terrible latency, and a single-threaded service can be very fast and not scale at
all.

**Q: What is Little's Law and why does it matter?**
Concurrency equals arrival rate times time in the system. It sizes pools: two thousand requests a
second at fifty milliseconds means a hundred in flight, so a smaller pool queues. More usefully, it
explains cascading failure — when a dependency slows, time in the system rises, concurrency rises,
threads run out, and requests that never touched that dependency start failing.

**Q: Why can't you run a server at 95% utilisation?**
Because queueing delay scales as one over one minus utilisation. At fifty percent you wait about
twice the service time; at ninety percent, ten times; at ninety-five, twenty. Headroom is not waste,
it is what absorbs variance — so I size for about seventy percent and treat the rest as the buffer.

**Q: Why measure p99 rather than the average?**
Because the average describes nobody — one five-second request among ninety-nine ten-millisecond ones
gives an average of sixty milliseconds that no user experienced. And the tail is not rare for the
people who matter: heavy users make the most requests, so they hit the p99 most often. With fan-out
it gets worse — a hundred backend calls each at a ten-millisecond p99 means roughly sixty-three
percent of page loads contain a p99 call, so a service tail becomes a page median.

**Q: What is an error budget?**
The failure a target permits — 99.9% over thirty days is forty-three minutes. It turns reliability
into a resource with an objective rule: budget left, ship features; budget spent, stop and fix
reliability. It also explains why a hundred percent target is wrong, since it forbids all change and
your dependencies are not a hundred percent regardless.

## Mini task

1. **Apply Little's Law to your flagship**: measure rps and average latency, compute concurrency, and
   **compare it with your thread pool and connection pool sizes.**
2. Load-test at 40%, 70%, 90% of capacity and **plot latency against utilisation.** Confirm the
   curve.
3. ⭐ **Compute the fan-out amplification for one real page**: count backend calls, take each p99, and
   calculate the probability that a page hits at least one.
4. Replace every average in your dashboards with **p50/p95/p99**. Note where the story changes.
5. ⭐ Find a place where percentiles are averaged across instances and **fix it with histograms.**
6. Write **three SLIs** users would actually feel, with SLO targets and the error budget in minutes.
7. ⭐ Slow one dependency by 500 ms under load and **watch Little's Law play out** — concurrency
   climbing, pool exhaustion, unrelated endpoints failing.
8. Memorise the latency table. **Then estimate, without a machine, how long 1 MB takes to read from
   SSD versus to send across the Atlantic.**

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes:** *"Our p99 latency has doubled but the average is unchanged. What is happening?"*

**⭐ The strong answer names a mechanism, not a list.** Something is affecting a minority of requests
— a slow shard, a cache miss path, a GC pause, one bad instance — **and then you connect it to
utilisation**: if the system has drifted from 70% to 85%, queueing alone doubles the tail while
barely moving the median. **Reaching for the utilisation curve is what distinguishes an answer that
knows the physics from one that lists suspects.**
