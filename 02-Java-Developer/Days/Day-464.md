# Day 464

**L-464** · Behavioural I — STAR, and the story bank

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> **⭐⭐ The most common behavioural failure is spending eighty percent of the answer on the situation and
> ten on what you did — because the interviewer is only scoring the part you skipped.**
>
> Today: STAR with the correct proportions, **the ten stories that answer forty questions**, the mapping
> table, and why a memorised script scores worse than rough notes.

---

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ STAR, with the proportions that matter

| ⭐ Part | ⭐ Length | ⭐ Contains |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **S**ituation | ⭐⭐ **2 sentences, maximum** | ⭐ just enough context to make the problem legible |
| ⭐ **T**ask | ⭐ 1 sentence | ⭐⭐ **what was YOUR responsibility specifically** |
| ⭐⭐ **A**ction | ⭐⭐ **60–70% of the answer** | ⭐⭐ **what YOU did, step by step, and WHY at each decision** |
| ⭐ **R**esult | ⭐ 2 sentences | ⭐⭐ **a number, plus what changed permanently** |

```
⭐⭐ THE FAILURE, which happens in most first attempts:
⭐   90 seconds of company background · 20 seconds of "so we fixed it" · no number.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ The interviewer's scorecard has boxes for OWNERSHIP, JUDGEMENT and IMPACT.
⭐     ⭐⭐ Situation fills none of them.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐ Rule of thumb: ⭐⭐ if you are 30 seconds in and have not said "so I", restart.
```

| ⭐⭐ "I" versus "we" | |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Use "we" for context, "I" for action** | ⭐⭐ "we decided" scores nothing; "I proposed X, and we chose it because Y" scores |
| ⭐ Fear of sounding arrogant | ⭐⭐ misplaced — **not claiming your work reads as not having done it** |
| ⭐ Credit others explicitly once | ⭐ it costs one clause and reads as maturity |

## ⭐ The R that most people waste

```
⭐ ❌ "...and it worked well."
⭐ ✅ ⭐⭐ "p99 went from 820 ms to 240 ms, and I added the load test to the pipeline
⭐        so a regression fails the build."
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ TWO components: the NUMBER, and ⭐⭐ THE THING THAT CHANGED PERMANENTLY.
⭐   ⭐ The second half is what distinguishes "fixed a bug" from "improved the system".
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ The story bank: ten stories, forty questions

| # | ⭐ Story | ⭐ It answers |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ 1 | ⭐⭐ **A hard technical problem you solved** | "hardest problem", "most complex thing", "proudest work" |
| ⭐ 2 | ⭐⭐ **A real failure** (Day 465) | "tell me about a failure", "a mistake", "something that went wrong" |
| ⭐ 3 | ⭐⭐ **A conflict / disagreement** (Day 465) | "disagreed with a colleague", "difficult teammate", "pushed back" |
| ⭐ 4 | ⭐ **Something you shipped end to end** | "ownership", "drove something", "beyond your role" |
| ⭐ 5 | ⭐ **Learning something fast** | "unfamiliar technology", "steep learning curve", "ambiguity" |
| ⭐ 6 | ⭐⭐ **A decision you disagreed with and committed to** | "disagree and commit", "followed a decision you opposed" |
| ⭐ 7 | ⭐ **Hard feedback you received** | "critical feedback", "how do you improve" |
| ⭐ 8 | ⭐⭐ **Prioritising / cutting scope under a deadline** | "tight deadline", "too much work", "trade-off" |
| ⭐ 9 | ⭐ **Helping someone else succeed** | "mentoring", "teamwork", "unblocked someone" |
| ⭐ 10 | ⭐ **Something you are genuinely proud of** | "proudest", "what would you show me" |

```
⭐⭐ WHY TEN IS ENOUGH: nearly every behavioural question is a re-skin of one of these.
⭐   "Tell me about a time you had to influence without authority"  ⇒ ⭐ story 3 or 6
⭐   "Tell me about a time you dealt with ambiguity"                ⇒ ⭐ story 5 or 8
⭐   "Tell me about a time you had to make a decision with incomplete data" ⇒ ⭐ story 1 or 8
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ You are not memorising 40 answers. ⭐⭐ You are indexing 10 experiences.
```

| ⭐⭐ The rule that catches people out | |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Never use the same story twice in one loop** | ⭐⭐ interviewers compare notes; repetition reads as one interesting year |
| ⭐ So: **at least 8 distinct stories**, from **at least 3 different contexts** | ⭐ projects, internships, coursework, open source |
| ⭐ Tag each story with which competencies it can serve | ⭐ so you can pick a fresh one live |

⚠️ **If your ten stories all come from one project, that is the finding, not the format.** Go and get a
second context — **an open source contribution, a small freelance job, a genuinely hard piece of
coursework** — because a single-source story bank is a career signal an interviewer will read even if
they say nothing.

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Notes, not scripts

```
⭐⭐ WRITE EACH STORY AS 5–7 BULLETS. ⭐⭐ NEVER AS PROSE.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ prose gets memorised ⇒ ⭐⭐ memorised delivery is audible and is scored DOWN
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ it also collapses the moment they interrupt with a follow-up — which they will
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ bullets force you to re-generate the sentences live, which sounds like thinking
⭐     ⭐⭐ because it IS thinking.
```

**⭐ The exception: the first sentence.** Have **one** rehearsed opening line per story that lands the
problem immediately — *"We were losing about one in every two hundred order events and nobody knew."*
**A strong first line buys you the rest of the answer.**

| ⭐ Format for each story card | |
|---|---|
| Title | one phrase, so you can find it under pressure |
| ⭐ Opening line | ⭐ the one rehearsed sentence |
| ⭐ 3–4 action bullets | ⭐⭐ **each with the *why*, not just the *what*** |
| ⭐⭐ Result + permanent change | ⭐⭐ the number and the system change |
| ⭐ Competency tags | ownership · judgement · conflict · learning · delivery |
| ⭐ The obvious follow-up | ⭐⭐ **and your answer to it** |

# Part 4 — ⭐ Frameworks you will meet, and what they are really testing

| ⭐ Company style | ⭐ What they score |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Amazon — Leadership Principles** | ⭐⭐ the most explicit version; **answers are mapped to a named principle and graded on data** |
| ⭐ Google — "Googleyness" / general cognitive | ⭐ structure, humility, collaboration |
| ⭐ Startups | ⭐⭐ **did you ship, alone, without being told to** |
| ⭐ Mid-size product companies | ⭐ ownership + communication + can you be trusted with a service |

| ⭐⭐ Beneath every framework, three questions | |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ 1. **Do you own outcomes, or tasks?** | ⭐ ownership |
| ⭐⭐ 2. **Do you make decisions with reasons?** | ⭐ judgement |
| ⭐⭐ 3. **Are you someone people want next to them at 3am?** | ⭐ collaboration under pressure |

> **⭐ Every behavioural question is one of those three, in costume.** If you can hear which one is being
> asked, **you can pick the story that answers it rather than the story you most want to tell** — which
> is the single biggest improvement most people can make.

⚠️ **Data beats adjectives, especially at Amazon-style loops.** "It was a big improvement" scores far
below "it cut the failure rate from 0.5% to 0.02%, which was about 900 fewer failed orders a week."
**Bring numbers into behavioural answers, not just technical ones.**

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **80% situation, 10% action** | ⭐⭐ the scored part is the part you skipped |
| ⭐⭐ **"We" throughout** | ⭐⭐ scores nothing — the panel cannot credit you |
| ⭐ Avoiding "I" to seem humble | ⭐⭐ not claiming your work reads as not having done it |
| ⭐ Ending with "and it worked well" | ⭐⭐ no number, no permanent change |
| ⭐ Only the number, no system change | ⭐ "fixed a bug" rather than "improved the system" |
| ⭐⭐ **Reusing one story across a loop** | ⭐⭐ interviewers compare notes |
| ⭐ Fewer than eight stories | ⭐ you will run out in a five-round loop |
| ⭐⭐ **All stories from one project** | ⭐⭐ a career signal, read silently |
| ⭐⭐ **Memorised prose** | ⭐⭐ audible, and it collapses on the first interrupt |
| ⭐ No prepared opening line | ⭐ the first fifteen seconds decide attention |
| ⭐ Not hearing which competency is being probed | ⭐⭐ answering the question you wanted |
| Adjectives instead of data | "big improvement" is unscoreable |

## Interview questions

**Q: How do you structure a behavioural answer?**
STAR, but with proportions people usually get backwards. Situation is two sentences, task is one, and
action is sixty to seventy percent of the answer — because ownership and judgement are what the scorecard
has boxes for, and neither of them lives in the situation. My check is that if I am thirty seconds in and
have not said "so I", I have spent too long on context. The result gets a number and one more thing: what
changed permanently, because that is the difference between fixing a bug and improving a system.

**Q: How many stories do you prepare?**
About ten, because nearly every behavioural question is a re-skin of one of them — a hard technical
problem, a real failure, a conflict, something shipped end to end, learning fast, disagreeing and
committing, hard feedback, cutting scope, helping someone, and something I am proud of. I am not
memorising forty answers, I am indexing ten experiences and tagging each with the competencies it can
serve. The constraint that matters is not reusing one across a loop, since interviewers compare notes and
repetition reads as one interesting year.

**Q: Why bullets rather than a written-out answer?**
Because prose gets memorised, memorised delivery is audible, and it collapses the moment they interrupt
with a follow-up — which they always do. Bullets force me to regenerate the sentences live, which sounds
like thinking because it is. The one exception is the opening line: I rehearse a single sentence per
story that lands the problem immediately, because a strong first line buys attention for the rest of the
answer.

**Q: What is actually being tested?**
Three things wearing different costumes. Do you own outcomes or tasks. Do you make decisions with
reasons you can state. And are you someone people want next to them at three in the morning. Frameworks
differ — Amazon maps answers to named leadership principles and grades hard on data, startups mostly want
evidence you shipped without being told to — but underneath it is those three. Hearing which one is being
asked lets me pick the story that answers it rather than the story I most want to tell.

**Q: Do numbers belong in behavioural answers?**
Yes, and leaving them out is the most common way a good story underperforms. "It was a big improvement"
is an adjective and cannot be scored; "it cut the failure rate from half a percent to two hundredths,
which was about nine hundred fewer failed orders a week" is evidence. It also demonstrates that I measure
things, which is itself the trait being probed — someone who reaches for a number under casual
questioning probably reaches for one at work.

## Mini task

1. ⭐⭐ Write **ten story cards** using the card format. One page each, bullets only.
2. ⭐ Write and rehearse the **single opening line** for each.
3. ⭐⭐ Tag each story with **competencies**, and build the **mapping table** from twenty common questions
   to your stories.
4. ⭐⭐ Check your **context diversity.** If eight of ten come from one project, that is today's real task.
5. ⭐ Record yourself answering three of them. **Time the situation section** — cut anything over 20
   seconds.
6. ⭐⭐ Count your **"we"s.** Rewrite each as "I did X, and we chose it because Y".
7. ⭐ Add a **number and a permanent change** to every result. Go and find the number if needed.
8. ⭐⭐ For each story, write the **obvious follow-up** and your answer to it.
9. ⭐ Have someone ask you a story question and **interrupt you mid-answer.** Practise recovering.
10. ⭐ Take twenty real behavioural questions and **map each to a story in under five seconds.** Speed
    here is the whole skill.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes:** *"Tell me about the hardest technical problem you have solved."*

**⭐ Two sentences of situation, and no more: orders were being created successfully while the downstream
event silently never fired, at about one in two hundred, and nothing detected it.**

**⭐ Then straight into action, with the why attached to each step: I reproduced it by killing the process
between the commit and the publish, which told me it was a dual write rather than a bug in the consumer.
I rejected two-phase commit because it blocks and takes availability down to the product of both systems.
I moved the event into the same transaction as the state change through an outbox table, then a relay
publishes it, keyed by aggregate id so ordering survives.**

**⭐ Then the result with both halves: zero lost events across the same kill test I used to reproduce it,
and I added that kill test to the pipeline so the regression cannot come back quietly. And I would
volunteer the limit — the relay polls, so there is up to a second of latency, and at higher volume it
becomes CDC on the outbox table.**
