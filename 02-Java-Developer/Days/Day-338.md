# Day 338

**L-338** · Observability at scale — cardinality, burn rate, and alerts people trust

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> **⭐ Monitoring tells you whether the things you predicted are happening. Observability is whether
> you can answer a question you did not think to ask in advance.**
>
> Day 182 wired up structured logs, metrics and tracing in one service. At scale, two things change
> and both are unforgiving: **cardinality decides your bill and your uptime, and alerting on causes
> instead of symptoms produces a pager nobody trusts.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ Cardinality — the thing that takes down monitoring systems

```
⭐ http_requests_total{method, status, endpoint}
⭐   5 methods × 8 statuses × 40 endpoints  = ⭐ 1,600 series. Fine.

⭐ ...add {user_id}  with 1,000,000 users
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ 1,600,000,000 series.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ Your monitoring system dies before your application does.
```

| ⭐ Rule | ⭐ Why |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Every label multiplies. Never add an unbounded one** | ⭐⭐ **user id, request id, email, URL with ids, error message** |
| ⭐ **Bucket instead** | ⭐ `plan=free/pro/enterprise`, not `user_id` |
| ⭐ **Normalise paths** | ⭐ `/users/{id}` — never `/users/12345` |
| ⭐ **Error *type*, not error message** | ⭐ a message containing an id is unbounded |
| ⭐ Set a series budget per service | ⭐ **and alert on your own metric growth** |

> **⭐ The one-line rule: metrics answer "how many and how fast"; logs and traces answer "which
> one".** Putting an identifier in a metric label is trying to make metrics do a job they cannot do,
> **and it is the most common way a team destroys its own monitoring.**

⚠️ **Histograms multiply too.** A latency histogram with 12 buckets and 3 labels is 36 series per
endpoint. **Useful, but budget for it** — and prefer histograms to summaries, because summary
quantiles **cannot be aggregated across instances** (Day 322).

## ⭐ The three signals and their real costs

| | ⭐ Cost | ⭐ Answers | ⭐ Strategy |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Metrics** | ⭐ **cheap, constant** | ⭐ how many, how fast, what percentage | ⭐ keep everything, guard cardinality |
| ⭐ **Logs** | ⭐⭐ **expensive, scales with traffic** | ⭐ what exactly happened to this request | ⭐ **sample the routine, keep all errors** |
| ⭐ **Traces** | ⭐ expensive | ⭐ **where the time went across services** | ⭐⭐ **tail-based sampling** |

> **⭐ Tail-based sampling is the detail that shows experience.** Head-based sampling decides at the
> start of a request, so you keep 1% at random — **and almost never the slow or failing ones you
> actually need.** Tail-based buffers the trace and decides *after* it completes: **keep every error,
> every trace over the p99, and 0.1% of the rest.** That is a tiny volume and it contains everything
> worth looking at.

# Part 2 — ⭐ RED, USE, and the golden signals

**⭐ Three frameworks; know which applies to what.**

| Framework | ⭐ For | ⭐ Measures |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **RED** | ⭐ **services / request-driven things** | ⭐ **Rate, Errors, Duration** |
| ⭐ **USE** | ⭐ **resources — CPU, disk, pools, queues** | ⭐ **Utilisation, Saturation, Errors** |
| ⭐ Four golden signals | services | latency, traffic, errors, **saturation** |

```
⭐ EVERY service:      rate · error rate · duration (p50/p95/p99)
⭐ EVERY resource:     utilisation · ⭐ SATURATION (queue depth, wait time) · errors
⭐ EVERY dependency:   ⭐ the same RED metrics, measured FROM THE CALLER
```

> **⭐ Saturation is the one people omit and the most predictive of the lot.** Utilisation tells you
> the present; **saturation — queue depth, connection-pool wait time, thread-pool queue length —
> tells you how close you are to the cliff on Day 322's curve.** A pool at 100% utilisation with zero
> wait time is fine; **the same pool with a rising wait time is about to fail.**

⚠️ **Measure dependencies from the caller, not only from the dependency.** The database says queries
take 2 ms; your service says the call takes 300 ms — **the 298 ms is connection-pool wait**, and
only the caller's view shows it.

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Alerting that people trust

## ⭐ Symptoms, not causes

| ⭐ Bad alert | ⭐ Why | ⭐ Good alert |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ CPU > 80% | ⭐ **so what? users may be fine** | ⭐ **p99 latency > SLO** |
| A pod restarted | it is supposed to | ⭐ **error rate > 1% for 5 min** |
| Disk 70% full | not yet a problem | ⭐ **disk full in < 4 hours at current rate** |
| ⭐ Queue depth > 1000 | ⭐ meaningless alone (Day 333) | ⭐ **time-to-drain > 10 min and rising** |

> **⭐ The rule: alert on what users experience, and use causes for diagnosis rather than paging.**
> A cause-based alert fires when nothing is wrong (CPU spikes harmlessly) and stays silent when
> something is (a bug returning wrong answers at low CPU). **Symptom alerts have the opposite
> properties.**

## ⭐⭐ Burn-rate alerting — the technique worth knowing

```
⭐ SLO 99.9% over 30 days ⇒ ⭐ error budget = 43 minutes (Day 322)

⭐ BURN RATE = how fast you are consuming it.
⭐   1×  → the budget lasts exactly 30 days
⭐   14.4× → ⭐⭐ the ENTIRE month's budget is gone in about 2 hours

⭐ MULTI-WINDOW, MULTI-BURN-RATE:
⭐   ⭐ fast: 14.4× over 1 hour   (and 5 min)  → ⭐ PAGE someone. Now.
⭐   ⭐ slow: 3×    over 6 hours  (and 30 min) → ⭐ a TICKET. Fix it this week.
```

| ⭐ Why this beats a static threshold | |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **A brief blip does not page** | ⭐ 5% errors for 30 seconds barely dents the budget |
| ⭐ **A slow bleed does** | ⭐⭐ **0.5% errors for a week exhausts it — and a "> 1%" threshold never fires** |
| ⭐ Severity is proportional to consequence | ⭐ paging is reserved for genuine budget emergencies |
| ⭐ **The short second window prevents flapping** | both windows must be burning to fire |

> **⭐ Being able to say "we alert on multi-window burn rate against the SLO, not on static error
> thresholds" places you well above the average answer**, because it shows alerting derived from an
> objective (Day 322) rather than from a number someone guessed.

## ⭐ Alert fatigue is the real failure mode

| ⭐ Symptom | ⭐ Fix |
|---|---|
| ⭐ Alerts routinely ignored | ⭐ **delete them — an ignored alert is worse than none** |
| ⭐ **No runbook** | ⭐ every alert links to one, or it is not actionable |
| One incident, forty alerts | ⭐ alert on the symptom; group and inhibit dependents |
| ⭐ **Alerts nobody owns** | ⭐ a named owner per alert |
| ⭐ Paging for non-urgent issues | ⭐ **page only for "act within 30 minutes"; everything else is a ticket** |

# Part 4 — ⭐ Correlation, and what to instrument

**⭐ The three signals are only useful when they connect:**

```
⭐ Alert (metric)  →  ⭐ trace id  →  the slow span  →  ⭐ logs for THAT request
```

| ⭐ Requirement | |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **A trace/correlation id on every log line** | ⭐⭐ **Day 182's MDC, cleared in `finally`** |
| ⭐ The id propagated across every hop | W3C `traceparent`; **including through queues** (Day 333) |
| ⭐ **Exemplars on metrics** | ⭐ a histogram bucket links to an example trace — the jump from "slow" to "why" |
| The id returned to the client | ⭐ a support ticket becomes one query |

⚠️ **Propagating a trace id through a message queue is the step teams skip**, and it is where the
end-to-end picture breaks. **Put it in a message header and restore it on consumption**, or every
async flow is an unlinkable island.

**⭐ What to instrument, in priority order:**

```
⭐ 1. RED at every service entry point            ⭐ non-negotiable
⭐ 2. RED for every outbound dependency, ⭐ from the caller's side
⭐ 3. Saturation for every pool, queue and executor
⭐ 4. ⭐ Business metrics — orders placed, payments failed
⭐    ⭐⭐ these detect the outage that all your technical metrics call healthy
⭐ 5. Build info and version, on every metric (Day 143)
```

> **⭐ Item 4 is the one that catches the worst incidents.** A deploy that silently drops 30% of
> orders can show perfect latency, zero errors and normal CPU. **The order-rate graph is the only
> thing that notices** — which is why business metrics belong in the monitoring stack, not only in
> the analytics warehouse.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ Unbounded metric labels | ⭐⭐ **cardinality explosion — monitoring dies first** |
| ⭐ Unnormalised URL paths | one series per id |
| ⭐ Error messages as labels | unbounded by construction |
| Summaries instead of histograms | ⭐ quantiles cannot be aggregated |
| ⭐ Head-based trace sampling | ⭐ **you keep the boring traces and lose the slow ones** |
| ⭐ Logging everything at scale | ⭐ cost grows with traffic; sample the routine |
| ⭐ **Alerting on causes** | ⭐⭐ **fires when fine, silent when broken** |
| ⭐ Static error thresholds | ⭐ **a slow bleed never trips them** |
| ⭐ Alerts with no runbook or owner | not actionable |
| ⭐ Paging for non-urgent issues | ⭐ **fatigue — the real failure mode** |
| ⭐ Trace ids not crossing queues | async flows become unlinkable |
| ⭐ **No business metrics** | ⭐⭐ **the silent outage nothing technical detects** |
| Measuring dependencies only from their side | pool wait time is invisible |

## Interview questions

**Q: What is the biggest operational risk in a metrics system?**
Cardinality. Every label multiplies series, so one unbounded label — a user id, a request id, a raw
URL, an error message containing an id — turns sixteen hundred series into a billion, and the
monitoring system dies before the application does. Metrics answer how many and how fast; logs and
traces answer which one. Putting an identifier in a label is asking metrics to do a job they cannot.

**Q: How do you sample traces?**
Tail-based. Head-based sampling decides when the request starts, so you keep one percent at random
and almost never the slow or failing ones. Tail-based buffers the trace and decides after it
finishes: keep every error, every trace above the p99, and a fraction of a percent of the rest. The
volume is tiny and it contains everything worth looking at.

**Q: What do you alert on?**
Symptoms users experience, not causes. CPU at eighty percent may be perfectly healthy, while a bug
returning wrong answers uses no CPU at all — so cause alerts fire when nothing is wrong and stay
silent when something is. I alert on latency against the SLO, error rate, and saturation, and keep
cause metrics for diagnosis once I am already looking.

**Q: What is burn-rate alerting?**
Alerting on how fast you are consuming the error budget rather than on a fixed error percentage. At
14.4 times the sustainable rate, a month's budget is gone in about two hours, so that pages; three
times over six hours is a ticket. It uses two windows, a long one for signal and a short one to stop
flapping. It beats static thresholds in both directions — a thirty-second blip does not wake anyone,
and a half-percent error rate bleeding for a week does, which a "greater than one percent" rule never
catches.

**Q: What metric catches the outage nothing else does?**
A business metric — orders placed per minute, payments succeeded. A deploy that silently drops thirty
percent of orders can show normal latency, zero errors and healthy CPU, because from the system's
point of view nothing failed. The order-rate graph is the only thing that notices, which is why those
metrics belong in the monitoring stack and not just the analytics warehouse.

## Mini task

1. ⭐ **Count your metric series.** Find the highest-cardinality label. **Compute what happens if
   your user count multiplies by ten.**
2. Add a `user_id` label in a test environment, generate 10,000 users, and **watch the series count
   and memory.** Then remove it.
3. Find any unnormalised path metric and **fix it to `/users/{id}`.**
4. ⭐ Implement **tail-based sampling**: keep all errors, all traces over p99, 0.1% otherwise.
   **Compare volume and usefulness against 1% head sampling.**
5. Add **RED to every endpoint and every outbound dependency from the caller's side.** Find one place
   where caller and dependency timings disagree — **that gap is pool wait time.**
6. Add **saturation** for every pool and executor. Load-test until one saturates and **confirm the
   metric moves before latency does.**
7. ⭐⭐ Implement **multi-window burn-rate alerts** for one SLO. Then simulate a 30-second 5% blip
   (should not page) and a week-long 0.5% bleed (should).
8. ⭐ Propagate a trace id **through a queue.** Confirm one trace spans producer and consumer.
9. ⭐ Add **two business metrics** and alert on a sustained drop. **Simulate a silent failure** that
   your technical metrics call healthy.
10. **Audit every alert**: does it have an owner and a runbook, and did anyone act on it last time?
    **Delete the ones that fail.**

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Three minutes:** *"You're on call. What do you want instrumented before the first page?"*

**⭐ The answer that scores is a priority order, not a list.** RED at every entry point and every
outbound dependency measured from the caller · saturation on every pool · **business metrics, because
they catch the outage nothing technical sees** · a correlation id on every log line that survives
queues · and tail-sampled traces.

**⭐ Then the alerting philosophy in one sentence** — symptoms not causes, burn rate not thresholds,
and every alert with an owner and a runbook. **Close on alert fatigue: an alert nobody acts on is
worse than no alert, so I delete them.** That last line is what makes it sound like on-call
experience rather than a tooling list.
