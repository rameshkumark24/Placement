# Day 386

**L-386** · S3 — the object store, presigned URLs, and four cost traps

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> **⭐⭐ S3 is not a filesystem with a URL. There are no directories, there is no rename, and there is no
> partial write — and every S3 mistake comes from forgetting one of those three.**
>
> Today: what strong consistency changed (and how much published advice predates it), **the storage-class
> maths that makes archiving small objects cost *more***, presigned URLs as an architectural move, and
> **the incomplete multipart uploads almost every account is silently paying for.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ The object store model

| ⭐ Property | ⭐ Consequence |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **No directories — only key prefixes** | ⭐⭐ `a/b/c.txt` is one flat key; the "folder" is a UI fiction |
| ⭐⭐ **No rename** | ⭐⭐ **rename = copy + delete**, and it costs and takes time proportional to size |
| ⭐⭐ **No partial write / append** | ⭐⭐ you replace the whole object; concurrent writers **last-writer-wins** |
| ⭐ Objects up to 5 TB | ⭐ >100 MB should use multipart upload |
| ⭐⭐ **11 nines of durability** | ⭐⭐ **you cannot build this yourself — it is the reason to use it** |
| ⭐ Availability ~99.9–99.99% | ⭐ durable ≠ always reachable |

```
⭐⭐ STRONG READ-AFTER-WRITE CONSISTENCY since December 2020 — for PUT, DELETE and LIST.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ A huge amount of published advice, blog posts and interview folklore PREDATES this
⭐     and tells you to work around eventual consistency. ⭐ Knowing the date is the point.
```

⚠️ **Consistency is not concurrency control.** Two writers to the same key still race and one silently
wins. **If that matters, use conditional writes (`If-Match` with an ETag) or keep the mutable state in a
database and the immutable bytes in S3** — which is the correct pattern anyway.

## ⭐ Throughput and prefixes

| | |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **3,500 PUT/POST/DELETE and 5,500 GET per second — per prefix** | ⭐ and prefixes scale horizontally |
| ⭐⭐ So a hot single prefix is a real limit | ⭐⭐ `uploads/2026-08-14/...` concentrates load on one date |
| ⭐ The fix | ⭐ spread the high-cardinality part **early** in the key: `uploads/{hash}/{date}/...` |

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ Storage classes, and the maths people skip

| Class | ⭐ Cost/GB-mo | ⭐ Retrieval | ⭐⭐ Minimum duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Standard** | ~$0.023 | instant, free | ⭐ none |
| ⭐ **Standard-IA** | ~$0.0125 | ⭐ instant, **per-GB retrieval fee** | ⭐⭐ **30 days** |
| ⭐ One Zone-IA | ~$0.010 | instant + fee | ⭐⭐ 30 days, **one AZ — it can be lost** |
| ⭐ **Glacier Instant** | ~$0.004 | instant + higher fee | ⭐⭐ **90 days** |
| ⭐ Glacier Flexible | ~$0.0036 | ⭐ minutes to hours | ⭐⭐ 90 days |
| ⭐⭐ **Deep Archive** | ⭐⭐ ~$0.00099 | ⭐⭐ **12 hours** | ⭐⭐ **180 days** |
| ⭐⭐ **Intelligent-Tiering** | ⭐ auto-tiers | ⭐ instant | ⭐⭐ small monitoring fee per object |

```
⭐⭐ TRAP 1 — THE ARCHIVING PARADOX
⭐ You lifecycle 50 MILLION objects averaging 8 KB to Standard-IA to "save money".
⭐   Storage saved:  400 GB × ($0.023 − $0.0125)  ≈ ⭐ $4.20/month
⭐   Transition cost: 50,000,000 × $0.01/1,000     ≈ ⭐⭐ $500 ONE-OFF
⭐   ⭐⭐ AND IA bills a MINIMUM OBJECT SIZE of 128 KB — so each 8 KB object is billed as 128 KB.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ You now pay MORE per month than before, and paid $500 for the privilege.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE RULE: lifecycle policies pay off on FEW LARGE objects, not MANY SMALL ones.
```

> **⭐ Intelligent-Tiering is the honest default when you do not know the access pattern** — it moves
> objects automatically with no retrieval fees between the instant tiers, at the cost of a small
> per-object monitoring charge. **That per-object charge is itself the reason it is wrong for billions
> of tiny objects.** The shape of the answer is always: **object size and object count first, class
> second.**

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Versioning, lifecycle and the two silent bills

```
⭐⭐ TRAP 2 — VERSIONING WITH NO EXPIRY
⭐ Versioning ON, no noncurrent-version expiration rule.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ Every overwrite keeps the old object FOREVER. A "delete" writes a delete marker
⭐     and frees NOTHING. ⭐⭐ A 200 GB bucket quietly becomes 4 TB.
⭐ ⇒ Fix: NoncurrentVersionExpiration (e.g. 30 days) on EVERY versioned bucket.

⭐⭐ TRAP 3 — INCOMPLETE MULTIPART UPLOADS
⭐ A multipart upload that fails leaves its uploaded PARTS in the bucket.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ They are BILLED, they do NOT appear in ListObjects, and NOTHING cleans them up.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ Fix — the one-rule lifecycle policy nearly every account is missing:
⭐     AbortIncompleteMultipartUpload: DaysAfterInitiation = 7
```

⚠️ **Trap 3 is the one to remember by name.** The parts are invisible in the console object list, so
people see a 50 GB bucket on a bill that says 900 GB and cannot explain it. **`aws s3api
list-multipart-uploads` is how you find them.** Add the abort rule to every bucket in your Terraform
module and it never happens again.

| ⭐ Protection worth having | |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Versioning + MFA Delete** | ⭐ ransomware and accidental-delete protection |
| ⭐⭐ **Object Lock (WORM)** | ⭐⭐ **immutable for a retention period — cannot be deleted by anyone, including root** |
| ⭐ Replication (CRR/SRR) | ⭐ another region or account — **and a separate account is the real backup** |
| ⭐ Lifecycle to Deep Archive | cheap long-tail compliance retention |

> **⭐ "Our backup is S3 versioning" is not a backup if the same credentials can delete the bucket.** A
> real backup is **in another account** that the production role cannot write to. **Object Lock in a
> separate account is the version of this that survives a compromised administrator.**

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ Presigned URLs: the architectural move

```
⭐⭐ WITHOUT: browser → your app (100 MB through your JVM) → S3
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ your heap, your threads, your bandwidth, your timeout, ⭐⭐ your scaling problem

⭐⭐ WITH:    browser asks your app for a presigned PUT URL   (⭐ tiny, fast, authorised)
⭐           browser uploads DIRECTLY to S3                   (⭐⭐ your app sees none of the bytes)
⭐           S3 event → SQS → your worker processes it        (⭐ async, Day 393)
```

```java
// ⭐ Generate a presigned PUT valid for 15 minutes
PutObjectRequest put = PutObjectRequest.builder()
    .bucket("uploads").key("u/" + userId + "/" + UUID.randomUUID())
    .contentType("image/jpeg").build();

PresignedPutObjectRequest presigned = presigner.presignPutObject(
    PutObjectPresignRequest.builder()
        .signatureDuration(Duration.ofMinutes(15))
        .putObjectRequest(put).build());
```

| ⭐ What you must still control | |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **You choose the key** | ⭐⭐ never let the client pick — path traversal and overwrite |
| ⭐ Short expiry | 15 minutes, not 7 days |
| ⭐⭐ **Size cannot be enforced on a presigned PUT** | ⭐⭐ use **POST with a policy** (`content-length-range`) if you must cap it |
| ⭐ Content type conditions | pin it in the signature |
| ⭐ Validate **after** upload | ⭐ the S3 event triggers your check — a signed URL is not a virus scanner |

> **⭐⭐ This is Day 327's rule made concrete: bytes should not pass through your application.** The
> presigned URL carries your authorisation without carrying your bandwidth. **It also means your upload
> path scales like S3 rather than like your task count.**

# Part 5 — ⭐ Access control, and the only thing that matters

| Control | ⭐ Verdict |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Block Public Access** | ⭐⭐ **on, at the account level, always** — the setting that ended the era of leaked buckets |
| ⭐ Bucket policy | ⭐ the primary control (Day 381) |
| ⭐ ACLs | ⭐⭐ **disabled by default now** — leave them disabled |
| ⭐⭐ Presigned URL | ⭐ time-limited access without making anything public |
| ⭐⭐ **CloudFront + OAC** | ⭐⭐ **public content served from a PRIVATE bucket** (Day 392) |
| ⭐ SSE-S3 / SSE-KMS | ⭐ encrypted at rest by default; KMS when you need key control (Day 395) |

⚠️ **"The bucket needs to be public because the website serves images from it" is wrong, and knowing why
is a small credibility marker.** CloudFront with an **Origin Access Control** reads the private bucket on
your behalf — **cheaper egress, cached at the edge, and the bucket stays private** (Day 392).

```
⭐⭐ TRAP 4 — REQUESTER PAYS FOR YOUR MISTAKES
⭐ Public bucket + a popular file + no CloudFront = ⭐⭐ you pay $0.09/GB for every download,
⭐ including by scrapers and by anyone who hot-links it. ⭐ There is no cap.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐ CloudFront in front is CHEAPER per GB and gives you a WAF and rate limiting.
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts |
|---|---|
| ⭐ Treating S3 as a filesystem | ⭐⭐ no rename, no append, no directories |
| ⭐ Designing around eventual consistency | ⭐ strong since Dec 2020 — the advice is stale |
| ⭐ Assuming consistency solves concurrent writes | ⭐⭐ last-writer-wins still applies |
| ⭐ A single hot key prefix | ⭐ per-prefix request limits |
| ⭐⭐ **Lifecycling millions of tiny objects to IA** | ⭐⭐ transition requests + 128 KB minimum ⇒ **costs more** |
| ⭐⭐ **Versioning without noncurrent expiry** | ⭐⭐ the bucket grows forever; deletes free nothing |
| ⭐⭐ **No abort-incomplete-multipart rule** | ⭐⭐ invisible, unlisted, billed parts |
| ⭐ Calling versioning a backup | ⭐⭐ same credentials can destroy it — use another account |
| ⭐⭐ **Streaming uploads through the application** | ⭐⭐ your heap and threads for someone else's bytes |
| ⭐ Letting the client choose the object key | ⭐ traversal and overwrite |
| ⭐ Assuming a presigned PUT can cap size | ⭐ it cannot — POST policy can |
| ⭐⭐ **A public bucket for web assets** | ⭐⭐ uncapped egress; CloudFront + OAC is cheaper and private |

## Interview questions

**Q: What is the mental model for S3?**
A flat key-value store of immutable objects with eleven nines of durability. There are no directories —
prefixes just look like folders in the console. There is no rename, so moving an object is a copy plus a
delete that costs time and money proportional to size. And there is no partial write or append, so
concurrent writers to the same key race and one silently wins. Almost every S3 design mistake is one of
those three forgotten. Reads have been strongly consistent after writes since the end of 2020, which is
worth knowing because a lot of published guidance predates it.

**Q: When does moving objects to a cheaper storage class cost you money?**
When the objects are small and numerous. Transitions are billed per request, so fifty million objects at
a cent per thousand is five hundred dollars up front, and infrequent-access classes bill a minimum object
size of a hundred and twenty-eight kilobytes, so an eight-kilobyte object is billed at sixteen times its
size. The result is a higher monthly bill plus a one-off charge. Lifecycle policies pay off on few large
objects; for many small ones the answer is either leave them in Standard or aggregate them first.

**Q: A bucket's billed size is far larger than the objects in it. Why?**
Two candidates. Versioning without a noncurrent-version expiration rule — every overwrite retains the old
version and a delete only writes a delete marker, so nothing is ever freed. Or incomplete multipart
uploads: failed uploads leave their parts behind, they are billed, and they do not appear in a normal
object listing, so the console shows fifty gigabytes and the bill says nine hundred. `list-multipart-
uploads` finds them, and the fix is a lifecycle rule aborting incomplete uploads after seven days, which
belongs in every bucket module.

**Q: How should a user upload a 200 MB file?**
Not through my application. The client asks for a presigned PUT URL, which is a small authorised
response, and then uploads directly to S3, so no bytes touch my heap, threads or bandwidth. S3 emits an
event to a queue and a worker does the processing asynchronously. I choose the key server-side rather
than letting the client pick, keep the expiry to about fifteen minutes, and validate content after
upload — a signed URL authorises, it does not inspect. If I need to enforce a maximum size at upload
time, it has to be a POST policy with a content-length-range, because a presigned PUT cannot express
that.

**Q: How do you serve public images without a public bucket?**
CloudFront with an Origin Access Control. The bucket stays private and only CloudFront can read it, the
content is cached at the edge so most requests never reach S3 at all, and CloudFront egress is cheaper
per gigabyte than S3 egress — so it is simultaneously more secure and less expensive. It also gives you
somewhere to attach WAF rules and rate limiting. A public bucket means uncapped egress charges for
anything anyone hot-links, with no control point at all.
