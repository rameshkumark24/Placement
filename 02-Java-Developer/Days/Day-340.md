# Day 340

**L-340** · Microservices vs monolith — Conway's law and the distributed monolith

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> **⭐ Microservices are an organisational technology. They solve a people problem and charge you in
> distributed-systems problems.**
>
> Day 306 argued the modular monolith at code scale. Today is the org-scale version — **the
> preconditions that must exist before a split helps, and the specific signature of the failure mode
> that gets you the costs without the benefits.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ Conway's law

> **⭐ "Organisations design systems that mirror their own communication structure." — Conway, 1967.**
> It is not advice. **It is an observation that holds whether you plan for it or not.**

| ⭐ Org shape | ⭐ Architecture you will get |
|---|---|
| ⭐ Frontend team, backend team, DBA team | ⭐ **a three-layer architecture with a shared database** |
| ⭐ **Teams by business capability** | ⭐ **services by business capability** |
| One team, one codebase | a monolith — and a coherent one |
| ⭐ Four teams sharing one deploy pipeline | ⭐⭐ **a distributed monolith, guaranteed** |

**⭐ The inverse Conway manoeuvre: change the org chart to get the architecture you want.** If you
want independently deployable services, **you need independently deployable teams** — with their own
on-call, their own pipeline and the authority to release. **Drawing service boundaries that do not
match team boundaries produces boundaries nobody defends.**

> **⭐ The sentence that reframes the whole question: "we should split into services" is a statement
> about the team, not the code.** If one team owns everything, splitting adds network calls between
> things that will still be deployed together.

# Part 2 — ⭐ What splitting buys, and what each benefit requires

| ⭐ Benefit | ⭐ Precondition — without this you do not get it |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Independent deploys** | ⭐⭐ **backward-compatible contracts and no shared database** |
| ⭐ **Independent scaling** | ⭐ the service genuinely has a different load profile |
| ⭐ **Failure isolation** | ⭐⭐ **timeouts, bulkheads, fallbacks (Day 339) — otherwise failure propagates anyway** |
| ⭐ **Team autonomy** | ⭐ that team owns deploy and on-call |
| Technology diversity | ⭐ **you can operate more than one stack** — usually a cost, not a benefit |

⚠️ **Every one of those is conditional, and the conditions are where teams fail.** Splitting a system
into six services that share a database and deploy together gives you **six times the operational
surface and none of the independence.**

## ⭐⭐ The preconditions checklist

**⭐ Before splitting anything, all of these must exist:**

```
⭐ 1. CI/CD per service, ⭐ deployable without coordinating with anyone
⭐ 2. Distributed tracing with correlation ids across every hop (Day 338)
⭐ 3. Centralised, searchable, structured logs
⭐ 4. Service discovery and health checking (Day 341)
⭐ 5. ⭐ Contract testing — or every deploy is a coordinated deploy
⭐ 6. ⭐ On-call ownership per service, with runbooks
⭐ 7. A platform capability — someone owns the pipeline, the mesh, the standards
⭐ 8. ⭐⭐ DATABASE PER SERVICE — the one that is skipped, and the one that matters most
```

> **⭐ Item 8 is not negotiable, and it is the one people compromise on first.** A shared database
> means a schema change requires coordinating every service that reads the table — **so you have
> distributed the code and not the coupling**, which is the worst of both.

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ The distributed monolith

**⭐ Learn the signature. Interviewers ask about it, and you will meet it.**

| ⭐ Symptom | ⭐ What it means |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Services must be deployed together, in order** | ⭐⭐ **no independent deploy — the primary benefit, gone** |
| ⭐ **A shared database** | schema changes are cross-team events |
| ⭐ One feature touches five repositories | ⭐ **the boundaries are wrong** — they cut across a capability |
| ⭐ **Long synchronous call chains** | ⭐ availability multiplies down: 5 × 99.9% ≈ 99.5% |
| ⭐ A shared library everyone must upgrade in lockstep | ⭐ a monolith with extra steps |
| ⭐ **No service can be tested alone** | you need the whole system running locally |
| Nobody can say who owns a service | Conway's law, failing |

```
⭐ THE AVAILABILITY ARITHMETIC — say this out loud:
⭐   A synchronous chain of 5 services at 99.9% each
⭐   ⇒ 0.999^5 ≈ ⭐⭐ 99.5%  ⇒ ⭐ 3.6 HOURS of downtime per month instead of 43 minutes.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ Every synchronous dependency you add makes you LESS available.
```

> **⭐ That arithmetic is the strongest single argument against chatty synchronous designs**, and it
> is why asynchronous messaging (Day 333) and caching are not just performance tools — **they are
> availability tools, because they remove a term from that product.**

# Part 4 — ⭐ Sizing, splitting, and the honest recommendation

## ⭐ How big is a service?

| ⭐ Bad heuristic | ⭐ Better |
|---|---|
| ⭐ "Micro" — as small as possible | ⭐⭐ **a bounded context** (Day 305) |
| One service per entity | ⭐ **one service per business capability** |
| Two-pizza team per service | ⭐ **a team can own several services; a service cannot have several owners** |
| Lines of code | ⭐ **can one team understand, deploy and be on call for it?** |

> **⭐ "Order Service" and "Payment Service" are capabilities. "User Service" that everything calls is
> a shared database with an HTTP interface** — and it becomes the bottleneck every other team waits
> on.

## ⭐ How to split — the strangler, applied

```
⭐ 1. Modularise INSIDE the monolith first (Day 306). ⭐ If you cannot draw the module, you cannot extract it.
⭐ 2. Split the DATA first — schema per module, no cross-schema queries or FKs (Day 306)
⭐ 3. Route through a facade so callers do not know where the code lives
⭐ 4. Extract ONE service — ⭐ the one with a real reason (team, scale, isolation, technology)
⭐ 5. Make the API coarse-grained — ⭐ an in-process loop becomes an N+1 over the network
⭐ 6. Add timeouts, retries, breakers, idempotency (Days 335, 339)
⭐ 7. Replace transactions crossing the new boundary with a saga (Day 342)
⭐ 8. ⭐ Measure. Then decide whether to do it again.
```

⚠️ **Step 8 is the discipline.** Most successful microservice architectures were extracted one
service at a time from a monolith that worked. **Most failures started by splitting everything at
once, before the domain was understood** (Day 306: you cannot draw the right boundaries in year one).

> **⭐ The recommendation to give, and to defend: start as a modular monolith, extract when a specific
> reason appears, and expect the answer to be "three or four services", not thirty.** Naming a small
> number is a stronger signal than naming a big one.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts |
|---|---|
| ⭐ Splitting code without splitting teams | ⭐ Conway's law wins; boundaries nobody defends |
| ⭐⭐ **A shared database across services** | ⭐⭐ **distributed code, undistributed coupling** |
| ⭐ Services that deploy together | ⭐ the primary benefit, discarded |
| ⭐ **Long synchronous chains** | ⭐⭐ **availability multiplies down** |
| ⭐ Splitting before the domain is understood | boundaries you cannot change cheaply |
| ⭐ Per-entity services | ⭐ a chatty graph; one feature spans five repos |
| A "User Service" everyone calls | a shared database with HTTP in front |
| ⭐ Fine-grained remote APIs | ⭐ an N+1 over the network |
| Splitting all at once | the failure mode of most migrations |
| ⭐ **No contract testing** | ⭐ every deploy becomes a coordinated deploy |
| Claiming failure isolation with no timeouts | failure propagates regardless (Day 339) |

## Interview questions

**Q: Microservices or a monolith?**
A modular monolith by default, extracted one service at a time when a specific reason appears — a
team boundary causing deploy contention, a genuinely different scaling profile, failure isolation
that matters, or a technology requirement. Microservices solve an organisational problem and charge
you in distributed-systems problems, so if you do not have the organisational problem you have bought
the bill without the goods.

**Q: What is Conway's law and why does it matter here?**
That a system's structure mirrors the communication structure of the organisation that built it. It
matters because service boundaries that do not match team boundaries are boundaries nobody defends —
they erode within months. The useful version is the inverse manoeuvre: if you want independently
deployable services, first create independently deployable teams, with their own pipeline and
on-call.

**Q: What is a distributed monolith and how do you recognise one?**
Services that must be deployed together, usually because they share a database or a lockstep library,
with long synchronous call chains and features that touch five repositories. It has every cost of
distribution — network failures, tracing, operational multiplication — and none of the independence.
The clearest test is whether any single service can be deployed on its own, on a Friday, without
telling anyone.

**Q: Why are synchronous chains an availability problem?**
Because availability multiplies. Five services at three nines each in a synchronous chain give about
99.5%, which is three and a half hours of downtime a month rather than forty-three minutes. That is
the real argument for asynchronous messaging and caching — they are availability tools, not just
performance ones, because they remove a term from that product.

**Q: How big should a service be?**
A bounded context — one business capability, ownable by one team who can deploy it and be on call for
it. Not "micro", and not one per entity, which produces a chatty graph where every feature spans five
repositories. A team can own several services; a service cannot have several owners.

## Mini task

1. **Draw your org chart, then your architecture.** Note every place they disagree — **each is a
   boundary under pressure.**
2. ⭐ **Run the preconditions checklist** honestly against your situation. **Count how many of the
   eight you have.**
3. ⭐ Compute the **availability of your longest synchronous chain.** Then remove one hop with a cache
   or an async event and recompute.
4. ⭐ Find one feature and **count the repositories it touches.** More than two is a boundary problem.
5. Take one service and ask: **can it be deployed alone, right now, without telling anyone?** If not,
   find out exactly what stops it.
6. ⭐ Check for a shared database. **List every service reading each table** — that list is your real
   coupling graph.
7. ⭐ Pick your **most extractable module** (Day 306) and write the eight-step plan for it,
   **including which transactions become sagas.** Do not extract it.
8. Find one fine-grained remote API and **count the calls a typical page makes.** Redesign it
   coarse-grained.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Three minutes:** *"We have twelve engineers and a monolith that's getting hard to work in. Should
we go microservices?"*

**⭐ The marker: you diagnose before prescribing.** *"Hard to work in" could be poor modularity, slow
tests, or deploy contention — **and only the third is a microservices problem.** Twelve engineers is
two or three teams, so the answer is at most two or three services, extracted one at a time, after
the modules exist inside the monolith.

**⭐ Then run the preconditions out loud and let them answer.** If they do not have database-per-
service, contract testing and per-team on-call, **splitting produces a distributed monolith** — and
saying that plainly is worth more than any diagram.
