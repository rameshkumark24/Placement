# Day 353

**L-353** · Design 9 — video streaming

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** BUILD

> **⭐ This is the first design where the dominant cost is not compute or storage. It is egress
> bandwidth — and it is not close.**
>
> Day 323 predicted it: ten thousand concurrent viewers at 5 Mbps is fifty gigabits per second. **The
> architecture is therefore "a CDN with an origin", and everything you build exists to feed it.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐ Requirements, and the number that dictates the shape

| ⭐ Functional | ⭐ Non-functional |
|---|---|
| Upload a video | ⭐⭐ **start playback < 2 s** |
| ⭐ **Transcode to multiple qualities** | ⭐ **no rebuffering** — the only metric users feel |
| ⭐ **Adaptive streaming** | ⭐ availability of playback 99.99% |
| Seek anywhere | ⭐ **upload may be slow; processing may take minutes** |
| Thumbnails, captions | ⭐⭐ **cost per delivered hour is a first-class requirement** |
| View counts | |

```
⭐ ESTIMATE
⭐   1M daily viewers · 30 min each · average bitrate 3 Mbps
⭐   ⭐ Concurrent at peak ≈ 1M × (30/1440) × 3 (peak factor) ≈ ⭐ 60,000 concurrent
⭐   ⭐⭐ EGRESS: 60,000 × 3 Mbps = ⭐⭐ 180 Gbps sustained
⭐   Uploads: 10k videos/day × 500 MB = 5 TB/day ingest
⭐   ⭐ Transcoded output ≈ 2–3× the source (five renditions) ⇒ ~15 TB/day stored
⭐   ⭐ Transcode CPU: 10k videos × ~5 min of CPU per rendition × 5 ⇒ ⭐ a large batch fleet
```

> **⭐ 180 Gbps is the number that decides the architecture.** No origin fleet you would build serves
> that economically. **A CDN is not an optimisation here — it is the delivery system**, and your
> services exist to produce files it can cache.

⚠️ **And say the cost framing, because it is unusual among these designs:** egress typically dominates
the bill, **so decisions that reduce bytes delivered — better codecs, right-sized renditions,
per-title encoding — are worth more than any efficiency you can find in your own code.**

# Part 2 — ⭐ Upload and the processing pipeline

```
⭐ client ──presigned multipart──► ⭐ OBJECT STORAGE (Day 327)   ⭐ bytes never touch your API
⭐                                        │ event
⭐                                        ▼
⭐   ⭐ SPLIT into chunks ──► ⭐ PARALLEL TRANSCODE (fan-out) ──► ⭐ PACKAGE (HLS/DASH)
⭐                                        │                             │
⭐                          thumbnails · captions · scan          ⭐ manifests + segments
⭐                                                                      ▼
⭐                                                              ⭐ ORIGIN STORAGE → CDN
```

| ⭐ Decision | ⭐ Reason |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Presigned multipart upload** | ⭐ a dropped mobile connection resumes; **no bytes through your service** |
| ⭐⭐ **Chunk, then transcode in parallel** | ⭐⭐ **a 2-hour film transcoded serially takes hours; split into 30-second chunks and it takes minutes** |
| ⭐ Renditions: 240p → 1080p (+4K) | one source in, five outputs |
| ⭐ **Status is a state machine** | `UPLOADED → TRANSCODING → PARTIALLY_READY → READY` |
| ⭐ Publish progressively | ⭐ **make 480p available while 1080p is still encoding** — the user can watch |

> **⭐ Parallel chunk transcoding is the deep-dive-worthy detail on the ingest side, and it has a
> catch: chunks must be split on keyframe boundaries**, or the segments cannot be decoded
> independently and the joins glitch. **The encoder is forced to insert keyframes at fixed intervals
> so that chunking, segmenting and quality switching all align.**

⚠️ **Transcoding is a batch workload with an unusual property: it is interruptible.** That makes it a
natural fit for cheap preemptible capacity — **and it means the queue must handle a worker vanishing
mid-chunk**, which chunk-level idempotency already gives you.

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ The deep dive: adaptive bitrate streaming

```
⭐ Video is not a file. It is ⭐ SEGMENTS (2–10 s each) at several qualities, plus a MANIFEST.

⭐ manifest.m3u8
⭐   ├─ 240p/  seg001.ts seg002.ts ...
⭐   ├─ 480p/  seg001.ts seg002.ts ...
⭐   ├─ 720p/  seg001.ts seg002.ts ...
⭐   └─ 1080p/ seg001.ts seg002.ts ...
⭐ ⭐⭐ THE CLIENT chooses which quality to fetch, ⭐ SEGMENT BY SEGMENT.
```

| ⭐ Consequence | ⭐ Why it matters |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **The server is dumb; the player is smart** | ⭐⭐ **no session state at all — every request is a plain cacheable GET** |
| ⭐ **Every segment is immutable** | ⭐ perfect CDN cacheability with a long TTL (Day 327) |
| ⭐ Quality adapts mid-playback | a train tunnel drops you to 240p rather than stalling |
| ⭐ **Seeking is free** | ⭐ jump to the segment covering that timestamp |
| Startup is fast | ⭐ **fetch the lowest rendition first, then climb** |

## ⭐ The player's algorithm, and the trade-off inside it

```
⭐ 1. Measure recent throughput (per-segment download speed)
⭐ 2. Check the BUFFER level (seconds of video held ahead)
⭐ 3. Choose the next segment's quality:
⭐      ⭐ buffer healthy AND throughput high  → step UP (one level, not to the top)
⭐      ⭐ buffer draining                      → step DOWN immediately
⭐ 4. Repeat every segment
```

| ⭐ Tension | |
|---|---|
| ⭐ Bigger segments | better compression, ⭐ **slower to adapt** |
| ⭐ Smaller segments | ⭐ responsive, **more requests and more overhead** |
| ⭐⭐ Typical: 2–6 seconds | the industry compromise |
| ⭐ **Aggressive upswitching** | ⭐⭐ a rebuffer, which users hate more than lower quality |

> **⭐ The principle to state: rebuffering is far worse than low quality.** A player that switches up
> eagerly and stalls scores worse on every user metric than one that sits conservatively at 720p.
> **That asymmetry is why buffer level, not throughput alone, drives the decision.**

⚠️ **Per-title encoding is the refinement worth naming:** a fixed ladder wastes bits on simple
content (a talking head at 1080p needs far less than an action scene). **Encoding each title at
bitrates chosen from its own complexity cuts egress materially — and egress is the bill.**

# Part 4 — ⭐ Delivery, live, and what breaks

## ⭐ CDN and storage tiers

| Concern | ⭐ Design |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Delivery** | ⭐⭐ **CDN serves ~99% of bytes; the origin sees almost nothing** |
| ⭐ Cache key | ⭐ the segment URL is immutable — **long TTL, no invalidation needed** (Day 327) |
| ⭐ **Popularity skew** | ⭐ a tiny fraction of videos is most of the traffic — **the long tail may miss the edge and that is fine** |
| ⭐ **Storage tiering** | ⭐ hot renditions on fast storage; **cold titles to archive, with a re-warm delay** |
| ⭐ Multi-CDN | ⭐ cost negotiation and failover — **choose per region by measured performance** |
| Private content | ⭐ signed URLs or DRM; **the manifest is the thing to protect** |

⚠️ **Do not delete unpopular renditions to save storage** without checking: the archive re-warm delay
becomes a playback failure for the one viewer a month who wants it. **Tier it, do not drop it.**

## ⭐ Live streaming — what changes

| | ⭐ VOD | ⭐ Live |
|---|---|---|
| Source | a complete file | ⭐ a continuous ingest (RTMP/SRT) |
| ⭐ **Transcode** | ⭐ batch, parallel, interruptible | ⭐⭐ **real time — you cannot fall behind, ever** |
| ⭐ Latency | irrelevant | ⭐⭐ **the whole problem: 5–30 s normal, 2–5 s with LL-HLS** |
| Manifest | static | ⭐ **rolling — updated every segment** |
| ⭐ Failure | retry the job | ⭐⭐ **the moment is gone — no retry exists** |
| DVR / rewind | inherent | a retained window, then it becomes VOD |

> **⭐ The live constraint is that transcoding must keep up in real time**, so there is no elasticity
> to lean on: **falling behind means dropping frames or increasing latency**, and neither can be
> retried. That is why live pipelines are provisioned rather than autoscaled.

## ⭐ What breaks

| ⭐ Breaks first | ⭐ Mitigation |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Egress cost** | ⭐⭐ **per-title encoding, better codecs, right-sized ladders — bytes are the bill** |
| ⭐ A viral video at the edge | ⭐ the CDN handles it — **this is what it is for** |
| ⭐ **Transcode backlog** | ⭐ queue depth = publish delay; **publish low renditions first** |
| Origin storage request rate | hashed prefixes (Day 327) |
| ⭐ Cold-tier retrieval | a slow first play for old content |
| ⭐ **Player rebuffering on poor networks** | conservative ABR, smaller startup segments |

```
⭐ MONITOR: ⭐⭐ REBUFFER RATIO and startup time (the only metrics users feel) · CDN hit rate
⭐          ⭐ egress bytes and cost per delivered hour · transcode queue depth
⭐          ⭐ plays started/min (business metric) · error rate by rendition
⭐ CUT:     recommendations, comments, DRM (⭐ name it), live, 4K
⭐ AT 10×:  ⭐ more CDN capacity and more transcode workers — ⭐ the shape is unchanged
```

## ⭐ Traps

| Trap | Consequence |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ Serving video from your own servers | ⭐⭐ **180 Gbps you cannot afford or operate** |
| ⭐ Uploads through the API | Day 327 — threads and bandwidth |
| ⭐ **Serial transcoding** | ⭐ hours instead of minutes |
| ⭐ Chunking off keyframe boundaries | ⭐ **segments that cannot decode independently** |
| ⭐ **Server-side quality selection** | ⭐⭐ session state, and it defeats CDN caching |
| ⭐ One giant file per quality | no seeking, no adaptation, no caching granularity |
| ⭐ Aggressive upswitching | ⭐ **rebuffers, which users hate more than 480p** |
| ⭐ A fixed bitrate ladder for all content | wasted egress on simple video |
| ⭐ **Waiting for all renditions before publishing** | minutes of avoidable delay |
| Deleting cold renditions | a playback failure for the rare viewer |
| Treating live as VOD with a shorter delay | real-time transcoding has no retry |
| ⭐ Optimising code before bytes | ⭐ **egress is the bill; your CPU is not** |

## Interview questions

**Q: What dominates this design?**
Egress bandwidth. Sixty thousand concurrent viewers at three megabits is a hundred and eighty
gigabits per second sustained, which no origin fleet I would build serves economically. So the
delivery system is a CDN, and everything I build exists to produce immutable, cacheable files for it.
It also reframes optimisation: reducing bytes delivered — better codecs, per-title encoding, a
right-sized ladder — is worth more than any efficiency in my own code.

**Q: How does adaptive bitrate work?**
The video is stored as short segments at several qualities plus a manifest, and the client chooses
which quality to fetch for each segment based on measured throughput and, more importantly, its
buffer level. The server is dumb — every request is a plain cacheable GET with no session state,
which is exactly what makes CDN delivery possible. It also gives free seeking and fast startup, since
you begin at a low rendition and climb.

**Q: Why does buffer level drive the decision rather than throughput?**
Because rebuffering is much worse than low quality. A player that upswitches eagerly on a throughput
spike and then stalls scores worse on every user metric than one that sits conservatively at 720p.
The asymmetry means you step down immediately when the buffer drains and step up cautiously, one
level at a time, only when it is healthy.

**Q: How do you transcode a two-hour film quickly?**
Split it into chunks and transcode them in parallel, then package. Serially it is hours; in parallel
it is minutes. The catch is that chunks must be split on keyframe boundaries or the segments cannot
be decoded independently, so the encoder inserts keyframes at fixed intervals to align chunking,
segmenting and quality switching. The workload is also interruptible, which makes it a good fit for
cheap preemptible capacity, provided each chunk is idempotent.

**Q: How does live differ?**
Latency becomes the whole problem and there is no retry. Transcoding must keep up in real time, so
you cannot lean on elasticity — falling behind means dropped frames or growing delay, and the moment
cannot be re-processed. The manifest becomes a rolling window rather than a static file, and typical
latency is five to thirty seconds, or two to five with low-latency HLS. That is why live pipelines
are provisioned rather than autoscaled.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Three minutes:** *"A user presses play. What happens in the next two seconds?"*

**⭐ The trace: fetch the manifest · request the first segment at a conservative rendition so playback
starts fast · fill the buffer · measure throughput · step up a level if the buffer is healthy · every
one of those requests served from a CDN edge as an immutable cacheable GET, with your origin
untouched.**

**⭐ Then the sentence that frames the whole design: "the server never decides the quality — the
player does, which is why there is no session state and why a CDN can serve essentially all of the
bytes."** That single property is what makes streaming at a hundred and eighty gigabits possible at
all.
