# Day 330 ⭐

**L-330** · ⭐ CAP done correctly — and PACELC, which matters more

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> **⭐ CAP is the most cited and most misunderstood theorem in system design. "Pick two" is wrong,
> and saying it is a reliable way to lose credibility in an interview.**
>
> Today: what CAP actually claims, the three specific misreadings, why PACELC describes your system
> 99.99% of the time — and the concrete question that replaces all of it.

---

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ What CAP actually says

| Letter | ⭐ The real definition | ⭐ What people think |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **C** | ⭐⭐ **Linearizability** — one copy, real-time order (Day 331) | "the data is consistent" |
| ⭐ **A** | ⭐⭐ **EVERY non-failing node returns a non-error response** | "the system is up" |
| ⭐ **P** | ⭐⭐ **the network may drop or delay messages arbitrarily** | "a node crashed" |

> **⭐ The theorem: in the presence of a network partition, you cannot have both linearizability and
> total availability.** That is all. It is a statement about **one specific failure mode**, not a
> taxonomy of databases.

## ⭐ The three misreadings

**⭐ 1. "Pick two of three."**

> **⭐ P is not a choice. Networks partition.** Cables are cut, switches fail, a GC pause looks
> exactly like a partition to everyone else. **You do not get to opt out of P** — so the real choice
> is only **CP or AP, and only during a partition.**

**⭐ 2. "MySQL is CA."**

**⭐ A single-node database is not a distributed system, so CAP does not classify it.** As soon as you
add a replica you must choose again. **"CA" describes a system that has decided not to survive
partitions — which is a legitimate stance, and it is what a single-leader database with manual
failover actually is.**

**⭐ 3. "We chose AP" as a design statement.**

**⭐ The choice only applies during a partition, which is rare.** Saying "we're AP" tells you nothing
about the other 99.99% of the time — **and that is precisely what PACELC fixes.**

## ⭐ The choice, concretely

```
⭐ A partition splits your cluster. A write arrives on the minority side.

⭐ CP — REFUSE IT           ⭐ AP — ACCEPT IT
⭐  "service unavailable"    ⭐  "OK" — and reconcile later
⭐  ⇒ correct, but down      ⭐  ⇒ up, but two versions of the truth exist
```

| ⭐ Choose CP when | ⭐ Choose AP when |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **A wrong answer costs more than no answer** | ⭐ **no answer costs more than a slightly stale one** |
| Balances, inventory, bookings, config | ⭐ feeds, likes, presence, analytics, carts |
| ⭐ Leader election, distributed locks | ⭐ session data, recommendations |

> **⭐ And the real-world detail that scores: it is a per-operation choice, not a per-system one.**
> The same e-commerce system should be AP for browsing the catalogue and CP for taking payment.
> **Answering "we'd be AP" for a whole system is the weak answer; naming which operations differ is
> the strong one.**

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ PACELC — the more useful model

```
⭐ if (Partition)      then (Availability or Consistency)
⭐ Else                then (Latency     or Consistency)
```

> **⭐ The insight: even with a healthy network, consistency costs latency.** Confirming a write on a
> quorum before answering means waiting for other machines — and **that trade-off is paid on every
> single request, whereas the CAP trade-off is paid a few minutes a year.**

| System | ⭐ Classification | ⭐ Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **DynamoDB / Cassandra (default)** | ⭐ **PA/EL** | available during partitions; **fast but eventually consistent normally** |
| ⭐ **Cassandra with QUORUM** | ⭐ PA/EC | ⭐ **same database, different consistency — it is a per-query knob** |
| ⭐ **PostgreSQL, single leader** | ⭐ PC/EC | refuses on the minority side; consistent reads from the leader |
| ⭐ **MongoDB (majority)** | ⭐ PC/EC | primary-only writes, majority reads |
| ⭐ **Spanner** | ⭐⭐ **PC/EC** | ⭐⭐ **consistent AND low latency — by buying atomic clocks** |
| ⭐ Redis (async replica) | ⭐ PA/EL | fast; a failover can lose writes (Day 205) |

> **⭐ The Cassandra row is the one to say out loud: consistency is a per-query setting, not a
> property of the database.** `ONE` versus `QUORUM` on the same cluster gives you two different
> points on the spectrum, chosen by the operation. **That single observation dismantles most
> "database X is CP" claims.**

⚠️ **Spanner deserves its own sentence.** It is genuinely PC/EC at low latency because Google put
GPS and atomic clocks in every datacentre, bounding clock uncertainty to a few milliseconds — and
**then deliberately waits out that uncertainty on every commit.** It is not a loophole in CAP; it is
paying the latency cost in a way most people cannot afford. **Naming `TrueTime` and the commit-wait
shows you know why it is not a counterexample.**

# Part 3 — ⭐ What actually happens during a partition

**⭐ Move from theory to mechanism. This is what interviewers want.**

| System | ⭐ Minority side behaviour |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Single-leader SQL** | ⭐ if it holds the leader: serves normally. If not: **no writes at all** |
| ⭐ **Quorum systems (Raft/Paxos)** | ⭐⭐ **the minority CANNOT elect a leader — it stops serving writes entirely** |
| ⭐ Cassandra with `ONE` | ⭐ **both sides accept writes** → divergence → LWW resolves it, losing data (Day 328) |
| ⭐ Cassandra with `QUORUM` | ⭐ the minority side fails writes — CP behaviour, by configuration |
| ⭐ **Multi-leader** | ⭐ both sides accept everything; **conflicts on heal** |

> **⭐ Why quorum systems need an odd number of nodes:** a 5-node cluster split 3/2 has a majority
> side that keeps working. **Split 2/2 in a 4-node cluster and neither side has a majority — you
> have paid for four machines and gained no availability over three.** That is the whole reason
> etcd, ZooKeeper and Raft clusters are 3, 5 or 7.

## ⭐⭐ The detection problem — the deepest point available

```
⭐ A node stops responding. Is it:
⭐   (a) dead?                    ⭐ (b) network-partitioned?
⭐   (c) in a 30-second GC pause? ⭐ (d) just slow?

⭐⭐ FROM THE OUTSIDE, THESE ARE INDISTINGUISHABLE.
```

> **⭐ This is the fundamental result behind everything today.** In an asynchronous network you cannot
> distinguish a failed node from a slow one — **which is why every distributed system relies on
> timeouts, and why every timeout is a guess trading false failovers against slow detection.**

**⭐ Two consequences worth stating:**

1. ⭐ **A long GC pause makes a healthy node look partitioned** — and when it wakes it believes it is
   still the leader. **Hence leases and fencing tokens** (Day 207, Day 328): the old leader's writes
   are rejected because its token is stale.
2. ⭐ **This is why FLP matters** — consensus is impossible in a fully asynchronous system with even
   one faulty node. **Real systems escape it by using timeouts, giving up guaranteed termination in
   exchange for termination in practice.**

# Part 4 — ⭐ The interview version

**⭐ Never say "we chose AP". Say what the user sees.**

| ⭐ Weak | ⭐ Strong |
|---|---|
| "It's AP" | ⭐ **"During a partition, users on the minority side can still read and post, but they won't see each other's posts until it heals — and we resolve conflicts by X"** |
| "It's CP" | ⭐ **"During a partition, the minority side returns 503 for writes; reads still work from cache with a staleness banner"** |
| "CAP says pick two" | ⭐ **"P isn't optional, so the choice is CP or AP during a partition — and PACELC is the more useful question, because the latency/consistency trade is paid on every request"** |

**⭐ The four questions that replace the theory:**

```
⭐ 1. Which operations must NEVER return a wrong answer?   ⇒ ⭐ those are CP
⭐ 2. Which must ALWAYS respond, even if stale?             ⇒ ⭐ those are AP
⭐ 3. ⭐ What does the user actually SEE on the minority side?
⭐ 4. ⭐ When the partition heals, HOW do we reconcile — and who decides?
```

> **⭐ Question 4 is the one almost nobody answers.** Choosing AP is easy; **saying what happens to
> the two divergent versions afterwards — LWW and its data loss, CRDTs, or a human review queue — is
> the part that is actual engineering.**

⚠️ **And the pragmatic truth to state confidently: partitions within a datacentre are rare, and most
systems that claim AP have never tested it.** Chaos-testing a partition (Day 286) and observing what
your system *actually* does is worth more than any classification — **because a system that has never
been partitioned is CP or AP only in theory.**

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ "Pick two of three" | ⭐⭐ **P is not optional — this signals you learned CAP from a diagram** |
| ⭐ Calling a single-node database CA | CAP does not classify non-distributed systems |
| ⭐ Defining C as "the data is correct" | ⭐ **it means linearizability specifically** |
| ⭐ Defining A as "the system is up" | ⭐ it means *every* non-failing node responds |
| ⭐ Classifying a whole system | ⭐ **it is per-operation, and often per-query** |
| ⭐ "We're AP" with no user-visible description | ⭐ **the answer says nothing** |
| ⭐ **No reconciliation plan** | ⭐⭐ **AP without conflict resolution is just divergence** |
| Ignoring the else branch | ⭐ latency vs consistency is paid on every request |
| Treating Spanner as a CAP counterexample | it pays the cost with clocks and commit-wait |
| ⭐ Even-numbered quorum clusters | ⭐ a 2/2 split has no majority |
| Assuming failures are detectable | ⭐ **slow and dead are indistinguishable** |
| Claiming AP without ever testing a partition | it is a hypothesis, not a property |

## Interview questions

**Q: Explain CAP.**
During a network partition you cannot have both linearizability and total availability. Consistency
there means linearizability specifically, availability means every non-failing node returns a
non-error response, and partition tolerance means the network can drop messages. "Pick two" is wrong,
because partitions happen whether you choose them or not — so the real choice is CP or AP, and only
while partitioned.

**Q: What is PACELC and why is it better?**
If partitioned, choose availability or consistency; else, choose latency or consistency. It is better
because the else branch describes your system almost all the time: consistency costs round trips to
other machines on every single request, whereas the CAP trade is paid for a few minutes a year. It
also explains why the same database can sit in two places — Cassandra at ONE is PA/EL and at QUORUM
is PA/EC, because consistency there is a per-query setting.

**Q: Is PostgreSQL CP or AP?**
A single instance is neither — CAP classifies distributed systems. With a leader and replicas it is
PC/EC: during a partition the side without the leader cannot accept writes, and normally reads from
the leader are consistent at the cost of going to the leader. If you read from replicas you have
chosen latency over consistency and inherited replication lag, which is the else branch making the
decision for you.

**Q: What actually happens during a partition?**
It depends on the quorum. A Raft or Paxos cluster's minority side cannot elect a leader, so it stops
accepting writes entirely — that is CP behaviour and it is why those clusters are three, five or
seven nodes, since an even split leaves no majority. A leaderless system with a low consistency level
accepts writes on both sides, diverges, and resolves later, usually by last-write-wins, which quietly
discards data.

**Q: How do you decide for a real system?**
Per operation. Browsing a catalogue should stay available and slightly stale; taking payment or
decrementing the last unit of stock should refuse rather than be wrong. Then I answer two questions
most people skip: what the user on the minority side actually sees, and how the two versions
reconcile when it heals — because choosing AP with no conflict-resolution plan is just choosing
divergence.

**Q: Why can't you just detect the failure and act?**
Because a dead node, a partitioned node and a node in a thirty-second garbage-collection pause are
indistinguishable from the outside in an asynchronous network. Every detector is a timeout, and every
timeout trades spurious failovers against slow detection. It is also why the paused node waking up
convinced it is still the leader is a real hazard, and why leases and fencing tokens exist.

## Mini task

1. **Write the correct one-sentence statement of CAP** and the three definitions, from memory.
2. ⭐ **Classify five systems you use in PACELC**, with a sentence of justification each.
3. ⭐ Take your flagship and **list every operation, marking each CP or AP.** Any operation you cannot
   classify is one whose requirements you do not know.
4. ⭐⭐ For each AP operation, **write what the user sees during a partition and how you reconcile
   after.** If you cannot write the reconciliation, it is not AP — it is undefined.
5. Run a 3-node quorum cluster (etcd or a Raft library). **Partition it 2/1 and observe both sides.**
   Then partition a 4-node cluster 2/2 and observe that neither works.
6. ⭐ Run Cassandra or a similar store at `ONE` and at `QUORUM`, **partition it, and demonstrate the
   two different behaviours from the same cluster.**
7. ⭐ **Induce a long GC pause** on a leader and observe whether the cluster fails over — and what the
   paused node believes when it wakes.
8. ⭐ Chaos-test a partition against your flagship (Day 286) and **write down what actually happened**
   versus what you predicted.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Three minutes:** *"Would you make your system CP or AP?"*

**⭐ The answer that scores refuses the framing politely and then does better.** *"Both — it is a
per-operation decision. Browsing is AP: I serve stale data with a staleness indicator. Checkout is
CP: on the minority side I return 503 rather than sell inventory twice. And the question I care more
about is the else branch, because the latency-versus-consistency trade is paid on every request,
whereas the partition trade is paid a few minutes a year."*

**⭐ Then close on reconciliation** — what happens to divergent writes when it heals, and who decides.
**Most candidates never get there, and it is the only part that is actually engineering.**
