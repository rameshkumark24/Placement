# Day 486

**L-486** · Company-specific preparation and reading the loop

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> **⭐⭐ Every round in a loop is scored by someone with a different job, and answering a hiring manager's
> question the way you would answer a bar raiser's is how strong candidates lose loops they should have
> won.**
>
> Today: the four loop archetypes and what each optimises for, **who is in the room and what they are
> actually scoring**, research that changes your answers rather than decorating them, and the
> after-action record that makes each loop improve the next.

---

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ Four loop archetypes

| ⭐ Archetype | ⭐ Shape | ⭐⭐ What it optimises for |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Big tech (FAANG-style)** | ⭐ OA → phone → 4–6 onsite: 2 DSA, 1 design, 1–2 behavioural, ⭐ a bar raiser | ⭐⭐ **consistency and false-negative tolerance** — they would rather reject a good candidate than admit a bad one |
| ⭐ **Product companies (mid-size)** | ⭐ screen → 1 DSA → 1 practical/take-home → design → HM | ⭐⭐ **can you do the actual job on Monday** |
| ⭐⭐ **Startups** | ⭐ founder chat → a real task → team fit | ⭐⭐ **ownership, breadth, and shipping without supervision** |
| ⭐ **Services / consultancies** | ⭐ aptitude → technical MCQ → technical → HR | ⭐ breadth, fundamentals, and process |

| ⭐ Consequence for preparation | |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Big tech** | ⭐⭐ **DSA depth and a rehearsed structure** — the process is standardised, so preparation transfers |
| ⭐ Product | ⭐⭐ **your project is the centrepiece** (Days 467–471) — practical over theoretical |
| ⭐⭐ **Startup** | ⭐⭐ shipped things, end to end, operated — **and "I ran it" beats "I built it"** |
| ⭐ Services | ⭐ fundamentals, and being unflustered by rapid-fire breadth questions |

> **⭐ Do not prepare for all four at once.** Know which loop you are in **before** you decide how to spend
> the two weeks before it. **A startup loop rewards project depth and punishes rehearsed DSA patter; a
> big-tech loop is nearly the reverse.**

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ Who is in the room, and what they score

| ⭐ Interviewer | ⭐⭐ Actually scoring | ⭐ How to answer them |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Recruiter** | ⭐ can you communicate, are your expectations realistic, are you real | ⭐⭐ **be easy to work with; defer compensation** (Day 488) |
| ⭐⭐ **Peer engineer** | ⭐⭐ **would I want to review this person's PRs** | ⭐ collaborative, specific, no bluffing |
| ⭐⭐ **Hiring manager** | ⭐⭐ **can you own a service and will you still be here in two years** | ⭐⭐ **ownership stories, and ask about the team's real problems** |
| ⭐⭐ **Bar raiser / cross-team** | ⭐⭐ **is this person above the current bar** — and **they can veto** | ⭐⭐ depth, honesty about limits, judgement over recall |
| ⭐ **Skip-level / director** | ⭐ scope, communication, whether you think about impact | ⭐ talk about outcomes and users, not implementation detail |

⚠️ **The bar raiser is the round people misread.** They are deliberately *not* from the hiring team, they
have no incentive to fill the role, and their job is to say no. **They probe depth and honesty, and they
notice a rehearsed answer immediately** — this is the round where "I don't know, here is how I'd reason
about it" (Day 466) is worth the most.

| ⭐ Tell in the question | ⭐ What they want |
|---|---|
| "Walk me through a project" from a **peer** | ⭐ technical depth and code-level reality |
| The same question from a **hiring manager** | ⭐⭐ **scope, ownership, what you decided and what it cost** |
| The same question from a **director** | ⭐⭐ **why it mattered, and what changed for users** |

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Research that changes your answers

```
⭐⭐ USELESS RESEARCH: reciting the About page. ⭐⭐ Everyone does it and it signals nothing.
⭐⭐ USEFUL RESEARCH: something that changes what you SAY.
```

| ⭐ Source | ⭐ What you get |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Their engineering blog** | ⭐⭐ **the problems they actually have** — reference one specifically |
| ⭐ Their public API docs | ⭐ their design conventions; you can critique them intelligently |
| ⭐⭐ **Their open source / GitHub** | ⭐⭐ code style, testing culture, review norms |
| ⭐ The job description, read closely | ⭐⭐ **the ordering of requirements is a priority list** |
| ⭐ Recent product launches and press | ⭐ what the business is currently betting on |
| ⭐ Employee posts, talks, conference videos | ⭐⭐ the actual stack, and who owns what |
| ⭐ Glassdoor/Blind, read sceptically | ⭐ interview format; **treat opinions as noise, formats as signal** |

```
⭐⭐ THE ONE-SENTENCE TEST FOR "WHY THIS COMPANY?":
⭐   ⭐⭐ Could this sentence be said about any of their competitors?
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ yes ⇒ it is worthless. ⭐⭐ Replace it with something specific and checkable.
⭐   ✅ "Your post about moving from a shared Postgres to per-tenant schemas is the exact problem
⭐      I hit in my own project, and I'd like to work on that at a scale where it actually hurts."
```

| ⭐ Also prepare | |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Two questions per interviewer type** | ⭐ a peer, a manager and a director need different questions |
| ⭐ The tech-stack overlap | ⭐⭐ where you match, **and one honest gap with how you would close it** |
| ⭐ Their scale | ⭐ so your design answers land at the right order of magnitude |

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ Running the loop, and the after-action

| ⭐ Practicality | |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Order your loops deliberately** | ⭐⭐ **interview at your third-choice company first.** The first loop of a cycle is always the worst |
| ⭐ Do not book five in one week | ⭐ you will be worse at all of them; **two a week is sustainable** |
| ⭐⭐ **Keep offers alive concurrently** | ⭐⭐ **leverage requires simultaneity** (Day 488) |
| ⭐ Ask the recruiter the format | ⭐⭐ **they will tell you** — rounds, language policy, whether it is a shared editor |
| ⭐ Confirm the editor and whether you can run code | ⭐ it changes how you practise |
| ⭐ Ask about timelines and other candidates | ⭐ it tells you how much time you will have to decide |

```
⭐⭐ THE AFTER-ACTION, WITHIN ONE HOUR, EVERY ROUND. ⭐ Non-negotiable.
⭐   1. ⭐ every question asked, as close to verbatim as you can
⭐   2. ⭐⭐ where you hesitated, and WHAT you would say next time
⭐   3. ⭐ anything you did not know ⇒ ⭐⭐ goes on the study list TODAY
⭐   4. ⭐ what the interviewer seemed to care about
⭐   5. ⭐ your read: strong / borderline / weak — ⭐⭐ then compare it to the outcome later
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ Loop 3 is dramatically better than loop 1 for people who do this, and identical for
⭐   those who do not. ⭐ Memory decays within a day; the record does not.
```

⚠️ **Calibrating your own read against outcomes is the underrated part.** Most people are systematically
wrong in one direction — **either they think they failed rounds they passed, or the reverse** — and
knowing which way you skew stops one bad-feeling round from wrecking the next one.

| ⭐ After a rejection | |
|---|---|
| ⭐ Ask for feedback | ⭐ you will usually get nothing; occasionally you get gold |
| ⭐⭐ **Read your own after-action instead** | ⭐⭐ it is more honest than most feedback anyway |
| ⭐ Note the cooling-off period | ⭐ typically 6–12 months; **it is not permanent** |
| ⭐ Do not over-fit to one rejection | ⭐⭐ loops have real variance — **a single no is weak evidence** |

## Interview questions

**Q: How does your preparation differ by company type?**
Substantially, which is why I decide the archetype before spending the two weeks. Big tech loops are
standardised and false-negative tolerant, so DSA depth and a rehearsed structure transfer well across
them. Product companies want to know whether I can do the job on Monday, so the project deep dive is the
centrepiece. Startups want ownership and breadth — evidence that I shipped and operated something without
supervision, where "I ran it" counts for more than "I built it". Preparing for all of them
simultaneously means being mediocre at each, because startup and big-tech preparation pull in nearly
opposite directions.

**Q: What is a bar raiser actually doing?**
Looking for a reason to say no, on purpose. They are deliberately not from the hiring team, so they have
no incentive to fill the role, and their job is to protect the standard rather than the headcount. That
changes how I answer: they probe depth and they notice rehearsed material immediately, so it is the round
where naming the boundary of what I know and reasoning out loud from there is worth the most. Treating it
as a formality is how people lose loops they were otherwise passing.

**Q: What does useful company research look like?**
Something that changes what I say, rather than something I recite. Their engineering blog is the best
source, because it tells me the problems they actually have — and being able to say "your post about
moving to per-tenant schemas is the exact problem I hit in my own project, at a scale where it actually
hurts" is specific and checkable. The test I apply to any "why this company" answer is whether the same
sentence could be said about their nearest competitor. If it could, it is worthless.

**Q: How do you sequence your interviews?**
Third-choice company first, deliberately, because the first loop of any cycle is the worst one regardless
of preparation — the rust is real and it comes off in a live round rather than in practice. Then no more
than two a week, because five in a week means being worse at all five. And I try to keep offers alive
concurrently rather than sequentially, since negotiation leverage comes from simultaneity, not from
having a good offer in hand after the others expired.

**Q: What do you do after each round?**
An after-action record within the hour: every question as close to verbatim as I can manage, where I
hesitated and what I would say instead, anything I did not know going straight onto the study list, what
the interviewer seemed to care about, and my own read of how it went. That last one matters because most
people are systematically miscalibrated in one direction, and comparing my read against actual outcomes
tells me which way I skew — which stops one bad-feeling round from damaging the next. People who keep the
record are noticeably better by their third loop; people who do not are the same as they were at the
first.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes:** *"Why do you want to work here?"* — for one real company, out loud.

**⭐ Apply the test before you record: could this sentence be said about their closest competitor? If yes,
throw it away. The answer has to contain something specific and checkable — a post they published, a
design decision in their public API, a problem their scale creates that yours did not.**

**⭐ The shape that works is three beats: the specific thing, the connection to something you have
actually done, and the honest want. "You wrote about moving from a shared Postgres to per-tenant schemas.
I hit exactly that boundary in my own system at twelve tenants, and I made a call I'm not sure survives
at ten thousand. I'd like to work on it where it actually hurts."**

**⭐ Notice what that also does: it plants a hook, admits a limit, and gives them somewhere to take the
conversation. It is Day 467's technique applied to a question people usually waste.**
