# Day 355 🚗

**P-355** · 🚗 **Design 11 — ride hailing** — ⭐⭐ 250,000 location writes a second

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** BUILD

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: ride hailing is two systems glued together — a very high-volume, lossy,
> ephemeral location pipeline, and a small, absolutely durable trip-and-payment system — and almost
> every mistake in this design comes from putting one of them in the other's database.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐ 0–8: scope and the number that splits the system

```
⭐⭐ FUNCTIONAL:
⭐   ① Driver goes online, streams location.
⭐   ② Rider requests a ride; system matches.
⭐   ③ ⭐ Live tracking during the trip.
⭐   ④ ⭐ Trip lifecycle and fare.
⭐   ⭐⭐ OUT: payments (⭐ Day 357), pricing/surge algorithms,
⭐     routing engine, pooling, driver onboarding.

⭐⭐ NON-FUNCTIONAL:
⭐   ⭐ 1 M active drivers at peak, ⭐⭐ LOCATION EVERY 4 SECONDS.
⭐   ⭐ 200k ride requests/hour peak ⇒ ~55/s. ⭐⭐ TINY.
⭐   ⭐ Match latency < 5 s; ⭐ ETA accuracy matters commercially.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ TRIP AND FARE RECORDS: ZERO LOSS. LOCATION PINGS:
⭐     LOSS IS FINE.

⭐⭐ ⇒ THE ESTIMATE THAT DEFINES THE DESIGN:
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ 1,000,000 DRIVERS ÷ 4 s = ***250,000 LOCATION
⭐     WRITES/SECOND.***
⭐   ⭐ Versus ⭐⭐ 55 RIDE REQUESTS/SECOND.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ A RATIO OF ***4,500 : 1***. ⭐ These are not one
⭐     workload, and treating them as one is the mistake the
⭐     whole design exists to avoid.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ 250k writes/s in Postgres is ⭐⭐ 250,000 DEAD TUPLES A
⭐     SECOND — autovacuum will never keep up, the table bloats,
⭐     and index scans degrade within hours (Day 320).
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ SO LOCATION LIVES IN MEMORY. THAT IS NOT AN
⭐     OPTIMISATION; IT IS THE ONLY OPTION.
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ The location pipeline

```
⭐⭐ THE RULE: ***CURRENT LOCATION IS EPHEMERAL STATE. TRIP
⭐   HISTORY IS DURABLE DATA. THEY GO IN DIFFERENT PLACES AND
⭐   THEY ARE WRITTEN BY DIFFERENT PATHS.***

⭐⭐ CURRENT LOCATION ⇒ ***REDIS***, keyed by driver:
⭐   ⭐ `GEOADD drivers <lon> <lat> driver:123` + a TTL-bearing
⭐     hash for status.
⭐   ⭐ Overwrite in place ⇒ ⭐⭐ CONSTANT MEMORY REGARDLESS OF
⭐     WRITE RATE. ⭐ 1 M drivers × ~100 B = 100 MB. ⭐ Trivial.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AND A TTL (~30 s) MEANS A DRIVER WHOSE APP DIES
⭐     ***DISAPPEARS AUTOMATICALLY.*** ⭐ No cleanup job, no
⭐     stale-driver bug — the expiry ***is*** the health check.

⭐⭐ THE PING PATH — ⭐ four decisions:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ DO NOT PUT 250k WRITES/S THROUGH A REQUEST/RESPONSE
⭐      HTTP HANDLER. ⭐ A persistent connection (WebSocket/gRPC
⭐      stream) per driver amortises the handshake — ⭐⭐ AND
⭐      Day 352'S GATEWAY DESIGN APPLIES DIRECTLY, INCLUDING
⭐      THE 100–200 INSTANCE FLEET.
⭐   ② ⭐ Batch: a driver app can send 3 points every 12 s
⭐      instead of 1 every 4 s ⇒ ⭐⭐ A 3× REDUCTION IN REQUEST
⭐      COUNT FOR ***FREE***, at the cost of 8 s of staleness
⭐      that matching already tolerates.
⭐   ③ ⭐ Adaptive rate: ⭐⭐ A DRIVER PARKED AND IDLE DOES NOT
⭐      NEED 4-SECOND UPDATES. ⭐ On-trip: 4 s. Idle and
⭐      stationary: 30 s. ⭐⭐ THIS ROUTINELY HALVES THE WHOLE
⭐      PIPELINE, AND IT IS A CLIENT-SIDE CHANGE.
⭐   ④ ⭐ Fire-and-forget into a bounded queue (Day 336) ⇒
⭐      ⭐⭐ A DROPPED PING IS INVISIBLE; A BLOCKED PING PATH IS
⭐      AN OUTAGE.

⭐⭐ ⇒ AND SEPARATELY: ***THE TRIP'S*** LOCATION TRAIL IS
⭐   BATCHED AND WRITTEN TO DURABLE STORAGE (or a log) ⭐ for
⭐   fare disputes, safety and analytics — ⭐⭐ ONE WRITE PER
⭐   TRIP, NOT ONE PER PING. ⭐ Same data, two lifetimes.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Geospatial indexing

```
⭐⭐ THE QUERY: "drivers within 3 km of this point, available,
⭐   right now."

⭐⭐ ① ***NAIVE SQL*** — `WHERE lat BETWEEN … AND lon BETWEEN …`
⭐   ⭐ ✘ Two-dimensional range on a one-dimensional B-tree ⇒
⭐     ⭐⭐ THE INDEX HELPS WITH ONE DIMENSION AND SCANS THE
⭐     OTHER.

⭐⭐ ② ⭐⭐ ***GEOHASH*** — interleave latitude and longitude
⭐   bits into one string. ⭐⭐ NEARBY POINTS SHARE A PREFIX, SO
⭐   A 2D PROXIMITY QUERY BECOMES A ***1D PREFIX RANGE***, which
⭐   any B-tree or sorted set can serve.
⭐   ⭐ Precision 6 ≈ 1.2 km × 0.6 km; precision 7 ≈ 150 m.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AND THE TRAP (Day 320): ***TWO POINTS 10 METRES
⭐     APART CAN HAVE COMPLETELY DIFFERENT PREFIXES IF A CELL
⭐     BOUNDARY RUNS BETWEEN THEM*** — ⭐ so you must always
⭐     query the cell ***plus its eight neighbours***.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ FORGETTING THE NEIGHBOURS PRODUCES A BUG THAT
⭐     APPEARS ONLY NEAR BOUNDARIES, WHICH IS TO SAY IT LOOKS
⭐     RANDOM AND IRREPRODUCIBLE.

⭐⭐ ③ ***S2 / H3*** — spherical cells (H3 is hexagonal).
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ HEXAGONS HAVE ***UNIFORM*** NEIGHBOUR DISTANCE, so
⭐     "ring of neighbours" is well defined, ⭐ and cell areas
⭐     distort far less near the poles than geohash rectangles.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Uber built and open-sourced H3 for exactly this, and
⭐     naming it here is apt.

⭐⭐ ④ ⭐⭐ REDIS `GEOSEARCH` — ⭐ geohash-based, built in, one
⭐   command, handles the neighbour problem for you.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ FOR THIS DESIGN THIS IS THE RIGHT ANSWER, AND SAYING
⭐     "I'D USE THE BUILT-IN AND HERE IS WHAT IT IS DOING
⭐     UNDERNEATH" IS BETTER THAN IMPLEMENTING GEOHASHING BY
⭐     HAND.

⭐⭐ AND THE DENSITY PROBLEM — ⭐ the follow-up that separates:
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ CITY CENTRE AT 6 p.m.: ONE CELL CONTAINS 3,000
⭐     DRIVERS. RURAL AREA: THE NEAREST DRIVER IS FOUR CELLS
⭐     AWAY.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ FIX: ***ADAPTIVE PRECISION*** — start narrow and widen
⭐     until you have enough candidates, ⭐ with a cap on both
⭐     count and radius. ⭐⭐ A FIXED RADIUS IS WRONG IN BOTH
⭐     DIRECTIONS SIMULTANEOUSLY.
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ Matching: an offer, not an assignment

```
⭐⭐ DAY 320'S CORE INSIGHT, AT SCALE:
⭐   ***A DRIVER IS NOT A PARKING SPOT, BECAUSE A SPOT NEVER
⭐      SAYS NO*** ⇒ ⭐⭐ AN ASSIGNMENT IS AN ***OFFER WITH AN
⭐      EXPIRY***, NOT A FACT.

⭐⭐ THE FLOW:
⭐   ① Rider requests ⇒ create a `ride` row, `status=matching`.
⭐   ② Candidate search: geo query ⇒ ~20 nearby available
⭐      drivers.
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ RANK BY ***ETA***, NOT BY STRAIGHT-LINE DISTANCE.
⭐      ⭐ 200 m across a river is twelve minutes. ⭐ Call the
⭐      routing service for the shortlist only —
⭐      ⭐⭐ CHEAP FILTER FIRST, EXPENSIVE RANK SECOND, which is
⭐      the same retrieve-then-rerank shape as Days 293 and 351.
⭐   ④ ⭐ Offer to the top candidate (or a small batch) with a
⭐      15-second expiry.
⭐   ⑤ ⭐⭐ ACCEPTANCE IS A CONDITIONAL `UPDATE`, AND THE ROWCOUNT
⭐      DECIDES — ⭐ when you offer to several, two will accept
⭐      within a couple of hundred milliseconds.
⭐   ⑥ ⭐ Decline or expiry ⇒ next candidate.

⭐⭐ THE TWO INVARIANTS, ENFORCED IN THE DATABASE:
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ A DRIVER HAS AT MOST ONE ACTIVE OFFER OR TRIP.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ A RIDE HAS AT MOST ONE ACCEPTED DRIVER.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Partial unique indexes, not application checks
⭐     (Day 187).

⭐⭐ AND THE STRATEGY TRADE, STATED AS A PRODUCT DECISION:
⭐   ⭐ Sequential ⇒ ⭐⭐ 20 DRIVERS × 15 s = FIVE MINUTES OF
⭐     SPINNER IN THE WORST CASE.
⭐   ⭐ Broadcast to all 20 ⇒ fast, ⭐⭐ AND 19 DRIVERS LEARN
⭐     THAT OFFERS ARE USUALLY ALREADY GONE, SO THEY STOP
⭐     RESPONDING.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ BATCHED ESCALATION: 3, THEN 5, THEN 10. ⭐ The
⭐     constraint is ***rider wait versus driver annoyance***,
⭐     which is a product decision, not a technical one — ⭐ and
⭐     saying that is what makes it a good answer.
```

# Part 5 — ⭐⭐ The deep dive: why matching is not a global optimum

```
⭐⭐ THE TEMPTING IDEA: batch requests for 10 seconds and solve
⭐   a global assignment (the Hungarian algorithm) for the
⭐   optimal rider-driver pairing.
⭐   ⭐ ✔ Genuinely better total wait times — ⭐ measurably so.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ ⭐⭐ ADDS UP TO 10 SECONDS OF LATENCY TO ***EVERY***
⭐     REQUEST, INCLUDING THE ONE THAT HAD AN IDLE DRIVER 100
⭐     METRES AWAY.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ ⭐⭐ AND IT IS A GLOBAL LOCK ON THE MATCHING DOMAIN:
⭐     ONE OPTIMISER PER CITY, WHICH IS A SCALING AND
⭐     AVAILABILITY BOTTLENECK.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE ANSWER: ***BATCH ONLY WHERE DENSITY MAKES IT
⭐     WORTH IT.*** ⭐ Dense city centre at peak ⇒ a short
⭐     batching window pays. ⭐ Sparse area ⇒ match greedily and
⭐     immediately. ⭐⭐ THE WINDOW IS A FUNCTION OF LOCAL
⭐     DENSITY, AND SAYING THAT IS A GENUINELY STRONG ANSWER.

⭐⭐ AND THE PARTITIONING THAT MAKES MATCHING SCALE:
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ PARTITION THE MATCHING SERVICE BY ***GEOGRAPHIC
⭐     REGION***, so each matcher owns a city and holds its
⭐     drivers in memory.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ NO CROSS-PARTITION COORDINATION, BECAUSE A RIDE IN
⭐     MUMBAI NEVER COMPETES WITH A RIDE IN DELHI. ⭐ It is Day
⭐     329's "keep the unit of work together", and geography
⭐     hands you the shard key for free.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Boundaries need care: ⭐⭐ A RIDER AT A CITY EDGE MUST
⭐     BE ABLE TO SEE DRIVERS ACROSS IT, which is the same
⭐     neighbour-cell problem one level up.
```

# Part 6 — ⭐ The trip, and the durable half

```
⭐⭐ THE TRIP STATE MACHINE — ⭐ and it must be durable and
⭐   explicit (Day 342):
⭐   `requested → matching → accepted → arrived → in_progress →
⭐    completed → paid`
⭐   ⭐ Plus `cancelled_by_rider`, `cancelled_by_driver`,
⭐     `no_drivers_found`, ⭐⭐ AND `payment_unknown` (Day 316).
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ EVERY TRANSITION IS A DATABASE WRITE WITH THE ACTOR,
⭐     TIME AND LOCATION. ⭐ This table is evidence in a dispute,
⭐     so it is append-heavy and audited.

⭐⭐ THE FARE — ⭐⭐ AND THE RULE FROM DAY 270: ***SNAPSHOT THE
⭐   PRICING, DO NOT REFERENCE IT.*** ⭐ Store base rate, per-km,
⭐   per-minute, surge multiplier and currency ***on the trip
⭐   row***. ⭐⭐ A PRICING CHANGE NEXT TUESDAY MUST NOT ALTER
⭐   LAST WEEK'S FARE, AND A JOIN TO A `pricing` TABLE MEANS IT
⭐   DOES.

⭐⭐ LIVE TRACKING for the rider:
⭐   ⭐ The rider subscribes to the driver's location — ⭐⭐ ONE
⭐     SUBSCRIBER PER ACTIVE TRIP, NOT A BROADCAST. ⭐ It is a
⭐     tiny fan-out and the cheap part of the system.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ SMOOTH ON THE ***CLIENT***: interpolate between
⭐     pings and snap to the road geometry. ⭐ Sending more
⭐     frequent pings to make the marker smoother is paying
⭐     server cost for a client-side rendering problem.

⭐⭐ AND THE OFFLINE-DRIVER CASE: ⭐⭐ MID-TRIP, THE DRIVER
⭐   ENTERS A TUNNEL FOR SIX MINUTES. ⭐ The trip must not be
⭐   cancelled by a heartbeat timeout — ⭐⭐ ON-TRIP DRIVERS GET
⭐   A FAR LONGER GRACE PERIOD THAN IDLE ONES, AND THE APP
⭐   BUFFERS AND REPLAYS ITS POINTS ON RECONNECT.
```

# Part 7 — ⭐ Bottlenecks and the summary

```
⭐⭐ WHAT BREAKS:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ THE INGESTION FLEET, NOT THE DATABASE. ⭐ 250k
⭐      persistent connections is 100–200 gateway instances
⭐      (Day 352). ⭐ Adaptive ping rates are the cheapest lever
⭐      by a distance.
⭐   ② ⭐ Redis geo index writes ⇒ ⭐⭐ SHARD BY REGION, WHICH
⭐      YOU WANTED ANYWAY FOR MATCHING. ⭐ One decision, two
⭐      problems.
⭐   ③ ⭐ The routing service ⇒ ⭐⭐ ONLY CALLED FOR ~20
⭐      SHORTLISTED CANDIDATES PER REQUEST, WHICH IS 1,100
⭐      CALLS/S — cacheable by (origin cell, destination cell)
⭐      for a few minutes.
⭐   ④ ⭐ Hot region at surge time ⇒ ⭐ the matcher for one city
⭐      is one process; ⭐⭐ SUB-PARTITION A LARGE CITY BY
⭐      DISTRICT AND ACCEPT SLIGHTLY WORSE MATCHES AT
⭐      BOUNDARIES.

⭐⭐ THE SUMMARY:
⭐   ***"Two systems, and the mistake is merging them: a
⭐      quarter of a million location writes a second that are
⭐      ephemeral and lossy, and fifty-five ride requests a
⭐      second that must never be lost — a ratio of about four
⭐      and a half thousand to one. So location is an overwrite
⭐      in Redis with a TTL, which means constant memory and a
⭐      dead driver disappearing on his own; trips are rows in
⭐      Postgres with a durable state machine and snapshotted
⭐      pricing. Matching is a geohash or H3 candidate search
⭐      including neighbour cells, ranked by ETA rather than
⭐      straight-line distance, offered with a fifteen-second
⭐      expiry and accepted by a conditional update. And the
⭐      matcher is partitioned by geography, which means no
⭐      cross-partition coordination at all, because a ride in
⭐      Mumbai never competes with a ride in Delhi."***
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ Writing location pings to Postgres | 250k dead tuples/s. Autovacuum never keeps up and the table bloats within hours |
| ⭐⭐ Appending location history rather than overwriting | Memory grows without bound. Current location is one value per driver |
| ⭐ No TTL on driver location | A crashed app leaves a phantom driver forever. The expiry *is* the health check |
| ⭐⭐ Forgetting neighbour cells in a geo query | A bug that only appears near cell boundaries, so it looks random |
| ⭐ A fixed search radius | Wrong in both directions at once: 3,000 candidates downtown, zero rurally |
| ⭐⭐ Ranking by straight-line distance | Two hundred metres across a river is twelve minutes |
| ⭐ Calling the routing service for every candidate | Cheap filter first, expensive rank on the shortlist only |
| ⭐⭐ Assignment instead of an offer | Drivers decline, sleep and go offline. The rider waits for someone never coming |
| ⭐ Broadcasting every offer to all candidates | Drivers learn offers are already gone and stop responding |
| ⭐⭐ Global batch optimisation everywhere | Ten seconds of latency for the rider with an idle driver 100 m away |
| ⭐ Referencing a pricing table from the trip | A price change next week alters last week's fare |
| ⭐⭐ Cancelling a trip on a heartbeat timeout | The driver is in a tunnel. On-trip grace must be far longer |
| ⭐ Increasing ping rate to smooth the map | A client rendering problem paid for with server capacity |

## Interview questions

**1. "Where do you store driver locations?"**
> ⭐⭐ Redis, overwritten in place, with a TTL. ⭐ A quarter of a million writes a second into Postgres
> is **a quarter of a million dead tuples a second — autovacuum never keeps up and index scans degrade
> within hours.** ⭐ Overwriting means constant memory regardless of write rate: a million drivers is
> about a hundred megabytes. ⭐⭐ And the TTL means **a driver whose app dies simply disappears — the
> expiry is the health check**, so there's no cleanup job and no stale-driver bug.

**2. "How do you find nearby drivers?"**
> ⭐ Geohash or H3 — interleaving lat and lon so that **a two-dimensional proximity query becomes a
> one-dimensional prefix range**, which any sorted structure can serve. ⭐⭐ Always query the cell **plus
> its eight neighbours**, because two points ten metres apart can sit either side of a boundary — and
> forgetting that gives you a bug that only appears near edges, so it looks random. ⭐ And adaptive
> precision, because a fixed radius returns three thousand candidates downtown and zero rurally. ⭐ In
> practice Redis `GEOSEARCH`, and I'd say what it's doing underneath.

**3. "What's the modelling insight?"**
> ⭐⭐ **A driver is not a parking spot, because a spot never says no.** ⭐ So an assignment is an offer
> with an expiry, and accept, decline and timeout are all first-class. ⭐ Acceptance is a conditional
> update whose rowcount decides, because when you offer to several, two will accept within a couple of
> hundred milliseconds — and the loser gets a designed UI state, not an error. ⭐ The invariants — one
> active offer per driver, one accepted driver per ride — go in partial unique indexes, not application
> checks.

**4. "Would you use a global matching optimiser?"**
> ⭐ Only where density makes it worth it. ⭐⭐ Batching ten seconds and solving an optimal assignment
> genuinely improves total wait times — **but it adds ten seconds to every request, including the one
> with an idle driver a hundred metres away**, and it's a global lock on the matching domain. ⭐⭐ So
> the batching window should be **a function of local density**: a short window downtown at peak,
> greedy immediate matching in a sparse area.

**5. "How does matching scale?"**
> ⭐⭐ Partition by geography — each matcher owns a city and holds its drivers in memory, ⭐⭐ **so there
> is no cross-partition coordination at all, because a ride in Mumbai never competes with a ride in
> Delhi.** ⭐ Geography hands you the shard key for free. ⭐ The care is at boundaries: a rider at a city
> edge must see drivers across it, which is the neighbour-cell problem one level up. ⭐ And a very large
> city sub-partitions by district, accepting slightly worse matches at internal edges.

**6. "The driver goes into a tunnel mid-trip."**
> ⭐ Nothing should happen. ⭐⭐ **On-trip drivers get a much longer grace period than idle ones** — a
> heartbeat timeout must not cancel a trip that's actually in progress — and the app buffers its points
> and replays them on reconnect, so the trail is complete for the fare and any dispute. ⭐ The rider's
> map freezes and the client interpolates; ⭐ if it's long, the app should say "reconnecting" rather
> than showing a stationary car, because a stale marker that looks live is worse than an honest one.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes.** Split it in the first sentence, because everything follows: **a quarter of a million
location writes a second that are ephemeral and lossy, and fifty-five ride requests a second that must
never be lost — a ratio of about four and a half thousand to one.** Then the consequence: **a quarter
of a million writes a second in Postgres is a quarter of a million dead tuples a second**, so location
is an overwrite in Redis, which gives constant memory regardless of write rate, **and a TTL means a
driver whose app dies simply disappears — the expiry is the health check.** Then the geo query: geohash
or H3 turns two-dimensional proximity into a one-dimensional prefix range, **always including the eight
neighbour cells, because two points ten metres apart can straddle a boundary and the resulting bug looks
random.** Then the modelling insight: **a driver is not a parking spot, because a spot never says no**,
so an assignment is an offer with an expiry, accepted by a conditional update whose rowcount decides.
Give the strategy trade as the product decision it is — **sequential means five minutes of spinner,
broadcast means drivers learn to ignore offers** — and name the ranking rule: **cheap geo filter first,
expensive ETA rank on the shortlist**, because two hundred metres across a river is twelve minutes.
Close on the scaling answer: **partition matching by geography, so there is no cross-partition
coordination at all** — and on the durable half, where the trip is a state machine with snapshotted
pricing, because a price change next Tuesday must not alter last week's fare.
