# Part 1 — What consensus is for, and what it is not for

```
⭐⭐ ***USE CONSENSUS WHEN EXACTLY ONE ANSWER MUST EXIST.***
⭐   ⭐ Who is the leader?
⭐   ⭐ What is the current cluster membership?
⭐   ⭐ Who holds this lock, and with which fencing token
⭐     (Day 411)?
⭐   ⭐ What is the current configuration?
⭐   ⭐⭐ THESE ARE SMALL, LOW-WRITE-VOLUME, HIGH-STAKES FACTS.

⭐⭐ ***DO NOT USE CONSENSUS FOR YOUR APPLICATION DATA.***
⭐   ⭐ Every write costs a majority round trip, ⭐ so throughput is
⭐     bounded by your slowest majority member and your write
⭐     latency includes a network hop to another AZ.
⭐   ⭐⭐ AND DAY 329's LESSON STILL GOVERNS: ***YOU WANT THE
⭐     WEAKEST CONSISTENCY THAT WORKS.*** ⭐ Most of your data
⭐     does not need a linearizable total order and paying for
⭐     one is a design error, not a safety measure.

⭐⭐ ⇒ SO THE SHAPE IS ALMOST ALWAYS: ***A SMALL CONSENSUS SYSTEM
⭐   HOLDING THE METADATA, AND A LARGE NON-CONSENSUS SYSTEM
⭐   HOLDING THE DATA.*** ⭐ etcd decides which Postgres is the
⭐   primary; ⭐ Postgres holds the rows.
```

# Part 2 — Terms: a logical clock for leadership

```
⭐ ⭐⭐ ***TIME IS DIVIDED INTO `TERMS`, NUMBERED, INCREASING.***
⭐   ⭐ Each term has ***at most one*** leader. ⭐ Some terms have
⭐     none (a split vote).
⭐   ⭐ Every message carries a term.
⭐   ⭐⭐ AND TWO RULES DO AN ENORMOUS AMOUNT OF WORK:
⭐     ⭐⭐ ① IF YOU SEE A ***HIGHER*** TERM, YOU IMMEDIATELY
⭐        BECOME A FOLLOWER AND ADOPT IT. ⭐ Even if you thought
⭐        you were the leader.
⭐     ⭐⭐ ② IF YOU SEE A ***LOWER*** TERM, YOU REJECT THE MESSAGE
⭐        AND TELL THE SENDER YOUR TERM.
⭐   ⭐⭐ ⇒ SO A PARTITIONED OLD LEADER THAT REJOINS IS DEMOTED BY
⭐     THE FIRST MESSAGE IT RECEIVES. ⭐ It cannot argue.
⭐     ⭐⭐ THAT IS DAY 409's LAMPORT CLOCK DOING EXACTLY WHAT
⭐     LAMPORT CLOCKS ARE FOR — ⭐ and it is why Raft never
⭐     compares wall clocks to decide who is in charge.
```

# Part 3 — Leader election, and the timing assumption

```
⭐ ① ⭐ A follower hears nothing for `election_timeout` →
⭐    becomes a ***candidate***, increments its term, votes for
⭐    itself, and requests votes.
⭐ ② ⭐ Each node votes ***at most once per term***, first come
⭐    first served ⭐ (subject to Part 5's restriction).
⭐ ③ ⭐ A majority of votes → leader. ⭐ It immediately sends
⭐    heartbeats to suppress other elections.
⭐ ④ ⭐ Split vote (nobody gets a majority) → ⭐ the term ends
⭐    with no leader and a new election begins.

⭐⭐ ***AND THE MECHANISM THAT MAKES ④ TERMINATE IS RANDOMISED
⭐   ELECTION TIMEOUTS*** — ⭐ typically 150–300 ms, drawn
⭐   independently on each node.
⭐   ⭐ Without randomisation, all followers time out together,
⭐     all become candidates together, ⭐⭐ ALL SPLIT THE VOTE, AND
⭐     REPEAT FOREVER.
⭐   ⭐ With it, one node reliably times out first and wins before
⭐     the others start.

⭐⭐ ⇒ AND THIS IS EXACTLY DAY 398's POINT ARRIVING: ***THAT
⭐   RANDOMISED TIMEOUT IS THE TIMING ASSUMPTION RAFT ADDS TO GET
⭐   AROUND FLP.*** ⭐ FLP says no deterministic algorithm
⭐   guarantees termination; ⭐⭐ RAFT IS NOT DETERMINISTIC HERE,
⭐   SO IT TERMINATES ***WITH PROBABILITY 1*** RATHER THAN WITH
⭐   CERTAINTY.
⭐   ⭐⭐ AND WHEN THE ASSUMPTION IS VIOLATED — A NETWORK SLOWER
⭐   THAN THE ELECTION TIMEOUT, OR A HOST SO THROTTLED IT CANNOT
⭐   HEARTBEAT (DAY 361) — ⭐ you see FLP in production as
⭐   ⭐⭐ ***A CLUSTER THAT KEEPS RE-ELECTING AND NEVER SERVES
⭐   ANYTHING***, ⭐ which is a real and recognisable etcd
⭐   failure.

⭐ ⭐ Sizing rule: `election_timeout` ≫ `heartbeat_interval` ≫
⭐   network RTT. ⭐⭐ SET THE TIMEOUT TOO LOW AND A GC PAUSE OR A
⭐   CFS THROTTLE TRIGGERS A NEEDLESS ELECTION; ⭐ set it too high
⭐   and failover is slow. ⭐ etcd defaults: 100 ms heartbeat,
⭐   1,000 ms election.
```

# Part 4 — Log replication

```
⭐ THE LEADER APPENDS TO ITS LOG AND SENDS `AppendEntries` TO
⭐   EVERY FOLLOWER. ⭐ Each message carries:
⭐   ⭐ `prevLogIndex`, `prevLogTerm` — ⭐⭐ THE ENTRY THE NEW ONES
⭐     MUST FOLLOW
⭐   ⭐ the new entries, ⭐ and the leader's `commitIndex`.

⭐ ⭐⭐ ***A FOLLOWER REJECTS IF ITS LOG DOES NOT CONTAIN AN ENTRY
⭐   AT `prevLogIndex` WITH `prevLogTerm`.*** ⭐ The leader then
⭐   decrements and retries until they agree, ⭐ then overwrites
⭐   the follower's divergent tail.

⭐⭐ ⇒ AND THAT SIMPLE CHECK GIVES YOU THE ***LOG MATCHING
⭐   PROPERTY***: ⭐⭐ ***IF TWO LOGS CONTAIN AN ENTRY WITH THE SAME
⭐   INDEX AND THE SAME TERM, THEN THE LOGS ARE IDENTICAL IN
⭐   EVERY ENTRY UP TO THAT POINT.***
⭐   ⭐ Proof sketch: ⭐ a leader creates at most one entry per
⭐     index per term, ⭐ and the consistency check propagates the
⭐     property inductively. ⭐⭐ SO ONE MATCHING PAIR CERTIFIES
⭐     THE ENTIRE PREFIX — ⭐ which is why the retry loop only
⭐     needs to find one agreement point.

⭐ ⭐⭐ ***COMMIT: AN ENTRY IS COMMITTED ONCE IT IS REPLICATED ON A
⭐   MAJORITY.*** ⭐ The leader advances `commitIndex` and tells
⭐   followers, ⭐ who then apply to their state machines in order.
⭐   ⭐ ⇒ ***COMMITTED MEANS DURABLE ACROSS ANY MINORITY
⭐     FAILURE.***
```

# Part 5 — ⭐⭐ Safety: the election restriction is the whole proof

```
⭐ ⭐⭐ THE QUESTION THAT MATTERS: ***HOW DO WE KNOW A NEW LEADER
⭐   HAS EVERY COMMITTED ENTRY?*** ⭐ If it did not, it could
⭐   overwrite committed data, ⭐⭐ AND THE SYSTEM WOULD HAVE
⭐   "AGREED" ON SOMETHING AND THEN UNAGREED.

⭐ ⭐⭐ ***THE ELECTION RESTRICTION: A VOTER REFUSES TO VOTE FOR A
⭐   CANDIDATE WHOSE LOG IS LESS UP-TO-DATE THAN ITS OWN.***
⭐   ⭐ "More up-to-date" = higher last term, ⭐ or same term and
⭐     longer log.

⭐⭐ AND NOW THE THREE-LINE PROOF, WHICH IS THE MOST ELEGANT
⭐   THING IN THE STAGE:
⭐   ⭐⭐ ① A COMMITTED ENTRY IS, BY DEFINITION, ON A ***MAJORITY***
⭐      OF NODES.
⭐   ⭐⭐ ② A WINNING CANDIDATE RECEIVED VOTES FROM A
⭐      ***MAJORITY*** OF NODES.
⭐   ⭐⭐ ③ ***ANY TWO MAJORITIES INTERSECT IN AT LEAST ONE
⭐      NODE.*** ⭐ That node has the committed entry, ⭐ and it
⭐      would have refused to vote for a candidate missing it.
⭐   ⭐⭐ ⇒ THEREFORE THE WINNER HAS EVERY COMMITTED ENTRY. ∎

⭐⭐ ⇒ AND NOTICE WHAT DID THE WORK: ***QUORUM INTERSECTION***
⭐   (DAY 343). ⭐ Not a clock, not a lock, not a coordinator —
⭐   ⭐⭐ THE ARITHMETIC FACT THAT TWO SETS EACH LARGER THAN HALF
⭐   MUST OVERLAP. ⭐ That single fact is load-bearing for Raft,
⭐   for Paxos, for Dynamo's R+W>N, and for every quorum system
⭐   you will ever meet.

⭐ ⭐⭐ ***ONE MORE SUBTLETY — THE FIGURE-8 PROBLEM:*** ⭐ a new
⭐   leader must ***not*** commit an entry from a ***previous***
⭐   term merely by counting replicas, ⭐ because a later leader
⭐   could still overwrite it. ⭐⭐ RAFT'S FIX: A LEADER COMMITS
⭐   ENTRIES FROM PRIOR TERMS ***ONLY*** BY COMMITTING A NEW
⭐   ENTRY OF ITS OWN TERM (IN PRACTICE, AN IMMEDIATE NO-OP ON
⭐   ELECTION). ⭐ Log Matching then carries the earlier entries
⭐   along. ⭐⭐ THIS IS THE PART EVERY NAIVE IMPLEMENTATION GETS
⭐   WRONG, AND IT IS WHY YOU DO NOT WRITE YOUR OWN (PART 7).
```

# Part 6 — The practical parts

```
⭐ ⭐⭐ ***READS ARE NOT FREE.*** ⭐ A leader that has been
⭐   partitioned may not know it has been deposed, ⭐⭐ SO SERVING
⭐   A READ FROM ITS LOCAL STATE CAN RETURN STALE DATA WHILE
⭐   CLAIMING TO BE LINEARIZABLE. ⭐ Three options:
⭐   ⭐ ① ***ReadIndex*** — confirm leadership with a heartbeat
⭐     round trip before answering. ⭐⭐ CORRECT, COSTS ONE RTT.
⭐   ⭐ ② ***Lease reads*** — assume leadership for `t <
⭐     election_timeout`. ⭐⭐ FAST, AND IT DEPENDS ON CLOCKS —
⭐     WHICH DAY 409 JUST TOLD YOU NOT TO TRUST. ⭐ Bounded, and a
⭐     real trade.
⭐   ⭐ ③ ***Follower reads*** with a read index — scalable, and
⭐     stale by construction unless you wait.
⭐   ⭐⭐ ⇒ "STALE READ FROM A PARTITIONED OLD LEADER" IS THE
⭐     CLASSIC RAFT BUG, AND IT IS WHY etcd's `--consistency=s`
⭐     EXISTS AND WHY YOU SHOULD NOT USE IT FOR A LOCK.

⭐ ⭐ ***MEMBERSHIP CHANGES*** — ⭐⭐ CHANGING THE CLUSTER SET IS
⭐   DANGEROUS BECAUSE TWO DIFFERENT MAJORITIES CAN EXIST DURING
⭐   THE TRANSITION. ⭐ Raft uses joint consensus, or
⭐   single-server-at-a-time changes. ⭐⭐ ADD ONE NODE, WAIT,
⭐   THEN THE NEXT. NEVER TWO AT ONCE.
⭐ ⭐ ***SNAPSHOTS*** — the log cannot grow forever; ⭐ snapshot
⭐   the state machine and discard the prefix. ⭐ A far-behind
⭐   follower gets the snapshot instead of the log.
```

# 💬 Interview

**1. ⭐⭐ "Explain Raft."**
> ⭐ Three parts: leader election, log replication and safety. ⭐ Time is divided into numbered terms,
> each with at most one leader; ⭐⭐ **every message carries a term, and seeing a higher term makes you
> immediately step down, so a partitioned old leader that rejoins is demoted by the first message it
> receives and can't argue.** ⭐ Election: a follower that hears nothing becomes a candidate and needs a
> majority; ⭐⭐ **randomised election timeouts are what break split votes, and that randomisation is
> precisely the timing assumption Raft adds to get around FLP — it terminates with probability one
> rather than with certainty.** ⭐ Replication: `AppendEntries` carries the previous index and term, a
> follower rejects on mismatch, ⭐⭐ **and that check gives you the Log Matching Property — if two logs
> share an entry with the same index and term, they're identical up to that point — so one matching
> pair certifies the whole prefix.** ⭐ Committed means replicated on a majority.

**2. ⭐⭐ "How does Raft guarantee a new leader has all committed entries?"**
> ⭐ The election restriction, and the proof is three lines. ⭐⭐ **A voter refuses to vote for a
> candidate whose log is less up-to-date than its own. A committed entry is by definition on a
> majority. A winning candidate got votes from a majority. Any two majorities intersect in at least one
> node — and that node has the committed entry and would have refused the vote. So the winner has
> every committed entry.** ⭐ And what did the work isn't a clock or a lock — ⭐⭐ **it's quorum
> intersection, the arithmetic fact that two sets each larger than half must overlap. That single fact
> is load-bearing for Raft, for Paxos, and for Dynamo's R plus W greater than N.**

**3. "Would you implement Raft?"**
> ⭐⭐ **No — never, not even as a learning exercise I then ship.** ⭐ The paper is readable and that's
> exactly what makes it dangerous: ⭐⭐ **the figure-8 case, membership changes, snapshot installation
> and read linearizability are all places where a plausible implementation is silently wrong and only
> fails during a partition — which is the one time you can't debug it.** ⭐ The figure-8 one specifically:
> ⭐⭐ **a new leader must not commit an entry from a previous term just by counting replicas; it commits
> a no-op of its own term first and lets Log Matching carry the old entries along.** ⭐ I'd use etcd or a
> database that embeds it. ⭐ What learning it buys is the failure modes, not the code.

**4. ⭐⭐ "How many nodes should a Raft cluster have?"**
> ⭐ An odd number. ⭐⭐ **Tolerance is floor of (N−1)/2, so three survives one failure, five survives
> two — and four survives only one, exactly the same as three, with more cost and slower writes because
> a bigger majority must acknowledge. An even cluster is strictly worse than the odd one below it.**
> ⭐ And I'd note the availability consequence: ⭐⭐ **a minority partition can't elect and can't commit —
> it's unavailable by design, which is why losing two of three etcd nodes stops your Kubernetes control
> plane dead.**

**5. ⭐⭐ "Is a read from a Raft leader always current?"**
> ⭐ No, and this is the classic bug. ⭐⭐ **A leader that's been partitioned may not know it's been
> deposed, so serving a read from its local state can return stale data while claiming to be
> linearizable.** ⭐ Three options: ReadIndex, which confirms leadership with a heartbeat round trip and
> costs one RTT; lease reads, which are fast ⭐⭐ **and depend on clocks — which is a real trade, given
> that clocks are the thing you can't trust**; or follower reads with a read index, which are scalable
> and stale unless you wait. ⭐ It's why etcd has a serializable read mode ⭐⭐ **and why you must not use
> it for a lock.**

**6. "What actually takes down an etcd cluster in practice?"**
> ⭐⭐ **A slow disk.** ⭐ Raft fsyncs before acknowledging, so one node with variable write latency
> stalls the whole cluster — ⭐⭐ **etcd on a network disk is the commonest Kubernetes control-plane
> incident there is, and the fix is local SSD.** ⭐ After that: an election timeout set too low, so a GC
> pause or a CFS throttle triggers needless elections; ⭐ a membership change adding two nodes at once,
> which can create two different majorities during the transition; ⭐ and a cross-region cluster, ⭐⭐ **where
> every write pays a cross-region round trip because commit requires the slowest majority member.**

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes.** Frame it and scope it: **consensus is for facts where exactly one answer must exist —
who's the leader, what's the membership, who holds this lock — and not for application data, because
every write costs a majority round trip and most data doesn't need a linearizable total order.** Then
the three parts. **Terms are a logical clock for leadership: every message carries one, and seeing a
higher term makes you step down immediately, so a partitioned old leader is demoted by the first
message it receives.** Election: majority vote, **with randomised timeouts as the thing that breaks
split votes — and that randomisation is exactly the timing assumption Raft adds to escape FLP, which
is why a network slower than the election timeout shows up as a cluster that keeps re-electing and
never serves anything.** Replication: **`AppendEntries` carries the previous index and term, the
follower rejects on mismatch, and that gives Log Matching — one agreeing entry certifies the entire
prefix.** Then the part that's actually subtle, and give the proof: **how do we know a new leader has
every committed entry? Because a voter refuses to vote for a less up-to-date log; a committed entry is
on a majority; a winner got a majority; and two majorities must intersect — so the intersecting node
had the entry and would have refused. It's quorum intersection doing all the work, which is the same
fact behind Paxos and behind R plus W greater than N.** Close on what learning it is *for*: **never
implement it — the figure-8 case and membership changes fail silently and only during a partition. Use
etcd. What you take away is the failure modes: tolerance is floor of N minus one over two, so four
nodes survives exactly as much as three and costs more; a minority partition is unavailable by design;
and the thing that actually pages you is a slow disk, because Raft fsyncs before acknowledging.**
