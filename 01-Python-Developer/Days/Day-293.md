# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ Why vector search alone is not enough

```
⭐⭐ FOUR QUERIES WHERE PURE VECTOR SEARCH FAILS, AND THEY ARE
⭐   NOT EDGE CASES — THEY ARE MOST OF A REAL WORKLOAD:

⭐ ① ⭐⭐ EXACT IDENTIFIERS — "clause 7.3.2", "form 16A".
⭐     ⭐ The embedding of an identifier is nearly meaningless;
⭐       all clause numbers look alike to a vector.
⭐ ② ⭐ RARE TERMS — a domain word appearing twice in the whole
⭐     corpus is exactly what the user typed, and exactly what
⭐     an embedding averages away.
⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ NEGATION — "leave that is NOT carried forward"
⭐     retrieves the passage about leave that IS carried
⭐     forward, ⭐ because those two sentences are nearly
⭐     identical in embedding space.
⭐ ④ ⭐ NUMBERS AND DATES — "the 2024 limit" happily retrieves
⭐     the 2019 one.

⭐⭐ SO: HYBRID. LEXICAL (BM25) ***AND*** VECTOR, FUSED.
⭐   ⭐ BM25 is exact-term matching with sensible weighting: it
⭐     nails ① ② ④ and is decades old, cheap and explicable.
⭐   ⭐ Vectors handle paraphrase and synonymy, which BM25
⭐     cannot: "can I take time off" vs "leave entitlement".
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THEY FAIL ON DIFFERENT QUERIES, WHICH IS THE ENTIRE
⭐     ARGUMENT. ⭐ Hybrid is not hedging; it is covering two
⭐     disjoint failure modes.
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ Fusing two rankings

```
⭐⭐ THE OBVIOUS APPROACH IS WRONG: normalise the two scores
⭐   and add them.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ BM25 SCORES ARE UNBOUNDED AND CORPUS-DEPENDENT;
⭐     COSINE SIMILARITIES SIT IN A NARROW BAND. ⭐ Normalising
⭐     them makes a number, not a meaning, and the weighting
⭐     you tune is valid for the query set you tuned it on and
⭐     no other.

⭐⭐ RECIPROCAL RANK FUSION — USE ***RANKS***, NOT SCORES:
⭐   ⭐ score(d) = Σ over systems of  1 / (k + rank(d))   (k≈60)
⭐   ⭐ rank 1 → 1/61 · rank 2 → 1/62 · rank 20 → 1/80
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ WHY IT WORKS: RANKS ARE COMPARABLE ACROSS SYSTEMS
⭐     AND SCORES ARE NOT. ⭐ A document ranked 2nd by both
⭐     beats one ranked 1st by one and 40th by the other —
⭐     ⭐ which is exactly the judgement you want.
⭐   ⭐ It has one parameter, it needs no tuning, and it is
⭐     about fifteen lines.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Reranking, where the biggest jump usually is

```
⭐⭐ RETRIEVAL AND RANKING ARE DIFFERENT JOBS:
⭐   ⭐ THE RETRIEVER (bi-encoder) embeds the query and the
⭐     document SEPARATELY, so it can precompute millions of
⭐     vectors — ⭐ fast, and necessarily approximate, because
⭐     the two texts never meet.
⭐   ⭐⭐ THE RERANKER (cross-encoder) reads THE QUERY AND THE
⭐     DOCUMENT ***TOGETHER*** and scores the pair. ⭐ Far more
⭐     accurate, and impossible to precompute — so it can only
⭐     run over a shortlist.

⭐⭐ THE PATTERN: RETRIEVE 50 CHEAPLY, RERANK TO 5 ACCURATELY.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ In practice this is usually the largest single quality
⭐     improvement available, ⭐⭐ AND IT HAS A SECOND EFFECT
⭐     THAT PAYS FOR IT: WITH 5 GENUINELY RELEVANT PASSAGES
⭐     INSTEAD OF 20 MIXED ONES, A ***CHEAPER GENERATOR***
⭐     PRODUCES A BETTER ANSWER. ⭐ The reranker often reduces
⭐     total cost rather than adding to it (Day 294).
⭐   ⭐ Cost: 50 pairs of latency per query. Budget it, cache
⭐     it, and measure whether it earns its place — ⭐ on the
⭐     eval set, like everything else today.
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ The evaluation set, which is the real deliverable

```
⭐⭐ BUILD IT BY HAND, BEFORE TUNING ANYTHING (Day 288).
⭐   ⭐ 100+ pairs: (question, the section ids that answer it)
⭐   ⭐ Drawn from real questions where possible; ⭐⭐ INCLUDE
⭐     THE HARD ONES DELIBERATELY — negation, multi-hop, exact
⭐     identifiers, questions the corpus CANNOT answer, and
⭐     questions whose answer changed between policy versions.
⭐   ⭐ Label SECTIONS, not chunks (Day 289) — ⭐⭐ SO THE EVAL
⭐     SET SURVIVES EVERY RE-CHUNKING, which is the whole point
⭐     of having one.

⭐⭐ THE METRICS, AND WHICH ONE MATTERS:
⭐   ⭐⭐ recall@k — is the right section in the top k?
⭐        ⇒ ⭐⭐ ***THIS IS THE CEILING ON THE WHOLE SYSTEM.***
⭐          If it is 0.71, then 29% of questions are unanswerable
⭐          NO MATTER WHAT THE MODEL DOES — and it will answer
⭐          them anyway, fluently.
⭐   ⭐ MRR — how high up? Matters because context windows are
⭐        ordered and models attend unevenly.
⭐   ⭐ nDCG — graded relevance, when some sections are partial.
⭐   ⭐⭐ AND THE ONE PEOPLE MISS: ***PRECISION AT THE k YOU
⭐        ACTUALLY SEND.*** ⭐ Twenty chunks of which three are
⭐        relevant is worse than five of which three are: the
⭐        irrelevant ones are distractors, and they cost tokens.
```

```
⭐⭐ THE ITERATION DISCIPLINE — THE WHOLE DAY IN ONE RULE:
⭐   ***CHANGE ONE THING. RE-RUN THE EVAL. RECORD THE NUMBER.***
⭐   | change | recall@10 | note |
⭐   | baseline: vector only, 500-token chunks | 0.62 | |
⭐   | structural chunking (Day 292) | 0.71 | |
⭐   | ⭐⭐ + ancestry prefix | 0.83 | ⭐ biggest single jump |
⭐   | + BM25 hybrid with RRF | 0.89 | |
⭐   | + cross-encoder rerank | 0.91 | ⭐ MRR 0.62 → 0.81 |
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THAT TABLE IS THE MOST PERSUASIVE ARTEFACT IN THIS
⭐     ENTIRE PROJECT, and it exists only if you resisted
⭐     changing three things at once.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Without it, a RAG system regresses silently forever,
⭐     ⭐⭐ BECAUSE EVERY ANSWER STILL READS FLUENTLY.
```

# Part 5 — ⭐⭐ End-to-end evaluation

```
⭐⭐ RETRIEVAL BEING RIGHT DOES NOT MAKE THE ANSWER RIGHT.
⭐   THREE THINGS TO MEASURE ON THE GENERATED OUTPUT:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ FAITHFULNESS — is every claim supported by a
⭐     retrieved passage? ⭐ The most important, because an
⭐     unfaithful answer is confidently wrong.
⭐   ② ⭐ ANSWER RELEVANCE — does it address the question asked?
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ CITATION CORRECTNESS — does each citation actually
⭐     contain the claim? ⭐ A citation that does not support
⭐     its sentence is worse than none: it manufactures trust.

⭐⭐ LLM-AS-JUDGE, WITH THE CAVEATS THAT MAKE IT USABLE:
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ MEASURE THE JUDGE. Hand-label 50 cases and compute
⭐     the judge's agreement with you. ⭐ An unvalidated judge
⭐     is a number generator, and it will happily produce a
⭐     stable, precise, meaningless score.
⭐   ⭐ Judges are biased toward LONG, CONFIDENT, well-formatted
⭐     answers — ⭐⭐ WHICH IS EXACTLY THE FAILURE MODE YOU ARE
⭐     TRYING TO DETECT.
⭐   ⭐ Use a different model, or at minimum a different prompt
⭐     and framing, from the generator.
⭐   ⭐ Ask for a JUDGEMENT PER CLAIM, not a score out of ten —
⭐     ⭐ per-claim verdicts are far more reproducible than a
⭐       holistic rating.
```

# Part 6 — ⭐ Query processing, each earning its place

```
⭐   ⭐ REWRITING — turn a conversational question into a
⭐     retrieval query. ⭐ Cheap and usually positive.
⭐   ⭐ EXPANSION — add synonyms and related terms. ⭐ Helps
⭐     recall, hurts precision; measure both.
⭐   ⭐ HyDE — generate a hypothetical answer and embed THAT.
⭐     ⭐ Sometimes a large win; ⭐⭐ adds a model call to
⭐       EVERY query, so it is a latency and cost decision as
⭐       much as a quality one.
⭐   ⭐ DECOMPOSITION — split a multi-hop question into parts,
⭐     retrieve for each. ⭐ Necessary for "does X apply to Y
⭐     given Z", ⭐⭐ AND IT MULTIPLIES YOUR COST AND LATENCY
⭐       BY THE NUMBER OF SUB-QUESTIONS.

⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ EVERY ONE OF THESE ADDS A FAILURE MODE AND A COST,
⭐   SO EVERY ONE MUST PROVE ITSELF ON THE EVAL SET. ⭐ A
⭐   technique that helps on three demo questions and hurts
⭐   recall by 4 points overall is a regression you shipped
⭐   because it felt clever.
```

# Part 7 — ⭐⭐ The "I don't know" path

```
⭐⭐ THE MOST IMPORTANT FEATURE IN THE SYSTEM, AND THE ONE
⭐   NOBODY DEMOS:
⭐   ⭐ If the best retrieval score is below a threshold, or
⭐     the reranker's top score is weak, or the retrieved
⭐     passages disagree ⇒ ⭐⭐ REFUSE, AND SAY WHY.
⭐   ⭐ "I could not find policy covering contractors in this
⭐     jurisdiction after 2025. The closest sections are …"
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ IN A POLICY DOMAIN, A CONFIDENT WRONG ANSWER IS
⭐     WORSE THAN NO ANSWER — because a user who gets nothing
⭐     asks a human, and a user who gets a fluent wrong answer
⭐     ***ACTS ON IT***.

⭐ ⭐ AND THE THRESHOLD IS CALIBRATED, NOT GUESSED: include
⭐   unanswerable questions in the eval set and ⭐⭐ MEASURE
⭐   THE REFUSAL RATE ON BOTH POPULATIONS. ⭐ You want high
⭐   refusal on the unanswerable ones and near-zero on the
⭐   answerable ones — ⭐⭐ AND THAT IS A CURVE YOU TUNE, WITH
⭐   A CHOSEN OPERATING POINT YOU CAN DEFEND.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐ Track refusal rate in production too: a sudden rise
⭐   means ingestion broke (Day 292), not that users got harder.
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Vector search alone | It fails on identifiers, rare terms, negation and numbers — most of a real workload. |
| Treating hybrid as hedging | The two methods fail on disjoint queries; that's the argument. |
| Normalising and adding scores | BM25 is unbounded and corpus-dependent; cosine isn't. Fuse ranks. |
| Skipping reranking | Usually the largest single quality gain available. |
| Assuming reranking costs more overall | Five good passages let a cheaper generator do better work. |
| Building the eval set after tuning | It gets written to pass. |
| Labelling chunks instead of sections | The eval set dies at the next re-chunking. |
| An eval set of easy questions | You measured nothing. Include negation, multi-hop and unanswerable. |
| Ignoring recall@k | It's the hard ceiling; the model will answer the missing 29% anyway. |
| Ignoring precision at your send-k | Irrelevant passages are distractors and cost tokens. |
| Changing three things at once | You learn nothing and can't attribute the change. |
| No results table | The system regresses silently, because answers stay fluent. |
| An unvalidated LLM judge | A stable, precise, meaningless number. Measure its agreement with you. |
| Judge and generator being the same model and prompt | It rewards its own style. |
| Holistic 1–10 scores | Per-claim verdicts are far more reproducible. |
| Adding HyDE or decomposition unmeasured | Each adds latency, cost and a failure mode. |
| No refusal path | A user who gets nothing asks a human; one who gets a fluent wrong answer acts on it. |
| A guessed refusal threshold | Calibrate on answerable and unanswerable populations, and pick an operating point. |
| Not tracking refusal rate in production | A rise means ingestion broke, not that questions got harder. |

## Interview questions

**Q: Why hybrid retrieval rather than vectors?**

> Because they fail on different queries, which makes it coverage rather than hedging. Pure vector
> search is weak on exact identifiers like "clause 7.3.2", on rare domain terms, on numbers and dates,
> and especially on negation — "leave that is not carried forward" is nearly identical in embedding
> space to the passage saying it is. BM25 handles all of those and is decades old, cheap and
> explicable; vectors handle paraphrase, which BM25 can't. And I fuse with reciprocal rank fusion
> rather than normalising scores, because BM25 scores are unbounded and corpus-dependent while cosine
> similarities sit in a narrow band — ranks are comparable across systems and scores aren't.

**Q: What is reranking and is it worth it?**

> The retriever embeds query and document separately, so it can precompute millions of vectors but
> the two texts never meet — fast and necessarily approximate. A cross-encoder reads the pair together
> and scores it, which is far more accurate and impossible to precompute, so it runs over a shortlist:
> retrieve fifty cheaply, rerank to five accurately. It's usually the largest single quality jump
> available, and it often pays for itself, because with five genuinely relevant passages instead of
> twenty mixed ones a cheaper generator produces a better answer.

**Q: How do you know your retrieval is good?**

> Recall@k on a hand-built evaluation set of a hundred-plus question-to-section pairs, built before I
> tuned anything, labelled at section level so it survives re-chunking, and deliberately including
> negation, multi-hop, exact identifiers and questions the corpus can't answer. Recall@k is the number
> that matters because it's a hard ceiling — at 0.71, twenty-nine percent of questions are
> unanswerable regardless of the model, and the model will answer them anyway, fluently. Then I change
> one thing at a time and record the number, which is how I know the ancestry prefix was worth twelve
> points and reranking was worth two.

**Q: How do you evaluate the generated answer?**

> Faithfulness, answer relevance and citation correctness — with citation correctness mattering more
> than people expect, because a citation that doesn't support its sentence is worse than no citation:
> it manufactures trust. I use an LLM judge, but I validate it first by hand-labelling fifty cases and
> measuring agreement, because an unvalidated judge is a number generator that produces stable,
> precise, meaningless scores. It's a different model from the generator, and I ask for a verdict per
> claim rather than a score out of ten, because per-claim judgements are far more reproducible — and
> judges are biased toward long, confident, well-formatted answers, which is exactly the failure mode
> I'm trying to catch.

**Q: What happens when it doesn't know?**

> It refuses, and says what it looked for. That's a feature, not a limitation: in a policy domain a
> user who gets nothing asks a human, and a user who gets a fluent wrong answer acts on it. The
> threshold is calibrated rather than guessed — my eval set includes unanswerable questions, so I can
> measure refusal rate on both populations and pick a defensible operating point on that curve. And I
> track refusal rate in production, because a sudden rise means ingestion broke, not that users
> started asking harder questions.

## Today's build

1. ⭐⭐ Build the eval set: 100+ questions labelled to sections, including 15 unanswerable and 15 negation or multi-hop.
2. ⭐ Measure baseline recall@5, recall@10, MRR and precision@k. Record them.
3. ⭐⭐ Add BM25 and fuse with RRF. Re-measure and record.
4. ⭐ Try score normalisation instead, and record how the weighting behaves on a second query set.
5. ⭐⭐ Add a cross-encoder reranker over the top 50. Measure recall, MRR, latency and cost.
6. ⭐⭐ With reranking on, try a cheaper generator and compare answer quality and total cost.
7. ⭐ Build the results table with one row per change. Never change two things in one row.
8. ⭐⭐ Implement faithfulness, relevance and citation-correctness scoring.
9. ⭐⭐ Hand-label 50 cases and compute your judge's agreement. Report the number.
10. ⭐ Deliberately feed the judge a long, confident, wrong answer and see what it scores.
11. ⭐ Add query rewriting and measure. Keep or drop based on the number.
12. ⭐⭐ Add HyDE and decomposition behind flags, measure both, and record the latency cost of each.
13. ⭐⭐ Implement the refusal path with a calibrated threshold, and plot refusal rate for answerable versus unanswerable.
14. ⭐ Choose and document your operating point.
15. ⭐⭐ Add refusal rate to your production dashboard and alert on a step change.
16. ⭐ Re-run the whole eval in CI nightly and fail on a regression beyond a threshold.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Three minutes: "How does retrieval work in your system?"**

Open with why it isn't just vectors, using failures rather than theory: **identifiers, rare terms,
numbers, and above all negation** — "leave that is not carried forward" sits almost on top of the
passage saying it is. So hybrid, and stress that it's coverage rather than hedging, **because the two
methods fail on disjoint queries.** Fuse with reciprocal rank fusion, and give the reason: **BM25
scores are unbounded and corpus-dependent, cosine sits in a narrow band, so ranks are comparable and
scores aren't.** Then reranking, explained by the architecture: **a bi-encoder never lets the query and
document meet, a cross-encoder reads them together** — so retrieve fifty cheaply, rerank to five
accurately, and note that it often *reduces* cost, because five good passages let a cheaper generator
do better work. Then the discipline, which is the real answer: **a hand-built eval set of a
hundred-plus question-to-section pairs, written before any tuning, labelled at section level so it
survives re-chunking**, with negation and unanswerable questions in it deliberately. **Recall@k is a
hard ceiling** — at 0.71, twenty-nine percent of questions are unanswerable no matter what the model
does, and it will answer them anyway. One change at a time, number recorded, which is how you know the
ancestry prefix was worth twelve points. Close on refusal as a feature: **a user who gets nothing asks
a human; a user who gets a fluent wrong answer acts on it** — with a threshold calibrated on
answerable and unanswerable populations rather than guessed.
