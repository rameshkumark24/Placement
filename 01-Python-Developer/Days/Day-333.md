**P-333** · 📬 **Message queues and event-driven architecture** — ⭐⭐ and the distributed monolith

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: a queue converts a synchronous failure into a delayed one, which is
> usually an upgrade — but only if you have decided who is responsible when the delay becomes
> permanent, and "we publish an event and hope" is not an answer to that question.**

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ What a queue actually buys, and what it charges

```
⭐⭐ THE FOUR REAL BENEFITS:
⭐   ① ⭐ ***TEMPORAL DECOUPLING*** — the consumer can be down
⭐        for an hour and nothing is lost.
⭐   ② ⭐⭐ ***LOAD LEVELLING*** — a 10× spike becomes a longer
⭐        queue rather than a 503. ⭐ This is the one that turns
⭐        Day 322's utilisation curve from a cliff into a slope.
⭐   ③ ⭐ ***LATENCY HIDING*** — the user's request returns in
⭐        40 ms; the 3-second email happens later.
⭐   ④ ⭐ ***FAN-OUT*** — one event, N independent consumers,
⭐        added without touching the producer.

⭐⭐ AND THE FOUR CHARGES, WHICH ARE REAL:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ ***YOU LOSE THE RETURN VALUE.*** The caller no
⭐        longer knows whether it worked. ⭐ Every "fire and
⭐        forget" needs a status the user can see, or a
⭐        guarantee they will be told.
⭐   ② ⭐⭐ ***EVERY CONSUMER MUST BE IDEMPOTENT*** (Day 335).
⭐        Not "should be". ⭐ Redelivery is normal operation.
⭐   ③ ⭐ ***ORDERING IS NO LONGER FREE.***
⭐   ④ ⭐⭐ ***DEBUGGING BECOMES ARCHAEOLOGY.*** A stack trace
⭐        stops at the publish. ⭐ Day 285's trace id through
⭐        the outbox row exists exactly for this.

⭐⭐ AND THE TEST FOR WHETHER YOU SHOULD QUEUE SOMETHING AT ALL:
⭐   ***IF THE USER MUST KNOW THE RESULT BEFORE THEY LEAVE THE
⭐      PAGE, IT IS NOT A QUEUE JOB. IT IS A SLOW REQUEST YOU
⭐      NEED TO MAKE FASTER.***
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ Commands vs events — the distinction that decides your architecture

```
⭐⭐ ***COMMAND***: "do this." ⭐ One recipient. ⭐ The sender
⭐   knows what should happen. ⭐ Named imperatively:
⭐   `SendWelcomeEmail`, `ChargeCard`.
⭐⭐ ***EVENT***: "this happened." ⭐ Zero or more recipients.
⭐   ⭐⭐ THE SENDER MUST NOT KNOW OR CARE WHO LISTENS. ⭐ Named
⭐   in the past tense: `OrderPlaced`, `PaymentCaptured`.

⭐⭐ THE TEST THAT CATCHES THE FAKE EVENT:
⭐   ***IF DELETING EVERY SUBSCRIBER WOULD BE A BUG, IT IS A
⭐      COMMAND WEARING AN EVENT'S NAME.***
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ `UserRegistered` with exactly one listener that sends
⭐     the welcome email, and the product breaks if it stops —
⭐     ⭐⭐ THAT IS A COMMAND, AND CALLING IT AN EVENT MEANS
⭐     NOBODY OWNS THE FAILURE.

⭐⭐ WHY IT MATTERS OPERATIONALLY:
⭐   ⭐ A command that fails ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE SENDER'S PROBLEM. Retry,
⭐     DLQ, alert, and someone is on call for it.
⭐   ⭐ An event with no listeners ⇒ ⭐ perfectly fine.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ CALLING A COMMAND AN "EVENT" IS HOW RESPONSIBILITY
⭐     GETS LOST. Six months later nobody can say who is
⭐     accountable for welcome emails not being sent, ⭐ and the
⭐     answer turns out to be nobody.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Choreography vs orchestration

```
⭐⭐ ***CHOREOGRAPHY*** — each service reacts to events and
⭐   emits its own. No conductor.
⭐   ⭐ Order → `OrderPlaced` → Payment reacts → `PaymentTaken`
⭐     → Shipping reacts → `Shipped` → Notification reacts.
⭐   ⭐ ✔ Loose coupling. ✔ Add a consumer without touching
⭐     anyone. ✔ No single bottleneck.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ ⭐⭐ NOBODY KNOWS THE WHOLE FLOW. ⭐ The business
⭐     process exists only as an emergent property of six
⭐     codebases — ⭐⭐ AND YOU CANNOT ANSWER "WHERE IS ORDER
⭐     123 RIGHT NOW?" WITHOUT SEARCHING SIX SETS OF LOGS.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ Cycles are easy to create and hard to see.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ ⭐⭐ COMPENSATION IS NEARLY IMPOSSIBLE TO REASON ABOUT
⭐     — if step 4 fails, who undoes 1–3? (Day 342.)

⭐⭐ ***ORCHESTRATION*** — one component owns the process and
⭐   calls each step.
⭐   ⭐ ✔ ⭐⭐ THE PROCESS IS IN ONE FILE, READABLE, TESTABLE,
⭐     AND HAS AN EXPLICIT STATE YOU CAN QUERY.
⭐   ⭐ ✔ Compensation is straightforward — the orchestrator
⭐     knows what it did.
⭐   ⭐ ✔ Timeouts and retries live in one place.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ The orchestrator becomes a dependency and can become
⭐     a god service (Day 299's fourth rot).
⭐   ⭐ ✘ It must be durable — ⭐ its state ***is*** the process.

⭐⭐ THE ANSWER THAT SCORES, AND IT IS NOT "IT DEPENDS":
⭐   ***ORCHESTRATE THE BUSINESS TRANSACTION. CHOREOGRAPH THE
⭐      SIDE EFFECTS.***
⭐   ⭐ Order → payment → inventory → shipment is a transaction
⭐     with money in it. ⭐⭐ ORCHESTRATE IT. Make it a state
⭐     machine with a row in a table.
⭐   ⭐ "Send a receipt, update analytics, warm a cache, notify
⭐     the CRM" are side effects. ⭐⭐ CHOREOGRAPH THEM — they
⭐     can fail independently without corrupting anything.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE DIVIDING LINE: ***IF A FAILURE HALFWAY THROUGH
⭐     LEAVES THE BUSINESS IN AN INVALID STATE, IT NEEDS AN
⭐     OWNER.***
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ The distributed monolith, and how you build one by accident

```
⭐⭐ THE SYMPTOM: you have microservices, ⭐ but a release
⭐   requires deploying four of them in a specific order, ⭐ and
⭐   one service being down breaks the others.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ YOU HAVE ALL THE COSTS OF DISTRIBUTION AND NONE OF
⭐     THE BENEFITS. ⭐ It is strictly worse than the monolith
⭐     you replaced.

⭐⭐ THE FIVE WAYS QUEUES CREATE ONE — ALL LOOK REASONABLE:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ REQUEST/REPLY OVER A QUEUE. ⭐ Service A publishes
⭐        and ***blocks waiting*** for a reply on another queue.
⭐        ⇒ ⭐⭐ THAT IS AN RPC WITH EXTRA STEPS, WORSE LATENCY,
⭐          AND NO CIRCUIT BREAKER. ⭐ If you need an answer
⭐          now, make an HTTP call — it is more honest.
⭐   ② ⭐ SHARED EVENT SCHEMAS WITH NO VERSIONING ⇒ every
⭐        consumer must deploy with the producer.
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ EVENTS THAT CARRY IDS ONLY, FORCING A CALL BACK TO
⭐        THE PRODUCER. ⭐ Now the consumer cannot process
⭐        anything while the producer is down — ⭐⭐ THE EXACT
⭐        COUPLING THE QUEUE WAS SUPPOSED TO REMOVE.
⭐        ⇒ ⭐ (Note the tension with Day 262's "IDs not
⭐          objects", which was about ***staleness***. ⭐⭐ THE
⭐          RESOLUTION: SEND THE ID ***PLUS*** AN IMMUTABLE
⭐          SNAPSHOT OF WHAT THE CONSUMER NEEDS — the fields
⭐          that were true at the time, which is what the
⭐          consumer usually wants anyway.)
⭐   ④ ⭐ ONE SHARED DATABASE BEHIND SEVERAL SERVICES (Day 340).
⭐   ⑤ ⭐ ORDERING DEPENDENCIES BETWEEN TOPICS: "process
⭐        payments only after inventory" — ⭐⭐ AN INVISIBLE
⭐        SYNCHRONISATION CONSTRAINT NOBODY DOCUMENTED.

⭐⭐ THE DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION, AND IT IS BRUTAL:
⭐   ***CAN EACH SERVICE BE DEPLOYED, AND ITS DEPENDENCIES
⭐      TAKEN DOWN FOR AN HOUR, WITHOUT ANY OTHER TEAM BEING
⭐      INVOLVED?*** ⭐ If not, it is one system that happens to
⭐   run in several processes.
```

# Part 5 — ⭐ Topologies and what to pick

```
⭐⭐ ① ***POINT-TO-POINT QUEUE*** — one producer, one logical
⭐      consumer group, work is distributed.
⭐      ⇒ ⭐ Jobs. ⭐ SQS, RabbitMQ, ⭐ Postgres `SKIP LOCKED`.
⭐⭐ ② ***PUB/SUB*** — one publisher, N independent subscribers,
⭐      each gets a copy.
⭐      ⇒ ⭐ Events. ⭐ SNS, Redis pub/sub (⭐⭐ FIRE-AND-FORGET,
⭐        NO DURABILITY — a disconnected subscriber simply
⭐        misses messages, which surprises people), Kafka
⭐        consumer groups.
⭐⭐ ③ ***LOG*** — an ordered, replayable, retained sequence.
⭐      ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE KEY PROPERTY IS ***REPLAY***: a new consumer
⭐        can read history from the beginning. ⭐ Kafka (Day 334).
⭐⭐ ④ ***ROUTED / TOPIC EXCHANGE*** — content-based routing
⭐      (`order.*.eu`). ⭐ RabbitMQ's strength.

⭐⭐ AND THE CHOICE IN ONE LINE EACH:
⭐   ⭐ Volume < a few thousand/s and you already run Postgres
⭐     ⇒ ⭐⭐ USE POSTGRES. Day 261. One less system.
⭐   ⭐ Managed, simple, at-least-once jobs ⇒ SQS.
⭐   ⭐ Complex routing, per-message TTL, priorities ⇒ RabbitMQ.
⭐   ⭐ High volume, replay, multiple independent consumers,
⭐     retention as a feature ⇒ ⭐⭐ KAFKA.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ "WE'LL USE KAFKA" FOR 50 MESSAGES/SECOND IS THE SAME
⭐     JUDGEMENT ERROR AS SHARDING A 250 MB DATABASE (Day 323).
```

# Part 6 — ⭐⭐ The event schema, which is a public API forever

```
⭐⭐ ONCE PUBLISHED, AN EVENT IS AN API YOU CANNOT SEE THE
⭐   CALLERS OF. ⭐⭐ AND IF IT IS RETAINED AND REPLAYABLE, IT IS
⭐   AN API YOU CANNOT EVEN ***CHANGE THE PAST*** OF.

⭐⭐ THE ENVELOPE — EVERY EVENT, NO EXCEPTIONS:
⭐   ⭐ `event_id`      — ⭐⭐ THE CONSUMER'S IDEMPOTENCY KEY.
⭐   ⭐ `event_type` + `version`
⭐   ⭐ `occurred_at`   — ⭐⭐ WHEN IT HAPPENED, NOT WHEN IT WAS
⭐     PUBLISHED. ⭐ These differ, and the difference is your
⭐     outbox lag.
⭐   ⭐ `trace_id`      — Day 285.
⭐   ⭐ `producer` + `schema_ref`
⭐   ⭐ `payload`

⭐⭐ COMPATIBILITY RULES:
⭐   ⭐ ✔ Adding an optional field is safe.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ ⭐⭐ REMOVING OR RENAMING A FIELD IS A BREAKING CHANGE
⭐     TO CONSUMERS YOU HAVE NEVER MET.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ ⭐⭐ CHANGING THE ***MEANING*** OF A FIELD IS THE WORST
⭐     ONE, BECAUSE NOTHING FAILS. ⭐ `amount` going from
⭐     cents to units passes every schema check and silently
⭐     changes every downstream number by 100×.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ New meaning ⇒ ⭐⭐ NEW FIELD OR NEW EVENT VERSION.
⭐     Never a redefinition.

⭐⭐ AND THE HARD DESIGN QUESTION — ***THIN OR FAT EVENTS?***
⭐   ⭐ THIN (id only): small, always current when re-fetched,
⭐     ⭐⭐ AND COUPLES THE CONSUMER TO THE PRODUCER'S UPTIME.
⭐   ⭐ FAT (full snapshot): independent, replayable,
⭐     ⭐⭐ AND STALE BY CONSTRUCTION — it describes a moment.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE RESOLUTION IS TO ASK WHAT THE CONSUMER NEEDS:
⭐     ***IF IT NEEDS THE FACT AS IT WAS, SEND IT FAT. IF IT
⭐     NEEDS THE CURRENT STATE, SEND THE ID AND ACCEPT THE
⭐     COUPLING — OR ASK WHY IT IS EVENT-DRIVEN AT ALL.***
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ An invoice consumer wants the price ***at the time***.
⭐     A cache-warmer wants ***now***. Different events.
```

# Part 7 — ⭐ The four things every consumer must have

```
⭐⭐ NO EXCEPTIONS, AND EACH ONE IS A PRODUCTION INCIDENT
⭐   WAITING:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ IDEMPOTENCY — a processed-events table keyed by
⭐        `event_id`, or a natural key. ⭐ Day 260's key rule.
⭐   ② ⭐⭐ A DEAD-LETTER QUEUE, ***WITH AN ALERT AND AN
⭐        OWNER.*** ⭐ A DLQ nobody watches is a silent data-loss
⭐        mechanism with a reassuring name.
⭐   ③ ⭐ RETRY WITH BACKOFF AND JITTER, and a classification:
⭐        ⭐ transient ⇒ retry; ⭐ permanent (bad payload) ⇒ DLQ
⭐        immediately, ⭐⭐ DO NOT RETRY A MALFORMED MESSAGE 25
⭐        TIMES.
⭐   ④ ⭐⭐ LAG MONITORING ON ***OLDEST MESSAGE AGE***, NOT
⭐        DEPTH (Day 262). ⭐ Depth of 10,000 draining in 20 s
⭐        is healthy; depth of 3 stuck for an hour is an outage.

⭐⭐ PLUS THE ONE PEOPLE FORGET: ⭐⭐ A ***REPLAY*** PROCEDURE.
⭐   ***"HOW DO I REPROCESS YESTERDAY'S EVENTS AFTER FIXING THE
⭐      BUG?"*** ⭐ If the answer is "we can't", your bug fix
⭐   cannot repair the damage the bug caused, ⭐⭐ AND THAT IS
⭐   THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN AN INCIDENT AND A PERMANENT DATA
⭐   PROBLEM.
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ Calling a command an event | Nobody owns the failure. Six months later, nobody is accountable for welcome emails |
| ⭐⭐ Request/reply over a queue | An RPC with worse latency and no circuit breaker. Make the HTTP call |
| ⭐⭐ Events carrying only IDs | The consumer can't work while the producer is down — the exact coupling the queue removed |
| ⭐ Choreographing a money flow | Nobody can answer "where is order 123?", and compensation is unreasonable |
| ⭐⭐ Changing a field's meaning | Nothing fails. Cents-to-units silently multiplies every downstream number by 100 |
| ⭐ Consumers that aren't idempotent | Redelivery is normal operation, not an edge case |
| ⭐⭐ A DLQ with no alert or owner | A silent data-loss mechanism with a reassuring name |
| ⭐ Retrying a malformed message 25 times | Classify first: transient retries, permanent goes straight to the DLQ |
| ⭐ Alerting on queue depth | Depth 10,000 draining in 20 s is fine; depth 3 stuck for an hour is an outage |
| ⭐⭐ No replay procedure | Your bug fix cannot repair the damage the bug caused |
| ⭐ Kafka for 50 messages a second | Same judgement error as sharding a 250 MB database |
| ⭐ Redis pub/sub for anything durable | Fire-and-forget. A disconnected subscriber simply misses messages |

## Interview questions

**1. "Choreography or orchestration?"**
> ⭐⭐ **Orchestrate the business transaction, choreograph the side effects.** ⭐ Order to payment to
> inventory to shipment has money in it and an invalid intermediate state, so it gets an owner: a state
> machine with a row I can query when someone asks where order 123 is. ⭐ Receipts, analytics,
> cache-warming and CRM sync are side effects — they can fail independently without corrupting
> anything, so they subscribe. ⭐⭐ The dividing line is whether a failure halfway through leaves the
> business in an invalid state.

**2. "How do you tell a command from an event?"**
> ⭐ Commands are imperative, have one recipient, and the sender knows what should happen. Events are
> past tense and the sender must not care who listens. ⭐⭐ The test: **if deleting every subscriber
> would be a bug, it's a command wearing an event's name** — and that mislabelling is how
> responsibility gets lost, because a failed command is the sender's problem while an event with no
> listeners is fine.

**3. "What's a distributed monolith and how do you spot one?"**
> ⭐ Microservices that must be deployed together in a specific order, where one being down breaks the
> others — all the costs of distribution, none of the benefits. ⭐⭐ The diagnostic is brutal: **can
> each service be deployed, and its dependencies taken down for an hour, without involving another
> team?** ⭐ Queues create them via request/reply over a queue, unversioned shared schemas, ID-only
> events that force a callback, a shared database, and undocumented cross-topic ordering requirements.

**4. "Thin or fat events?"**
> ⭐ Depends on what the consumer needs, and I'd say which. ⭐⭐ **If it needs the fact as it was — an
> invoice needs the price at the time — send a snapshot.** If it needs current state, sending the ID
> is honest, but then the consumer is coupled to the producer's uptime and I'd ask why it's
> event-driven. ⭐ The practical middle is ID plus an immutable snapshot of the fields the consumer
> actually uses.

**5. "What must every consumer have?"**
> ⭐ Idempotency keyed on `event_id`, because redelivery is normal. ⭐⭐ A DLQ **with an alert and a
> named owner** — a DLQ nobody watches is silent data loss with a reassuring name. ⭐ Retry with
> backoff, jitter, and classification so malformed payloads go straight to the DLQ instead of being
> retried twenty-five times. ⭐ Lag alerting on oldest-message age rather than depth. ⭐⭐ And a replay
> procedure, because without one your bug fix can't repair the damage the bug caused.

**6. "When would you not use a queue?"**
> ⭐⭐ When the user needs the result before they leave the page — **that's not a queue job, that's a
> slow request I need to make faster.** ⭐ And when the volume doesn't justify the operational surface:
> a few thousand a second with Postgres already running is `SKIP LOCKED`, not Kafka. ⭐ The cost I'd
> quote is losing the return value, needing idempotency everywhere, losing free ordering, and debugging
> becoming archaeology.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes.** Open with the trade: **a queue converts a synchronous failure into a delayed one**,
which is usually an upgrade — but only if someone has decided who's responsible when the delay becomes
permanent. Give the four benefits fast, and stop on load levelling, because it's the one that turns
the utilisation cliff into a slope. Then the distinction that decides the architecture: **commands have
one recipient and a sender who cares; events are past tense and the sender must not know who listens**
