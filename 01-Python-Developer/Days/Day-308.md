# Day 308 ⏱️

**P-308** · ⏱️ **The LLD method** — ⭐⭐ requirements to working code in forty-five minutes

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** LEARN + BUILD

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: an LLD interview is not testing whether you know design patterns. It is
> testing whether you can produce a defensible design under time pressure while thinking out loud —
> which means the clock is part of the problem, and most failures are time-management failures rather
> than knowledge failures.**
>
> ⭐ You have designed two real systems. ⭐⭐ Today is doing it in forty-five minutes, with someone
> watching, on a problem you have never seen.

---

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ What is actually being assessed

```
⭐⭐ FIVE THINGS, IN THE ORDER AN INTERVIEWER NOTICES THEM:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ CAN YOU SCOPE? — do you narrow an open problem into
⭐        something buildable, out loud, ⭐ or do you try to
⭐        build everything and finish nothing?
⭐   ② ⭐ DO YOUR ABSTRACTIONS FIT THE PROBLEM — ⭐ neither a
⭐        god class nor a plugin framework for a parking lot.
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ DO YOU HANDLE THE HARD CASE — ⭐ concurrency,
⭐        failure, the invariant. ⭐⭐ THIS IS WHERE MOST
⭐        CANDIDATES ARE SEPARATED (Day 310).
⭐   ④ ⭐ CAN YOU WRITE CODE THAT RUNS — ⭐ not pseudocode, not
⭐        a diagram with `// TODO`.
⭐   ⑤ ⭐⭐ CAN YOU EXPLAIN A TRADE-OFF — including one you
⭐        chose against.

⭐⭐ AND WHAT IS ***NOT*** BEING ASSESSED, WHICH PEOPLE WASTE
⭐   TIME ON: ⭐ knowing all 23 GoF patterns · perfect UML
⭐   syntax · exhaustive getters and setters · handling every
⭐   edge case you can imagine.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ A DESIGN WITH THREE CLASSES THAT RUNS AND HANDLES
⭐     THE RACE BEATS TWELVE CLASSES THAT DO NOT COMPILE, EVERY
⭐     SINGLE TIME.
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ The four ways people fail

```
⭐⭐ ① ⭐⭐ SILENCE. ⭐ Ten minutes of thinking with a pen.
⭐     ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE INTERVIEWER CANNOT SCORE THINKING THEY CANNOT
⭐       HEAR — and worse, they cannot correct you, so a wrong
⭐       assumption at minute 3 is discovered at minute 40.
⭐     ⇒ ⭐ Narrate: "I'm deciding whether Spot knows about
⭐       Vehicle or the other way round; I'll go with…"

⭐⭐ ② JUMPING STRAIGHT TO CODE. ⭐ Typing at minute two.
⭐     ⇒ ⭐ You will design the wrong thing quickly, and the
⭐       sunk cost makes you defend it.

⭐⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ GOLD-PLATING. ⭐ An abstract factory, a plugin
⭐     registry and an event bus — for a parking lot.
⭐     ⇒ ⭐⭐ THIS READS AS ***POOR JUDGEMENT***, NOT AS
⭐       SOPHISTICATION, because the interviewer's next thought
⭐       is "what would they do to our codebase?"

⭐⭐ ④ ANALYSIS PARALYSIS AT REQUIREMENTS. ⭐ Fifteen minutes
⭐     of clarifying questions.
⭐     ⇒ ⭐ Five questions, then ⭐⭐ STATE YOUR ASSUMPTIONS AND
⭐       MOVE. "I'll assume single building, multiple floors —
⭐       tell me if that's wrong" ⭐ converts a question into
⭐       progress.

⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ ALL FOUR ARE TIME-MANAGEMENT FAILURES. ⭐ Which is
⭐   why the clock in Part 3 is the actual content of this day.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ The forty-five-minute clock

```
⭐⭐ 0–5 · REQUIREMENTS AND SCOPE
⭐   ⭐ up to 5 questions, each of which CHANGES THE DESIGN
⭐   ⭐ state 4–6 functional requirements back, out loud
⭐   ⭐⭐ SAY THE OUT-OF-SCOPE LIST EXPLICITLY. ⭐ "No payments
⭐     integration, no persistence, no UI — say if you want any
⭐     of those instead." ⭐⭐ THIS SINGLE SENTENCE PREVENTS
⭐     THE MOST COMMON FAILURE, WHICH IS RUNNING OUT OF TIME.

⭐⭐ 5–10 · ENTITIES AND VALUE OBJECTS
⭐   ⭐ nouns → candidates → ⭐⭐ PRUNE HARD (Part 5)
⭐   ⭐ name the enums and value objects (Day 304)
⭐   ⭐ say which is the aggregate and what invariant it holds

⭐⭐ 10–15 · INTERFACES AND THE MAIN FLOW
⭐   ⭐ the 2–3 interfaces where a decision lives
⭐   ⭐⭐ WALK ONE END-TO-END FLOW ALOUD before typing —
⭐     ⭐ "car arrives → find spot → issue ticket" — because
⭐     this is where you discover a missing class for free

⭐⭐ 15–30 · CODE THE CORE
⭐   ⭐ the aggregate, the strategy interface, the main
⭐     operation. ⭐⭐ STUBS WITH HONEST SIGNATURES FOR THE REST.

⭐⭐ 30–40 · THE HARD PART
⭐   ⭐⭐ CONCURRENCY (Day 310), THE INVARIANT, AND ONE
⭐     EXTENSION THEY ASK FOR. ⭐ Expect the extension: it is
⭐     always coming, and the design is really being judged on
⭐     how much it costs.

⭐⭐ 40–45 · TESTS AND TRADE-OFFS
⭐   ⭐ name the 3 tests you'd write first
⭐   ⭐ one thing you'd change with more time, ⭐⭐ AND ONE
⭐     THING THAT BREAKS AT 100×

⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE RULE THAT SAVES THE INTERVIEW: ***AT MINUTE 30,
⭐   STOP ADDING CLASSES.*** ⭐ Whatever exists at minute 30 is
⭐   what you will defend. ⭐⭐ CANDIDATES WHO ARE STILL
⭐   CREATING CLASSES AT MINUTE 38 FINISH WITH NOTHING THAT RUNS
⭐   AND NO ANSWER ON CONCURRENCY.
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ The five questions worth asking

```
⭐⭐ EACH MUST CHANGE THE DESIGN. IF THE ANSWER WOULD NOT
⭐   CHANGE WHAT YOU BUILD, DO NOT SPEND A MINUTE ON IT.

⭐ ① ⭐⭐ "HOW MANY CONCURRENT USERS, AND DOES IT NEED TO BE
⭐     EXACTLY CORRECT?" ⭐⭐ THE HIGHEST-VALUE QUESTION IN ANY
⭐     LLD INTERVIEW. ⭐ It tells you whether Part 30–40 is
⭐     locks, atomic operations or nothing at all — and asking
⭐     it early signals you know it is coming.

⭐ ② ⭐ "SINGLE PROCESS, OR MULTIPLE?" ⇒ ⭐⭐ DECIDES WHETHER
⭐     AN IN-PROCESS LOCK IS EVEN A LEGITIMATE ANSWER (Day 310).

⭐ ③ ⭐ "IS X FIXED OR CONFIGURABLE?" ⇒ decides whether a
⭐     strategy interface is justified or gold-plating.

⭐ ④ ⭐ "WHAT MUST NEVER HAPPEN?" ⇒ ⭐⭐ ASKS THE INTERVIEWER
⭐     TO HAND YOU THE INVARIANT, and they usually will.

⭐ ⑤ ⭐ "DO WE NEED HISTORY, OR ONLY CURRENT STATE?" ⇒ decides
⭐     whether you model events or mutate in place.

⭐ ⇒ ⭐ AND THEN: ⭐⭐ "I'LL ASSUME THE REST — STOP ME IF AN
⭐   ASSUMPTION IS WRONG." ⭐ Interviewers reliably interrupt
⭐   when it matters, which makes assuming safer than asking.
```

---

# Part 5 — ⭐⭐ From nouns to classes: prune hard

```
⭐⭐ THE TECHNIQUE: UNDERLINE THE NOUNS, ⭐ THEN DELETE MOST
⭐   OF THEM.
⭐   ⭐ "A ***parking lot*** has multiple ***floors***. Each
⭐     floor has ***spots*** of different ***sizes***. A
⭐     ***vehicle*** arriving is issued a ***ticket***. On
⭐     exit, a ***fee*** is calculated by ***duration***."

⭐⭐ THREE PRUNING RULES:
⭐   ⭐ ① A NOUN WITH NO BEHAVIOUR AND NO IDENTITY IS AN
⭐        ATTRIBUTE OR AN ENUM. ⭐ `Size` ⇒ `enum SpotSize`.
⭐        `Colour` ⇒ ⭐⭐ NOT A CLASS, AND NOT EVEN A FIELD
⭐        UNLESS SOMETHING USES IT.
⭐   ⭐ ② A NOUN THAT IS ONLY A CALCULATION IS A ***METHOD OR A
⭐        STRATEGY***, not a class. ⭐ `Fee` ⇒ `Money` returned
⭐        by `PricingStrategy.calculate()`.
⭐   ⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ A NOUN THAT ONLY EXISTS IN ONE SENTENCE OF THE
⭐        PROBLEM IS PROBABLY OUT OF SCOPE. ⭐ Say so out loud
⭐        and move on.

⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ TARGET: 4–6 CLASSES. ⭐ More than eight in a
⭐   45-minute problem and you will not finish. ⭐⭐ AND THE
⭐   PRUNING ITSELF IS SCORED — an interviewer watching you
⭐   reject `Colour` as a class is watching judgement, which is
⭐   the thing they cannot test any other way.
```

---

# Part 6 — ⭐⭐ What to actually code

```
⭐⭐ THE RULE: ***CODE THE PART WHERE THE DESIGN DECISION
⭐   LIVES. STUB THE REST WITH HONEST SIGNATURES.***
⭐   ⭐ For a parking lot that is: the allocation method (with
⭐     its race), and the strategy interface. ⭐⭐ NOT
⭐     `Vehicle.__init__`, WHICH IS FOUR LINES OF NOTHING AND
⭐     COSTS YOU TWO MINUTES.

⭐ ⭐ WHAT MAKES CODE LOOK SENIOR IN THIS SETTING:
⭐   ⭐ type hints on every signature — ⭐ free, and it makes
⭐     the design readable without explanation
⭐   ⭐ enums instead of strings (Day 298: meaning → type)
⭐   ⭐⭐ RAISING A ***DOMAIN EXCEPTION*** RATHER THAN RETURNING
⭐     `None` — ⭐ `raise NoSpotAvailable()` says what happened;
⭐     `return None` makes every caller guess
⭐   ⭐ a `Protocol` for the one thing that varies (Day 300)
⭐   ⭐⭐ AND A `main()` OR A TEST THAT ACTUALLY RUNS THE FLOW —
⭐     ⭐ worth more than two extra classes, because it proves
⭐     the pieces fit together, which is the only claim a
⭐     diagram cannot make.

⭐ ⭐ AND WHAT TO SAY WHILE STUBBING: ⭐⭐ "I'M STUBBING
⭐   PERSISTENCE — IT'S A REPOSITORY INTERFACE AND THE
⭐   IMPLEMENTATION IS NOT WHERE THE INTERESTING DECISION IS."
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ That sentence converts an omission into a decision.
```

# Part 7 — ⭐⭐ Talking while designing

```
⭐   ⭐ NARRATE ***DECISIONS***, NOT TYPING. ⭐ "I'm making
⭐     Spot own its occupancy rather than the Lot owning a map,
⭐     because the invariant is per-spot" ⭐⭐ IS THE ENTIRE
⭐     SIGNAL. ⭐ "Now I'll write a for loop" is noise.
⭐   ⭐ STATE TRADE-OFFS AS YOU MAKE THEM: ⭐ "a dict of
⭐     available spots per size is O(1) allocation but more
⭐     state to keep consistent; ⭐ I'll scan for now and note
⭐     it."
⭐   ⭐⭐ AND THE RECOVERY MOVE, WHICH IS SCORED POSITIVELY:
⭐     ***"I'D CHANGE THAT — HERE'S WHY."*** ⭐ Realising your
⭐     own design is wrong and saying so is a strong signal.
⭐     ⭐⭐ DEFENDING A DESIGN YOU HAVE STOPPED BELIEVING IN IS
⭐     THE STRONGEST NEGATIVE SIGNAL IN THE WHOLE INTERVIEW.
⭐   ⭐ When they suggest something, ⭐⭐ ENGAGE WITH THE
⭐     TRADE-OFF RATHER THAN COMPLYING INSTANTLY. ⭐ "That
⭐     would make X easier and Y harder — do you want to
⭐     optimise for X?" ⭐ Instant agreement reads as having no
⭐     opinion.
⭐   ⭐ THE LAST TWO MINUTES, ALWAYS: ⭐ what I'd add, what I'd
⭐     test first, ⭐⭐ AND WHAT BREAKS AT 100× — which
⭐     converts an LLD answer into evidence that you think about
⭐     systems (Day 310, and Stage 9).
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Silent thinking | Unhearable thinking is unscorable, and a wrong assumption goes uncorrected for 37 minutes. |
| Coding at minute two | You'll design the wrong thing quickly and then defend it. |
| Patterns for their own sake | A plugin registry for a parking lot reads as poor judgement, not sophistication. |
| Fifteen minutes of clarifying questions | Five questions, then state assumptions and move. |
| Questions whose answers change nothing | Don't spend a minute on them. |
| Not stating what's out of scope | The single sentence that prevents running out of time. |
| Turning every noun into a class | Prune to 4–6; the pruning itself is being scored. |
| A class for a calculation | That's a method or a strategy. |
| Still adding classes at minute 38 | Nothing runs and there's no concurrency answer. |
| Coding constructors and getters | Code where the decision lives; stub the rest. |
| Returning `None` for a failure | A domain exception says what happened; `None` makes every caller guess. |
| Strings instead of enums | Connascence of meaning, for free. |
| No runnable `main()` | It's the only proof the pieces fit, which a diagram can't give. |
| Narrating typing instead of decisions | "Now a for loop" is noise; the reason for an ownership choice is the signal. |
| Defending a design you've stopped believing | The strongest negative signal available. |
| Instantly agreeing with every suggestion | Reads as having no opinion; engage with the trade-off. |
| Ending without trade-offs | Two minutes on what you'd change and what breaks at 100× is free marks. |

## Interview questions

**Q: How do you approach a 45-minute design problem?**

> With a clock, because most failures are time-management rather than knowledge. Five minutes on
> requirements — up to five questions that would each change the design, then requirements stated back
> and an explicit out-of-scope list, which is the single sentence that stops me running out of time.
> Five on entities, pruning hard to four to six classes. Five walking one flow aloud, which is where I
> find the missing class for free. Fifteen coding the part where the decision lives. Ten on
> concurrency and whatever extension they ask for. Five on tests and trade-offs. And a hard rule: at
> minute thirty I stop adding classes, because whatever exists then is what I'll defend.

**Q: What's the most valuable question to ask?**

> "How many concurrent users, and does it need to be exactly correct?" It decides whether the hard
> part of the design is a lock, an atomic operation, or nothing at all — and asking it in the first
> five minutes signals that I know the concurrency question is coming, which is where most candidates
> get separated. Close behind: "single process or multiple", because it decides whether an in-process
> lock is even a legitimate answer, and "what must never happen", which asks the interviewer to hand
> me the invariant.

**Q: How do you decide what's a class?**

> Underline the nouns, then delete most of them. A noun with no behaviour and no identity is an
> attribute or an enum — size becomes `SpotSize`, colour usually isn't even a field. A noun that's
> only a calculation is a method or a strategy — a fee is `Money` returned by a pricing strategy. And
> a noun appearing in one sentence of the problem is probably out of scope, which I say out loud. I
> target four to six classes, and I'm aware the pruning itself is being scored: rejecting `Colour` as
> a class in front of someone is a demonstration of judgement that no question can test directly.

**Q: What do you code, given fifteen minutes?**

> The part where the design decision lives — for a parking lot, the allocation method including its
> race, and the strategy interface — and honest stubs for everything else, with a sentence saying why:
> "I'm stubbing persistence; it's a repository interface and the implementation isn't where the
> interesting decision is." That converts an omission into a decision. I make sure there's a `main()`
> or a test that actually runs the flow, because that's the only proof the pieces fit together, and
> it's worth more than two extra classes.

**Q: What do you do if you realise your design is wrong at minute 25?**

> Say so, and say why. "I'd change this — the invariant is per-spot, so `Spot` should own its
> occupancy rather than the lot owning a map" is scored positively, because noticing your own mistake
> under time pressure is exactly the behaviour they want on a team. The strong negative signal is the
> opposite: defending a design you've stopped believing in. Same with suggestions — I engage with the
> trade-off rather than agreeing instantly, because instant agreement reads as having no opinion.

## Today's build

1. ⭐⭐ Write the clock on a card and keep it visible. You will use it eleven more times this stage.
2. ⭐ Write your five standing questions, in priority order.
3. ⭐⭐ Take any LLD problem you haven't seen. Set a 45-minute timer and record yourself.
4. ⭐⭐ Play the recording back. Count your silent stretches over 20 seconds.
5. ⭐ Check where you were at minute 30. Were you still adding classes?
6. ⭐⭐ Count your classes. If more than eight, redo the pruning and note what you'd cut.
7. ⭐ Check that something in your solution actually runs.
8. ⭐⭐ Do a second problem, changing only one thing: narrate every decision aloud.
9. ⭐ Compare the two recordings for how much of the design is audible.
10. ⭐⭐ Practise the recovery move: mid-problem, deliberately change a decision and explain why.
11. ⭐ Practise the last two minutes standalone: tests, one change, what breaks at 100×.
12. ⭐⭐ Write your out-of-scope sentence as a template you can say in ten seconds.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes: "How do you approach a design interview?"**

Lead with the diagnosis, because it reframes the whole thing: **most LLD failures are
time-management failures, not knowledge failures** — silence, coding at minute two, gold-plating, and
fifteen minutes of clarifying questions. So you run a clock: five minutes scoping, five on entities,
five walking a flow aloud, fifteen coding, ten on the hard part, five on trade-offs. Name the two
rules that do the most work. **Say the out-of-scope list explicitly** — one sentence that prevents the
commonest failure — and **stop adding classes at minute thirty**, because whatever exists then is what
you'll defend, and candidates still creating classes at 38 finish with nothing running and no answer
on concurrency. Give your highest-value question and why: **"how many concurrent users, and does it
need to be exactly correct?"** decides whether the hard part is a lock, an atomic operation, or
nothing — and asking it early signals you know it's coming. Then pruning: **underline the nouns and
delete most of them**, because a noun with no behaviour is an enum and a noun that's a calculation is
a strategy — and the pruning is itself being scored. Close on the two behavioural signals: **narrate
decisions rather than typing**, and if you realise you're wrong, say so — **defending a design you've
stopped believing in is the strongest negative signal in the interview.**
