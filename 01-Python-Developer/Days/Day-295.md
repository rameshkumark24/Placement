# Day 295 📝

**P-295** · 📝 **The ADRs you never wrote** — ⭐⭐ reconstructing the decisions

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** BUILD

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: every magic number in this project is an unrecorded decision, and a
> decision you cannot name is one you will re-make by accident — usually differently, usually in a
> hurry, usually six months later when the person who made it first was you.**
>
> ⭐ The flagship recorded five ADRs *before* building (Day 252). ⭐⭐ This project could not: half
> of its decisions only became visible when a number moved. Today you go back and find them.

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ Why this project accumulated hidden decisions

```
⭐⭐ THE FLAGSHIP'S DECISIONS ANNOUNCED THEMSELVES. ⭐ "Which
⭐   database", "how do we do auth", "is this one service or
⭐   two" — ⭐ big, discrete, obviously decisions, made in
⭐   advance because you could not proceed without them.

⭐⭐ THIS PROJECT'S DECISIONS ARRIVED AS ***DEFAULTS***:
⭐   ⭐ `chunk_size = 800`        — ⭐⭐ WHY 800?
⭐   ⭐ `k = 5`                   — ⭐⭐ WHY 5?
⭐   ⭐ `REFUSAL_THRESHOLD = 0.32`— ⭐⭐ WHERE DID THAT COME FROM?
⭐   ⭐ `rrf_k = 60`              — ⭐ copied from a paper
⭐   ⭐ `overlap = 0.15`          — ⭐ it was in an example
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ NONE OF THESE FELT LIKE A DECISION AT THE MOMENT IT
⭐     WAS MADE. ⭐ Each was "a reasonable starting value" — and
⭐     a reasonable starting value that shipped is a decision
⭐     with no author.

⭐⭐ WHY IT MATTERS, CONCRETELY:
⭐   ⭐ In four months someone (you) will double `chunk_size` to
⭐     "improve context". ⭐⭐ RECALL DROPS FOUR POINTS AND
⭐     NOBODY CONNECTS THE TWO, because the answers still read
⭐     well (Day 293).
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ An ADR saying "800 chosen after measuring 400/800/1600;
⭐     1600 lost 6 points of recall" ⭐⭐ COSTS TEN MINUTES NOW
⭐     AND SAVES THAT ENTIRE INVESTIGATION.
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ Archaeology: four places decisions hide

```
⭐⭐ ① EVERY CONSTANT. ⭐ Grep for numeric literals in config
⭐     and pipeline code. ⭐⭐ EACH ONE IS AN UNRECORDED
⭐     DECISION UNTIL PROVEN OTHERWISE. ⭐ Most will take
⭐     thirty seconds to justify or one minute to admit you
⭐     copied — and both outcomes are useful.

⭐⭐ ② EVERY PLACE YOU WROTE A COMMENT SAYING "for now",
⭐     "temporary", "TODO", or "seems to work".
⭐     ⇒ ⭐⭐ THOSE COMMENTS ARE DECISIONS WEARING AN APOLOGY.

⭐⭐ ③ THE GIT LOG. ⭐ A commit that changes a default, or
⭐     reverts something, marks a decision point — ⭐ and the
⭐     message often contains the only surviving reasoning.
⭐     ⭐⭐ A REVERT IS THE HIGHEST-VALUE ARCHAEOLOGY THERE IS:
⭐     it means you tried something and it was wrong, which is
⭐     precisely the evidence an ADR wants.

⭐⭐ ④ ANYWHERE YOU HESITATED. ⭐ You remember hesitating. If
⭐     you had to think for ten minutes, it was a decision, and
⭐     ⭐⭐ THE FACT THAT IT WAS HARD IS ITSELF THE MOST USEFUL
⭐     THING TO RECORD — it tells the next person the
⭐     alternatives were close, so the change they are
⭐     contemplating is not obviously wrong.

⭐ ⇒ ⭐ Do this as a single 45-minute pass over the repository.
⭐   ⭐⭐ EXPECT TWENTY CANDIDATES AND KEEP THE SIX THAT WOULD
⭐   CHANGE SOMEONE'S BEHAVIOUR. ⭐ An ADR for every constant
⭐   is a filing system, not a record.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Writing one honestly, after the fact

```
⭐⭐ THE TRAP, AND IT IS ALMOST IRRESISTIBLE: ***RETCONNING.***
⭐   ⭐ You write "we chose structural chunking because section
⭐     boundaries are semantic boundaries" — ⭐⭐ WHEN WHAT
⭐     ACTUALLY HAPPENED IS THAT FIXED-SIZE CHUNKING SPLIT A
⭐     TABLE IN HALF, YOU GOT ANNOYED, AND YOU CHANGED IT.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ The rationalisation is not exactly false. ⭐⭐ BUT IT
⭐     RECORDS A DELIBERATION THAT DID NOT OCCUR, AND IT TEACHES
⭐     THE NEXT READER — YOU — THAT YOU REASON MORE CAREFULLY
⭐     THAN YOU DO. ⭐ Which is how the same mistake happens
⭐     twice.

⭐⭐ A FORMAT THAT RESISTS IT — FIVE SECTIONS, AND THE THIRD IS
⭐   THE UNUSUAL ONE:
⭐   ⭐ WHAT WE DID — the decision, stated flatly.
⭐   ⭐ WHAT WE KNEW AT THE TIME — including "nothing; it was
⭐     the library default".
⭐   ⭐⭐ WHAT WE DID ***NOT*** KNOW — ⭐ the section that makes
⭐     the document honest and useful. "We had no eval set when
⭐     we chose this."
⭐   ⭐ WHAT IT WOULD TAKE TO REVISIT — the experiment, named,
⭐     so the next person does not have to design it.
⭐   ⭐ WHAT WE OBSERVED SINCE — filled in later, and this is
⭐     ⭐⭐ WHERE AN ADR STOPS BEING PAPERWORK AND STARTS BEING
⭐     A RECORD OF LEARNING.

⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ AND IT IS ENTIRELY LEGITIMATE TO WRITE: "CHOSEN
⭐   ARBITRARILY FROM A TUTORIAL. NEVER MEASURED. FIRST THING
⭐   I WOULD TEST." ⭐ That is a stronger sentence than a
⭐   fabricated rationale, and in an interview it is far
⭐   stronger, because it is obviously true.
```

# Part 5 — ⭐ Superseding, never editing

```
⭐   ⭐ AN ADR IS IMMUTABLE ONCE ACCEPTED. ⭐ Changed your mind?
⭐     Write ADR-009, mark ADR-004 `Superseded by 009`, and
⭐     ⭐⭐ KEEP 004 EXACTLY AS IT WAS.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE PAIR IS WORTH FAR MORE THAN THE CORRECTED
⭐     DOCUMENT: it shows what you believed, what changed, and
⭐     what evidence moved you. ⭐ That is the artefact of an
⭐     engineer who learns.
⭐   ⭐ Exactly the Day 289 rule about decision records, applied
⭐     to your own reasoning — ⭐ and for the same reason: an
⭐     edit destroys the evidence that you changed your mind.

⭐   ⭐ THE LOG READS AS A NARRATIVE: "started with vector-only
⭐     because it was the obvious thing; added lexical when
⭐     negation queries failed; added reranking when MRR was
⭐     the bottleneck rather than recall."
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THAT SEQUENCE IS THE MOST CONVINCING THING IN THE
⭐     REPOSITORY, because nobody can fabricate a plausible
⭐     ORDER of discoveries.
```

# Part 7 — ⭐⭐ What ADRs are for in an interview

```
⭐⭐ THEY CONVERT ***"I BUILT A THING"*** INTO ***"I MADE
⭐   CHOICES"***, WHICH IS THE ENTIRE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN A
⭐   TUTORIAL AND ENGINEERING.

⭐   ⭐ "I used hybrid search" ⇒ ⭐ a fact about your code.
⭐   ⭐⭐ "I STARTED WITH VECTORS, FOUND NEGATION QUERIES

⭐     BECAUSE THE SCORES AREN'T COMPARABLE, AND RECALL@10 WENT

⭐⭐ AND THE MOST USEFUL ADR YOU OWN IS THE ONE WHERE YOU WERE
⭐   WRONG. ⭐ It is the only kind that proves the process is
⭐   real rather than reconstructed — ⭐⭐ BECAUSE NOBODY
⭐   FABRICATES A RECORD OF THEIR OWN MISTAKE.
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Leaving constants unexamined | Every magic number is an unrecorded decision. |
| Ignoring "for now" comments | Those are decisions wearing an apology. |
| Skipping the git log | A revert is the highest-value archaeology there is. |
| Retconning a rationale | It records a deliberation that didn't happen and teaches you to trust yourself too much. |
| Omitting "what we didn't know" | That section is what makes the document honest and useful. |
| No named experiment for revisiting | The next person has to design it, so they won't. |
| Pretending a default was a choice | "Copied from a tutorial, never measured" is stronger and obviously true. |
| An ADR per constant | That's a filing system, not a record. Keep the six that change behaviour. |
| Editing an accepted ADR | The superseded pair is worth more than the corrected document. |
| A threshold recorded without its cost | It reads as caution rather than a chosen trade-off. |
| Not marking structural decisions as permanent | "Revisit if: never" is itself information. |
| Skipping the "would I do it again" pass | It's the answer to the question that most often decides interviews. |
| Holding a position with no experiment that would change it | You inherited it. Say so. |
| Hiding the decision you got wrong | It's the only kind nobody fabricates. |

## Interview questions

**Q: How do you document decisions in a project like this?**

> Differently from the flagship, and I'd say why. There the decisions announced themselves — which
> database, how auth works — so they were recorded before building. Here most of them arrived as
> defaults: chunk size 800, k of 5, an RRF constant copied from a paper. None of those felt like
> decisions when they were made, but a reasonable starting value that ships is a decision with no
> author. So I did an archaeology pass — every constant, every "for now" comment, every revert in the
> git log, and everywhere I remember hesitating — and kept the six that would change someone's
> behaviour.

**Q: Isn't writing an ADR after the fact just rationalising?**

> It is, if you let it. The trap is writing "we chose structural chunking because section boundaries
> are semantic boundaries" when what actually happened is that fixed-size chunking split a table and I
> got annoyed. That's not false, but it records a deliberation that didn't occur and teaches me I
> reason more carefully than I do, which is how the same mistake happens twice. The format I use
> resists it: what we did, what we knew at the time, what we *didn't* know, what it would take to
> revisit, and what we observed since. And "chosen arbitrarily from a tutorial, never measured, first
> thing I'd test" is a legitimate entry — a stronger one than a fabricated rationale, because it's
> obviously true.

**Q: Which ADR is the most useful?**

> The one where I was wrong, because it's the only kind nobody fabricates. Mine is that I tuned chunk
> size before adding the ancestry prefix, so the comparison ran on a worse baseline and the conclusion
> doesn't hold — it needs repeating. Close behind is the hybrid retrieval one, because it has numbers
> in it: recall@10 went from 0.83 to 0.89, which makes it the easiest decision to defend and the
> hardest to argue with.

**Q: What do you do when you change your mind?**

> Write a new ADR and mark the old one superseded, never edit it. The pair is worth much more than the
> corrected document, because it shows what I believed, what changed, and what evidence moved me — and
> that sequence, read in order, is the most convincing thing in the repository, since nobody can
> fabricate a plausible order of discoveries. It's the same rule as the decision records in the
> system itself: an edit destroys the evidence that you changed your mind.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes: "How did you make your design decisions?"**

Start by distinguishing the two projects, because it shows you noticed: **the flagship's decisions
announced themselves and were recorded before building; this one's arrived as defaults** — chunk size,
k, a constant copied from a paper — and none of them felt like a decision at the time, which is
exactly why they went unrecorded. **A reasonable starting value that ships is a decision with no
author**, and four months later someone doubles it, recall drops four points, and nobody connects the
two because the answers still read fine. So describe the archaeology: every constant, every "for now"
comment, every revert in the git log, and everywhere you hesitated — then keeping only the six that
would change someone's behaviour. Name the trap explicitly: **retconning a rationale isn't exactly
false, but it records a deliberation that didn't happen and teaches you that you reason more carefully
than you do.** The format that resists it includes what you *didn't* know at the time, and it's
entirely legitimate to write "copied from a tutorial, never measured, first thing I'd test." Close on
the two things that make the log worth reading: **you supersede rather than edit, so the pair shows
what changed your mind**, and **the most useful ADR you own is the one where you were wrong — because
it's the only kind nobody fabricates.**
