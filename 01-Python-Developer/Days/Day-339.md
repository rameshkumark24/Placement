# Day 339 🧯

**P-339** · 🧯 **Resilience patterns** — ⭐⭐ the anatomy of a cascading failure

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: large outages are almost never caused by the thing that broke — they are
> caused by how the healthy parts of the system responded to it, and the single most common
> accelerant is retries, which convert a small failure into a load multiplier aimed at the component
> least able to withstand it.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ The anatomy, told as one story

```
⭐⭐ LEARN THIS AS A NARRATIVE. YOU WILL RECOGNISE IT IN EVERY
⭐   PUBLIC POST-MORTEM YOU EVER READ.

⭐   ① ⭐ A cache node dies. Hit ratio drops from 99% to 60%.
⭐   ② ⭐ Database load rises 40×. Queries slow from 5 ms to
⭐        400 ms.
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ APP THREADS/WORKERS NOW BLOCK 400 ms EACH. ⭐ By
⭐        Little's Law, in-flight requests rise 80× — ⭐⭐ AND
⭐        THE POOL IS EXHAUSTED.
⭐   ④ ⭐ Requests queue. Latency crosses the client's 2 s
⭐        timeout.
⭐   ⑤ ⭐⭐ CLIENTS RETRY. ARRIVAL RATE ***TRIPLES***, AND EVERY
⭐        RETRY TARGETS THE ALREADY-SATURATED DATABASE.
⭐   ⑥ ⭐⭐ HEALTH CHECKS TIME OUT. THE LOAD BALANCER EJECTS
⭐        HEALTHY PODS (Day 325).
⭐   ⑦ ⭐ Remaining pods take the ejected traffic and die faster.
⭐   ⑧ ⭐⭐ TOTAL OUTAGE. ⭐ Now you restart everything —
⭐        ⭐⭐ AND THE COLD CACHE PLUS THE RECONNECTION STAMPEDE
⭐        KILLS IT AGAIN. ⭐ This is the part that turns a
⭐        20-minute incident into a two-hour one.

⭐⭐ THE THREE OBSERVATIONS THAT MATTER:
⭐   ⭐ ① ***THE ORIGINAL FAULT WAS SURVIVABLE.*** One cache
⭐     node. ⭐⭐ EVERY SUBSEQUENT STEP WAS THE SYSTEM'S OWN
⭐     BEHAVIOUR.
⭐   ⭐ ② ⭐⭐ RETRIES AND HEALTH CHECKS — THE TWO MECHANISMS
⭐     ***BUILT TO IMPROVE RELIABILITY*** — DID THE MOST
⭐     DAMAGE.
⭐   ⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ RECOVERY IS A SECOND OUTAGE UNLESS IT IS DESIGNED.
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ Timeouts: the one everybody gets wrong first

```
⭐⭐ THE DEFAULT IN MOST LIBRARIES IS ***NO TIMEOUT***.
⭐   ⭐ `requests.get(url)` — ⭐⭐ WAITS FOREVER.
⭐   ⭐ `httpx` — has a 5 s default, ⭐ which is better and still
⭐     probably wrong for your case.
⭐   ⭐ A DB connection with no `statement_timeout` — forever.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ ONE HUNG DEPENDENCY WITH NO TIMEOUT WILL CONSUME
⭐     EVERY WORKER YOU HAVE, AND THE SERVICE THAT DIES IS
⭐     ***YOURS***, NOT THEIRS.

⭐⭐ HOW TO PICK ONE — ⭐⭐ NOT BY FEEL:
⭐   ***TIMEOUT ≈ p99.9 OF THE DEPENDENCY, ROUNDED UP.***
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Below p99 and you fail requests that would have
⭐     succeeded ⭐⭐ AND YOU RETRY THEM, ADDING LOAD.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Far above and you hold resources for work already
⭐     abandoned.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ AND IF YOU DON'T KNOW THE p99.9, THAT IS THE
⭐     FINDING: YOU ARE CALLING SOMETHING YOU HAVE NOT
⭐     MEASURED.

⭐⭐ FOUR TIMEOUTS, NOT ONE — ⭐ people set the last and forget
⭐   the first three:
⭐   ⭐ connect · read · write · ⭐⭐ TOTAL (the pool wait is
⭐     part of it, and it is invisible in most libraries).
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ `httpx.Timeout(connect=1, read=3, write=3, pool=1)`

⭐⭐ AND THE RULE FROM DAY 325, RESTATED AS THE LAW IT IS:
⭐   ***TIMEOUTS MUST DECREASE AS YOU GO INWARD, AND A DEADLINE
⭐      (Day 336) ENFORCES THIS AUTOMATICALLY.***
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Retries: necessary, and the accelerant

```
⭐⭐ THE PARADOX: retries fix transient failures ⭐⭐ AND CAUSE
⭐   CASCADING ONES. ⭐ Both are true, so the engineering is in
⭐   the constraints.

⭐⭐ THE FIVE RULES — ALL FIVE, EVERY TIME:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ ONLY RETRY IDEMPOTENT OPERATIONS, or ones carrying an
⭐        idempotency key (Day 335).
⭐   ② ⭐⭐ ONLY RETRY ***RETRYABLE*** ERRORS. ⭐ 503, 429,
⭐        connection reset, timeout — yes. ⭐ 400, 401, 403,
⭐        404, 422 — ⭐⭐ NEVER. RETRYING A 400 IS PURE LOAD
⭐        WITH ZERO CHANCE OF SUCCESS.
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ EXPONENTIAL BACKOFF ***WITH JITTER***. ⭐ Without
⭐        jitter, every client that failed at t retries at
⭐        t+1s ***together***, forever, in a synchronised wave
⭐        you created.
⭐        ⇒ ⭐ Full jitter: `sleep = random(0, min(cap, base *
⭐          2**n))`. ⭐⭐ NOT "backoff ± 10%".
⭐   ④ ⭐⭐ A ***RETRY BUDGET***, NOT A RETRY COUNT.
⭐        ⇒ ⭐ "Retries may not exceed 10% of total requests."
⭐        ⇒ ⭐⭐ THIS IS THE RULE THAT ACTUALLY PREVENTS THE
⭐          CASCADE, AND ALMOST NOBODY IMPLEMENTS IT. A count
⭐          of 3 still means 3× load when ***everything***
⭐          fails; a budget caps the total amplification no
⭐          matter how bad things get.
⭐   ⑤ ⭐⭐ NEVER RETRY AT MORE THAN ONE LAYER. ⭐ 3 retries in
⭐        the SDK × 3 in your client × 3 at the gateway =
⭐        ⭐⭐ 27 REQUESTS FOR ONE USER ACTION. ⭐ Pick the layer
⭐        closest to the user that can still be safe, and turn
⭐        the others ***off explicitly*** — including the ones
⭐        your libraries enable by default.
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ Circuit breakers and bulkheads

```
⭐⭐ ***CIRCUIT BREAKER*** — stop calling a dependency that is
⭐   clearly broken.
⭐   ⭐ CLOSED ⇒ calls pass, failures counted.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ OPEN ⇒ FAIL IMMEDIATELY WITHOUT CALLING. ⭐ This is
⭐     the point: ⭐⭐ IT PROTECTS ***YOU*** FROM WAITING, AND IT
⭐     PROTECTS ***THEM*** FROM THE LOAD THAT IS KEEPING THEM
⭐     DOWN.
⭐   ⭐ HALF-OPEN ⇒ after a cooldown, allow a ***few*** probes.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE MOST COMMON BUG: FLOODING A RECOVERING SERVICE
⭐     THE INSTANT THE BREAKER CLOSES. ⭐ Ramp, don't restore
⭐     — ⭐⭐ THE SAME LAW AS DAY 325'S SLOW-START AND DAY 326'S
⭐     STAMPEDE. Third appearance.

⭐⭐ THRESHOLDS THAT WORK:
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ ERROR ***RATE***, NOT COUNT (5 failures out of 5 is
⭐     an outage; 5 out of 5,000 is Tuesday).
⭐   ⭐ A minimum request volume, ⭐⭐ OR ONE FAILURE AT 3 A.M.
⭐     WITH NO TRAFFIC TRIPS THE BREAKER.
⭐   ⭐ Per-dependency, ⭐ and often per-endpoint.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AND YOU MUST DEFINE THE FALLBACK ***BEFORE*** OPENING
⭐     THE BREAKER: stale cache, a default, a degraded response,
⭐     or an honest error. ⭐ A breaker with no fallback just
⭐     converts a slow failure into a fast one — ⭐ which is
⭐     still an improvement, but say which one you chose.

⭐⭐ ***BULKHEAD*** — isolate resources so one dependency cannot
⭐   consume them all.
⭐   ⭐ A separate bounded connection pool / semaphore per
⭐     downstream.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ WITHOUT IT: THE RECOMMENDATIONS SERVICE GETS SLOW AND
⭐     TAKES DOWN CHECKOUT, BECAUSE BOTH DRAW FROM THE SAME
⭐     WORKER POOL. ⭐ With it, recommendations degrade and
⭐     checkout is untouched.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ In Python: an `asyncio.Semaphore(20)` per dependency,
⭐     ⭐ or separate thread pools, ⭐⭐ OR SEPARATE PROCESSES
⭐     FOR TRULY CRITICAL PATHS.
```

```python
# ⭐ Bulkhead + breaker + timeout, composed. ~15 lines, and the
# ⭐ ORDER of the layers is the design.
class Dependency:
    def __init__(self, name: str, limit: int):
        self._sem = asyncio.Semaphore(limit)          # ⭐⭐ BULKHEAD
        self._breaker = Breaker(rate=0.5, min_calls=20, cooldown=30)

    async def call(self, fn, *a, fallback=None):
        if self._breaker.is_open:                     # ⭐⭐ FAIL FAST
            return fallback()                         # ⭐ decided ahead
        try:
            async with self._sem:                     # ⭐ bounded
                async with asyncio.timeout(3.0):      # ⭐ bounded
                    r = await fn(*a)
            self._breaker.record_success()
            return r
        except (TimeoutError, TransientError):
            self._breaker.record_failure()
            return fallback() if fallback else raise_
```

# Part 5 — ⭐⭐ Static stability, and graceful degradation

```
⭐⭐ ***STATIC STABILITY*** (the AWS term worth borrowing):
⭐   ***THE SYSTEM CONTINUES OPERATING CORRECTLY WHEN ITS
⭐      DEPENDENCIES ARE UNAVAILABLE, USING STATE IT ALREADY
⭐      HAS.***
⭐   ⭐ Availability zones keep serving when the control plane
⭐     is down, because instances don't need it to keep running.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE DESIGN QUESTION IT GENERATES: ***WHAT DOES MY
⭐     SERVICE NEED AT ***STEADY STATE*** THAT IT DOESN'T NEED
⭐     TO ***START***?*** ⭐ Anything on that list is a
⭐     dependency you can survive losing.

⭐⭐ EXAMPLES YOU CAN BUILD:
⭐   ⭐ Cache config/feature flags locally with a long TTL ⇒
⭐     the flag service can be down for an hour.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ SERVE STALE ON ERROR (Day 327's `stale-if-error`).
⭐   ⭐ Pre-provision capacity rather than relying on autoscaling
⭐     during an incident — ⭐⭐ THE CONTROL PLANE IS OFTEN THE
⭐     THING THAT IS BROKEN.
⭐   ⭐ Keep a local copy of the data needed for the critical
⭐     path, even if it is slightly stale.

⭐⭐ ***GRACEFUL DEGRADATION*** — decide the tiers in advance:
⭐   ⭐ Tier 1 (never degrade): auth, checkout, payment.
⭐   ⭐ Tier 2: search, listings — serve stale/cached.
⭐   ⭐ Tier 3 (drop silently): recommendations, related items,
⭐     "customers also bought", analytics beacons.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE PRODUCT CONVERSATION MUST HAPPEN BEFORE THE
⭐     INCIDENT. ⭐ At 3 a.m. nobody has the authority to decide
⭐     that recommendations can be blank, ⭐⭐ SO WITHOUT THE
⭐     PRIOR DECISION THE ONLY AVAILABLE ACTION IS TO LET
⭐     EVERYTHING FAIL EQUALLY.
```

# Part 6 — ⭐ Idempotency, dependency direction, and the failure domain

```
⭐⭐ THREE STRUCTURAL DEFENCES THAT BEAT ANY PATTERN:

⭐⭐ ① ***IDEMPOTENCY MAKES RETRIES SAFE***, and retries are how
⭐      you survive transient failure. ⭐ Day 335 is a resilience
⭐      day in disguise.

⭐⭐ ② ***DEPENDENCY DIRECTION.*** ⭐ The critical path must not
⭐      depend on the non-critical one.
⭐   ⭐ ❌ Checkout calls the recommendations service to log an
⭐      impression.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ NOW A NICE-TO-HAVE IS ON THE MONEY PATH. ⭐ Make it
⭐     an event (Day 333), fire-and-forget, bounded queue.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ DRAW THE DEPENDENCY GRAPH AND MARK THE CRITICAL
⭐     PATH IN RED. ⭐ Every red-to-non-red edge is a bug you
⭐     can see with your eyes.

⭐⭐ ③ ***FAILURE DOMAINS / CELLS.*** ⭐ Partition users into
⭐      independent cells, each with its own stack.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ A BAD DEPLOY OR A POISON REQUEST TAKES OUT 1/N OF
⭐     USERS INSTEAD OF ALL OF THEM. ⭐ Shuffle sharding takes
⭐     this further: assign each customer a ***random subset***
⭐     of nodes, so two customers rarely share the same full
⭐     set, ⭐⭐ AND ONE ABUSIVE CUSTOMER CANNOT TAKE DOWN ANY
⭐     OTHER CUSTOMER COMPLETELY.
```

# Part 7 — ⭐⭐ Recovery, which is where the second outage lives

```
⭐⭐ THE MOMENT AFTER YOU FIX THE FAULT IS THE MOST DANGEROUS
⭐   MOMENT.

⭐⭐ FOUR RECOVERY HAZARDS:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ COLD CACHE. ⭐ 100% miss on a database already
⭐      struggling. ⭐ Warm it, or ramp traffic (Day 326).
⭐   ② ⭐⭐ THE THUNDERING HERD OF RECONNECTS. ⭐ Every client
⭐      reconnects at once — ⭐ jitter reconnection, always.
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ THE BACKLOG SURGE. ⭐ An hour of queued messages
⭐      arrives at once and takes down the service that just
⭐      came back. ⭐ Rate-limit consumption during recovery.
⭐   ④ ⭐ RETRY STORMS from clients that have been failing for
⭐      an hour and have all their backoff timers about to
⭐      expire.

⭐⭐ ⇒ THE UNIFYING LAW, FOR THE THIRD TIME IN THIS STAGE:
⭐   ***RECOVERY MUST BE RAMPED, NEVER INSTANT.***
⭐   ⭐ Day 325's LB slow-start. ⭐ Day 326's cache warm-up.
⭐   ⭐ Day 339's breaker half-open. ⭐⭐ ONE LAW, THREE
⭐     APPEARANCES — SAY IT THAT WAY AND IT SOUNDS LIKE
⭐     UNDERSTANDING RATHER THAN A LIST.

⭐⭐ AND THE PRACTICE THAT PROVES ANY OF THIS: ⭐⭐ ***GAME
⭐   DAYS.*** ⭐ Kill a cache node in staging under load. Add
⭐   400 ms of latency to a dependency. Fill a disk. ⭐ Do it on
⭐   a Tuesday afternoon with everyone watching, ⭐⭐ BECAUSE
⭐   THE ALTERNATIVE IS DOING IT AT 3 A.M. WITH NOBODY WATCHING.
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ No timeout on an HTTP or DB call | One hung dependency consumes every worker. The service that dies is yours |
| ⭐ Timeout picked by feel | It should be p99.9 of the dependency. If you don't know it, that's the finding |
| ⭐⭐ Retrying non-retryable errors | Retrying a 400 is pure load with zero chance of success |
| ⭐⭐ Backoff without jitter | Every failed client retries in the same instant, forever, in a wave you built |
| ⭐⭐ Retry count instead of a retry budget | Three retries still means 3× load when everything fails |
| ⭐⭐ Retries at multiple layers | 3 × 3 × 3 is 27 requests for one user action |
| ⭐ Circuit breaker on error count | Five failures out of five thousand is Tuesday. Use a rate plus a minimum volume |
| ⭐⭐ Flooding a recovering dependency when the breaker closes | Ramp, don't restore. Same law as slow-start and cache warm-up |
| ⭐ Breaker with no defined fallback | You converted a slow failure into a fast one and called it resilience |
| ⭐⭐ Shared worker pool for all dependencies | Recommendations gets slow and takes down checkout |
| ⭐ Critical path depending on a nice-to-have | Draw the graph, mark critical in red, and every red-to-non-red edge is the bug |
| ⭐⭐ No plan for recovery | Cold cache, reconnect herd and backlog surge give you a second outage |
| ⭐ Degradation tiers decided during the incident | At 3 a.m. nobody has authority to blank recommendations, so everything fails equally |

## Interview questions

**1. "Walk me through a cascading failure."**
> ⭐ A cache node dies, hit ratio drops, database load rises forty times, queries slow, ⭐⭐ **app
> workers block so in-flight requests rise by Little's Law and the pool exhausts**, latency crosses the
> client timeout, clients retry and triple the arrival rate, health checks time out so the load
> balancer ejects healthy pods, and the survivors die faster. ⭐⭐ Three observations: **the original
> fault was survivable; the two mechanisms built to improve reliability — retries and health checks —
> did the most damage; and the restart is a second outage unless it's designed.**

**2. "How do you make retries safe?"**
> ⭐ Five constraints. Idempotent operations only. Retryable errors only — never a 400. Exponential
> backoff with **full jitter**, not backoff plus or minus ten percent, or every client comes back in
> the same instant. ⭐⭐ **A retry budget rather than a count** — retries capped at ten percent of total
> requests — because a count of three still means triple load when everything fails, and the budget is
> the rule that actually prevents the cascade. ⭐ And retries at exactly one layer, with the others
> turned off explicitly, or three layers of three becomes twenty-seven requests.

**3. "What does a circuit breaker actually protect?"**
> ⭐⭐ Both sides. It stops me waiting on something clearly broken, **and it stops me adding the load
> that's keeping them down.** ⭐ Thresholds on error *rate* with a minimum volume, or one failure at 3
> a.m. with no traffic trips it. ⭐⭐ Half-open probes a few requests and then **ramps rather than
> restores** — flooding a just-recovered service is the classic bug, and it's the same law as
> load-balancer slow-start and cache warm-up. ⭐ And I'd decide the fallback before opening it: stale
> cache, a default, or an honest error.

**4. "What's a bulkhead and why do you need one?"**
> ⭐ A bounded resource pool per dependency, so one slow downstream can't consume everything. ⭐⭐
> Without it, **the recommendations service gets slow and takes down checkout, because they draw from
> the same worker pool.** ⭐ In Python that's a semaphore per dependency, separate thread pools, or
> separate processes for genuinely critical paths. ⭐ It pairs with dependency direction: the money path
> must never call the nice-to-have synchronously.

**5. "What is static stability?"**
> ⭐ The system keeps operating correctly when its dependencies are unavailable, using state it already
> has. ⭐⭐ The design question it generates is useful: **what does my service need at steady state that
> it doesn't need to start?** ⭐ Locally cached feature flags with a long TTL, serving stale on error,
> pre-provisioned capacity instead of relying on autoscaling during an incident — ⭐⭐ because **the
> control plane is often the thing that's broken.**

**6. "You've fixed the fault. What now?"**
> ⭐⭐ Recovery is where the second outage lives. ⭐ Cold cache means a hundred percent miss rate on a
> database that's already struggling; every client reconnects simultaneously; an hour of queued
> messages arrives at once; and every client's backoff timer expires around the same moment. ⭐⭐ So:
> **ramp traffic, warm the cache, jitter reconnects, rate-limit the backlog drain** — recovery must be
> ramped, never instant. ⭐ And I'd have rehearsed it in a game day on a Tuesday afternoon rather than
> discovering it at 3 a.m.
