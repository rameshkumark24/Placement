# Day 381 🔑

**P-381** · 🔑 **IAM I** — policies, ⭐⭐ evaluation logic, and least privilege

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: IAM is a deny-by-default evaluation engine, not a permission list — and
> almost every IAM bug is someone reasoning about it as if it were a list, which is why "but I gave it
> S3 access" is the most common sentence in an AWS troubleshooting thread.**

---

# Part 1 — The four things a policy talks about

```
⭐⭐ EVERY AUTHORISATION QUESTION IN AWS IS THE SAME QUESTION:
⭐   ***MAY THIS PRINCIPAL PERFORM THIS ACTION ON THIS RESOURCE
⭐   UNDER THESE CONDITIONS?***

⭐ ① ⭐ PRINCIPAL — ⭐ who. ⭐ A user, ⭐⭐ A ROLE (WHICH IS WHAT
⭐    YOU ACTUALLY WANT — DAY 382), or an AWS service acting on
⭐    your behalf.
⭐ ② ⭐ ACTION — ⭐ `s3:GetObject`, `ec2:RunInstances`.
⭐    ⭐ `service:Operation`, and the service prefix is not always
⭐    the service name you say out loud.
⭐ ③ ⭐ RESOURCE — ⭐ an ARN.
⭐    `arn:aws:s3:::my-bucket/uploads/*`
⭐    ⭐⭐ AND THE TRAP THAT COSTS EVERYONE AN HOUR: THE BUCKET
⭐    AND THE OBJECTS ARE ***TWO DIFFERENT RESOURCES.***
⭐    ⭐ `arn:aws:s3:::my-bucket`   → ListBucket
⭐    ⭐ `arn:aws:s3:::my-bucket/*` → GetObject
⭐    ⭐⭐ A POLICY WITH ONLY THE SECOND CANNOT LIST. A POLICY WITH
⭐      ONLY THE FIRST CANNOT READ.
⭐ ④ ⭐⭐ ***CONDITION*** — ⭐ the part that turns a broad policy
⭐    into a narrow one, ⭐⭐ AND THE PART MOST PEOPLE NEVER USE.

⭐⭐ AND TWO PLACES A POLICY CAN LIVE, WHICH IS THE OTHER HALF:
⭐   ⭐ ***IDENTITY POLICY*** — attached to the principal.
⭐     "This role may read that bucket."
⭐   ⭐ ***RESOURCE POLICY*** — attached to the thing.
⭐     "This bucket may be read by that role."
⭐   ⭐⭐ THEY ARE NOT REDUNDANT. ⭐ A resource policy can grant to
⭐     a principal in ***another account***, ⭐ and it is the only
⭐     way to express "anyone in my organisation".
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ The evaluation algorithm, in order

> Learn this as an ordered list, not as a feeling. Every confusing IAM outcome is explained by one of
> these six steps.

```
⭐⭐ GIVEN A REQUEST, AWS EVALUATES:

⭐ ① ⭐⭐ ***EXPLICIT DENY ANYWHERE → DENY. FULL STOP.***
⭐    ⭐ Any policy at any level. ⭐⭐ NOTHING OVERRIDES IT — not
⭐    an admin policy, not the account root, not a resource
⭐    policy. ⭐ This is why a `Deny` in an SCP is the strongest
⭐    control in AWS.
⭐ ② ⭐ SERVICE CONTROL POLICY (organisation) must allow.
⭐    ⭐⭐ AN SCP NEVER ***GRANTS*** ANYTHING — IT ONLY BOUNDS.
⭐    ⭐ An account with AdministratorAccess and an SCP denying
⭐    `s3:Delete*` cannot delete objects. ⭐⭐ THIS IS THE
⭐    "PRODUCTION CANNOT BE DELETED BY ANYONE" CONTROL.
⭐ ③ ⭐ ALLOW must exist — from the identity policy ***or*** the
⭐    resource policy. ⭐⭐ EXCEPT CROSS-ACCOUNT, WHERE ***BOTH***
⭐    SIDES MUST ALLOW.
⭐ ④ ⭐ PERMISSION BOUNDARY (if set) must also allow.
⭐    ⭐ The boundary is a ceiling on a principal: ⭐⭐ IT LETS YOU
⭐    SAFELY DELEGATE POLICY-WRITING WITHOUT DELEGATING
⭐    PRIVILEGE ESCALATION.
⭐ ⑤ ⭐ SESSION POLICY (if the credentials came from AssumeRole
⭐    with one) must also allow.
⭐ ⑥ ⭐⭐ OTHERWISE → ***IMPLICIT DENY.*** ⭐ Default is no.

⭐⭐ ⇒ THE TWO FACTS THAT EXPLAIN 90% OF IAM CONFUSION:
⭐   ⭐⭐ ① ***AN EXPLICIT DENY BEATS EVERYTHING.***
⭐   ⭐⭐ ② ***CROSS-ACCOUNT NEEDS BOTH SIDES.*** ⭐ "I gave the
⭐      role permission and it still fails" is nearly always a
⭐      missing bucket policy, ⭐ or the reverse.
```

# Part 3 — Reading a policy properly

```json
{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    {
      "Sid": "ReadOwnPrefixOnly",
      "Effect": "Allow",
      "Action": ["s3:GetObject", "s3:PutObject"],
      "Resource": "arn:aws:s3:::uploads-prod/tenant/${aws:PrincipalTag/tenant}/*",
      "Condition": {
        "Bool":          {"aws:SecureTransport": "true"},
        "StringEquals":  {"s3:x-amz-server-side-encryption": "aws:kms"}
      }
    },
    {
      "Sid": "ListButOnlyOwnPrefix",
      "Effect": "Allow",
      "Action": "s3:ListBucket",
      "Resource": "arn:aws:s3:::uploads-prod",
      "Condition": {
        "StringLike": {"s3:prefix": "tenant/${aws:PrincipalTag/tenant}/*"}
      }
    }
  ]
}
```

```
⭐⭐ WHAT THAT POLICY DEMONSTRATES, AND WHY IT IS THE SHAPE TO
⭐   COPY:
⭐   ⭐ Two statements because ⭐⭐ BUCKET AND OBJECTS ARE
⭐     DIFFERENT RESOURCES — ⭐ the trap from Part 1, in code.
⭐   ⭐⭐ ***A VARIABLE IN THE RESOURCE ARN*** — `${aws:PrincipalTag/…}`
⭐     ⭐ so one policy serves every tenant, ⭐⭐ AND MULTI-TENANT
⭐     ISOLATION BECOMES AN IAM PROPERTY RATHER THAN AN
⭐     APPLICATION `WHERE` CLAUSE YOU MIGHT FORGET (DAY 306).
⭐   ⭐ `s3:prefix` on the ***list***, because otherwise the role
⭐     can enumerate every tenant's keys even if it cannot read
⭐     them — ⭐⭐ AND A KEY LIST IS OFTEN THE LEAK.
⭐   ⭐ `aws:SecureTransport` — ⭐ deny plaintext.
⭐   ⭐ An encryption condition on the ***write***, ⭐⭐ WHICH IS
⭐     HOW YOU ENFORCE ENCRYPTION RATHER THAN HOPE FOR IT.

⭐⭐ THE CONDITION KEYS WORTH MEMORISING:
⭐   ⭐ `aws:PrincipalOrgID` ⭐⭐ — THE BEST SINGLE CONDITION IN
⭐     AWS. ⭐ On a resource policy it means "anyone in my org and
⭐     nobody else", ⭐ and it survives new accounts.
⭐   ⭐ `aws:SourceIp`, `aws:SourceVpce`, `aws:SourceArn`
⭐   ⭐ `aws:RequestTag/*`, `aws:ResourceTag/*` — tag-based
⭐   ⭐ `aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent` — ⭐⭐ AND ITS TRAP: ROLE
⭐     SESSIONS FROM SERVICES DO NOT HAVE IT, ⭐ so a blanket
⭐     `Deny if not MFA` locks out your automation.
⭐   ⭐⭐ AND THE ONE THAT BITES: ***`StringEquals` ON A KEY THAT
⭐     IS ABSENT EVALUATES TO FALSE, SO AN ALLOW SILENTLY DOES
⭐     NOT APPLY*** — ⭐ use `…IfExists` when the key is optional,
⭐     ⭐⭐ AND NEVER USE `…IfExists` IN A `DENY`, BECAUSE THAT IS
⭐     A DENY THAT STOPS APPLYING WHEN THE KEY IS MISSING.
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ Least privilege that actually happens

```
⭐⭐ WHY "WRITE THE PERFECT POLICY UP FRONT" FAILS: ***NOBODY
⭐   KNOWS THE ACTION LIST.*** ⭐ A simple upload flow touches
⭐   `s3:PutObject`, `s3:AbortMultipartUpload`, `kms:GenerateDataKey`
⭐   and `kms:Decrypt` — ⭐ and you will discover the last two at
⭐   3 a.m. because the error says AccessDenied on KMS, not S3.

⭐⭐ ⇒ SO LEAST PRIVILEGE IS ***A RATCHET YOU TIGHTEN WITH
⭐   EVIDENCE, NOT A DOCUMENT YOU WRITE ONCE.***

⭐ THE WORKFLOW THAT WORKS:
⭐   ① ⭐ Start deliberately broad ***in dev only***, scoped to
⭐      the resources it will really touch.
⭐   ② ⭐ Exercise every path, including the error paths.
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ READ ***CLOUDTRAIL*** FOR THE ACTUAL ACTIONS. ⭐ Or let
⭐      IAM Access Analyzer generate a policy from the trail —
⭐      ⭐⭐ IT WRITES THE ACTION LIST FOR YOU FROM REAL USE.
⭐   ④ ⭐ Tighten. ⭐ Deploy to staging. ⭐ Watch for AccessDenied.
⭐   ⑤ ⭐ Use ***Access Advisor*** ("services last accessed") on
⭐      every role quarterly ⭐⭐ AND DELETE PERMISSIONS UNUSED FOR
⭐      90 DAYS — ⭐ this is the step that keeps it tight after
⭐      the first month, ⭐⭐ AND IT IS THE ONE EVERYONE SKIPS.

⭐⭐ AND THE MEASUREMENT THAT MAKES IT REAL: ***COUNT YOUR
⭐   POLICIES CONTAINING `"Resource": "*"` AND DRIVE THE NUMBER
⭐   DOWN.*** ⭐ It is a metric, it is trivially queryable, and it
⭐   correlates with actual blast radius better than any audit.
```

# Part 5 — The traps, ranked by how much damage they do

```
⭐ ① ⭐⭐ ***`iam:PassRole` — THE ESCALATION NOBODY READS.***
⭐    ⭐ If a principal may pass a role to a service it may
⭐    create, ⭐⭐ IT EFFECTIVELY ***HAS*** THAT ROLE. ⭐ "Can
⭐    launch EC2" + "can pass the admin role" = admin, via one
⭐    instance with a user-data script.
⭐    ⭐⭐ ⇒ ALWAYS CONSTRAIN: `PassRole` ON SPECIFIC ROLE ARNs,
⭐      WITH `iam:PassedToService` PINNED.
⭐ ② ⭐⭐ WILDCARD IN ***ACTION AND RESOURCE TOGETHER.***
⭐    ⭐ `"Action": "s3:*"` on one bucket is defensible.
⭐    ⭐⭐ `"Action": "s3:*", "Resource": "*"` IS EVERY BUCKET IN
⭐    THE ACCOUNT INCLUDING THE BACKUP ONE.
⭐ ③ ⭐ AWS-MANAGED POLICIES AS A DEFAULT. ⭐ `AmazonS3FullAccess`
⭐    is convenient and roughly 100× broader than any real need.
⭐    ⭐⭐ USE THEM TO ***LEARN THE ACTION NAMES***, NOT TO GRANT.
⭐ ④ ⭐ `NotAction` — ⭐⭐ IT INVERTS YOUR MENTAL MODEL AND IS
⭐    ALMOST ALWAYS A MISTAKE IN AN `Allow`. ⭐ "Allow everything
⭐    except IAM" also allows every service launched next year.
⭐ ⑤ ⭐ POLICY SIZE LIMITS (6,144 chars for a managed policy)
⭐    ⭐⭐ PUSH TIRED PEOPLE TOWARD WILDCARDS. ⭐ Split into
⭐    several policies instead; ⭐ a role can hold ten.
⭐ ⑥ ⭐⭐ `"Principal": "*"` IN A TRUST OR BUCKET POLICY WITH NO
⭐    CONDITION. ⭐ That is the public-bucket headline. ⭐ If you
⭐    genuinely need broad, ⭐⭐ USE `aws:PrincipalOrgID`.
⭐ ⑦ ⭐ A `Deny` with a condition you got backwards — ⭐⭐ THE
⭐    LOCKOUT THAT NEEDS ROOT TO UNDO. ⭐ Test denies in a
⭐    sandbox account, ⭐ and never on the policy protecting your
⭐    own access.
```

# Part 6 — The Python half: how boto3 finds credentials

```python
# ⭐⭐ THE RESOLUTION ORDER botocore ACTUALLY USES — memorise it,
# ⭐ because "works on my laptop, AccessDenied in the container"
# ⭐ is always a difference in *which* of these won.
#
#   1. explicit kwargs to boto3.client(...)      ⭐⭐ AVOID
#   2. env: AWS_ACCESS_KEY_ID / SECRET / SESSION_TOKEN
#   3. ~/.aws/credentials  (shared credentials file)
#   4. ~/.aws/config       (profiles, SSO, role_arn + source)
#   5. ⭐ container credentials  (ECS task role)
#      AWS_CONTAINER_CREDENTIALS_RELATIVE_URI
#   6. ⭐ IMDS               (EC2 instance role)   ⭐ Day 382

import boto3, botocore

sess = boto3.Session()                    # ⭐ no keys anywhere
creds = sess.get_credentials()
print(creds.method)                       # ⭐⭐ THE DEBUG LINE:
# 'env' | 'shared-credentials-file' | 'container-role' | 'iam-role'
print(sess.client("sts").get_caller_identity()["Arn"])
# ⭐⭐ ⇒ THE TWO LINES THAT END 90% OF "WHY IS THIS DENIED":
# ⭐   WHICH CREDENTIAL SOURCE WON, AND WHO AM I ACTUALLY?
```

```
⭐⭐ AND THE BEST DEBUGGING TOOL IN AWS IS THE ERROR MESSAGE —
⭐   ⭐ people paste "AccessDenied" and stop reading. ⭐ The full
⭐   text names all three parts of the question:

⭐   User: arn:aws:sts::111:assumed-role/app-task/abc  ← ⭐ WHO
⭐   is not authorized to perform: s3:PutObject        ← ⭐ WHAT
⭐   on resource: arn:aws:s3:::uploads-prod/x.jpg      ← ⭐ WHICH
⭐   because no identity-based policy allows            ← ⭐⭐ WHY

⭐⭐ ⇒ THAT LAST CLAUSE IS THE DIAGNOSIS. ⭐ "No identity-based
⭐   policy allows" = your role is missing it. ⭐ "With an
⭐   explicit deny in a service control policy" = stop editing
⭐   the role, ⭐⭐ THE ORG IS BLOCKING YOU AND NO ROLE CHANGE WILL
⭐   EVER FIX IT.
```

# Part 7 — Testing a policy before it reaches production

```bash
# ⭐ Does this role actually have it? — no deploy required
aws iam simulate-principal-policy \
  --policy-source-arn arn:aws:iam::111:role/app-task \
  --action-names s3:PutObject kms:GenerateDataKey \
  --resource-arns arn:aws:s3:::uploads-prod/tenant/a/x.jpg

# ⭐⭐ AND THE ONE THAT FINDS THE REAL PROBLEM:
# ⭐ what in this account is reachable from outside it?
aws accessanalyzer list-findings --analyzer-arn ...
```

```
⭐⭐ PUT THE SIMULATOR IN CI FOR YOUR CRITICAL PATHS. ⭐ Six
⭐   assertions — ⭐ "the app role CAN write uploads", ⭐⭐ "THE APP
⭐   ROLE ***CANNOT*** READ THE BACKUP BUCKET" — ⭐ turn IAM into
⭐   something with tests, ⭐⭐ AND THE NEGATIVE ASSERTIONS ARE THE
⭐   VALUABLE ONES, BECAUSE NOTHING ELSE IN YOUR PIPELINE EVER
⭐   NOTICES A POLICY GETTING BROADER.
```

# 💬 Interview

**1. ⭐⭐ "Walk me through how AWS decides whether a request is allowed."**
> ⭐ Deny by default, then in order: ⭐⭐ **an explicit deny anywhere wins immediately and nothing
> overrides it**; then the SCP must allow, and note that an SCP only bounds, it never grants; then an
> allow must exist in an identity policy or a resource policy, ⭐⭐ **except cross-account, which needs
> both sides**; then a permission boundary if set must allow; then a session policy if present must
> allow; otherwise implicit deny. ⭐ The two facts that explain nearly all confusion are the explicit
> deny beating everything, and cross-account needing both halves.

**2. "Why does my role have `s3:GetObject` and still can't list the bucket?"**
> ⭐⭐ **Because the bucket and the objects are two different resources.** ⭐ `arn:aws:s3:::b` is the
> bucket, which is what `ListBucket` acts on; `arn:aws:s3:::b/*` is the objects, which is what
> `GetObject` acts on. ⭐ You need two statements. ⭐ And the good version puts an `s3:prefix`
> condition on the list statement, ⭐⭐ **because otherwise the role can enumerate every key it can't
> read — and in a multi-tenant bucket the key list is itself the leak.**

**3. ⭐⭐ "What's `iam:PassRole` and why do you care?"**
> ⭐ It's the permission to hand an existing role to a service. ⭐⭐ **It's the most under-appreciated
> escalation path in AWS: if a principal can launch EC2 and can pass an admin role, it is admin — one
> instance with a user-data script and it's done.** ⭐ So `PassRole` never gets a wildcard; it's pinned
> to specific role ARNs with an `iam:PassedToService` condition. ⭐ It's the first thing I look for
> when reviewing a policy that looks harmless.

**4. "How do you actually achieve least privilege?"**
> ⭐ Not by writing the perfect policy up front, because nobody knows the action list — a simple S3
> upload also needs `kms:GenerateDataKey` and `kms:Decrypt`, and you find that out in production.
> ⭐⭐ **It's a ratchet you tighten with evidence:** start scoped-but-broad in dev, exercise every path
> including errors, read CloudTrail or let Access Analyzer generate the policy from real usage,
> tighten, watch staging for AccessDenied. ⭐⭐ **Then the part everyone skips — Access Advisor
> quarterly, delete anything unused for 90 days.** ⭐ And I track one number: policies containing
> `Resource: "*"`, driven down over time.

**5. "A policy allows the action but the request is still denied. Where do you look?"**
> ⭐⭐ **First I read the whole error message rather than the word AccessDenied** — it names the
> principal, the action, the resource and the reason, ⭐ and the reason clause is the diagnosis. ⭐ If
> it says an explicit deny in an SCP, no role change will ever fix it and I go to the org. ⭐ If it's
> cross-account, I check both sides. ⭐ Then a permission boundary or session policy, ⭐ and I confirm
> *which* identity I actually am with `get_caller_identity`, ⭐⭐ **because "works locally, denied in
> the container" is nearly always a different credential source winning, not a policy problem.**

**6. "What's a condition key you'd reach for often?"**
> ⭐⭐ **`aws:PrincipalOrgID`** — on a resource policy it means "anyone in my organisation and nobody
> else", and it keeps working as accounts are added. ⭐ It's how you get broad internal sharing without
> a `Principal: "*"`. ⭐ And one trap worth stating: ⭐⭐ **a `StringEquals` on an absent key evaluates
> false, so an Allow silently doesn't apply** — you use `…IfExists` for optional keys, ⭐ but never in
> a `Deny`, because that produces a deny that stops applying exactly when the key is missing.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes.** Lead with the reframe: **IAM isn't a permission list, it's a deny-by-default
evaluation engine** — which is why "but I gave it S3 access" is the most common sentence in an AWS
thread. Then give the algorithm in order, because ordering is the whole thing: **explicit deny
anywhere wins and nothing overrides it; SCPs bound but never grant; an allow must exist in an identity
or resource policy, and cross-account needs both; then boundary, then session policy; otherwise
implicit deny.** Give the concrete trap that proves you've used it: **the bucket and the objects are
different resources, so `GetObject` on `bucket/*` doesn't let you list, and the good policy puts an
`s3:prefix` condition on the list statement because a key list in a multi-tenant bucket is itself the
leak.** Then the escalation people miss: **`PassRole` — if you can launch EC2 and pass an admin role,
you are admin**, so it's pinned to specific ARNs with `PassedToService`. Then the honest process:
**least privilege is a ratchet you tighten with evidence, not a document you write once** — CloudTrail
or Access Analyzer generates the action list, Access Advisor prunes it quarterly, **and I track the
count of policies containing `Resource: "*"` as a number that goes down.** Close on debugging: **read
the whole AccessDenied message — the reason clause tells you whether to edit the role or stop touching
it because the org is denying you.**
