# Day 486 🏢

**P-486** · 🏢 **Company prep and the loop** — ⭐⭐ nobody scores you; a committee does

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: you are not being evaluated by the person in the room — you are generating
> written evidence that a committee will read without you present, so the goal in every round is to hand
> your interviewer sentences they can quote in a debrief they write forty minutes later.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ How the decision is actually made

```
⭐ ***THE MENTAL MODEL MOST CANDIDATES HAVE:*** ⭐ four people
⭐   each decide yes or no, and majority wins.
⭐ ***WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENS:***
⭐   ⭐ ① each interviewer writes a ***structured debrief***
⭐     within an hour — ⭐⭐ USUALLY BEFORE THEY SPEAK TO ANYONE
⭐     ELSE, DELIBERATELY, TO AVOID CONTAMINATION;
⭐   ⭐ ② each is assigned ***specific signals*** to probe, not
⭐     "general impressions" (Part 2);
⭐   ⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ THE HIRING MANAGER OR A COMMITTEE READS THE WRITTEN
⭐     PACKET ⭐ — ⭐⭐ MOST OF THEM NEVER MET YOU;
⭐   ⭐ ④ the debate is about ***evidence***, ⭐ and a strong
⭐     claim with no supporting example loses to a weaker claim
⭐     with one.

⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ ***THE PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCE, AND IT CHANGES HOW YOU
⭐   ANSWER:*** ⭐ your interviewer is a ***witness***, not a
⭐   judge.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ THEY CAN ONLY WRITE DOWN WHAT YOU SAID. ⭐ A brilliant
⭐     thought you had and did not articulate does not exist in
⭐     the packet.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ SO GIVE THEM ***QUOTABLE SENTENCES***: ⭐ "recall went
⭐     from 0.62 to 0.91 and two-thirds of that was free" ⭐ is
⭐     something they can transcribe. ⭐⭐ "I IMPROVED THE
⭐     RETRIEVAL QUALITY" IS NOT.

⭐ ***AND THE ASYMMETRY WORTH KNOWING:***
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ ONE STRONG "NO HIRE" USUALLY OUTWEIGHS THREE WEAK
⭐     "HIRES" ⭐ at most companies, because the cost of a bad hire
⭐     is modelled as far higher than a missed good one.
⭐   ⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ THERE IS NO ROUND YOU CAN SAFELY WRITE OFF, ⭐ and
⭐     the round you were least worried about is the usual place
⭐     people lose.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ The four loop archetypes

```
⭐ ① ⭐⭐ ***BIG TECH*** — ⭐ 4–6 rounds, standardised, ⭐ heavy
⭐   DSA, ⭐ formal levelling, ⭐ a bar raiser, ⭐ committee
⭐   decision, ⭐⭐ AND OFTEN NO TEAM ATTACHED UNTIL AFTER THE
⭐   OFFER.
⭐   ⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ OPTIMISE: DSA SPEED AND STRUCTURED COMMUNICATION.
⭐     ⭐ Your project matters less than at any other archetype.

⭐ ② ⭐⭐ ***SCALE-UP (SERIES B–D)*** — ⭐ 3–5 rounds, ⭐ heavy
⭐   project deep dive, ⭐ a practical take-home or pairing
⭐   session, ⭐⭐ HIRING MANAGER HAS REAL DISCRETION.
⭐   ⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ OPTIMISE: THE DEEP DIVE AND "CAN YOU SHIP". ⭐ This
⭐     is the archetype where Days 467–471 pay most.

⭐ ③ ***EARLY STARTUP*** — ⭐ 2–3 rounds, ⭐ founder-led,
⭐   ⭐⭐ UNSTRUCTURED, ⭐ decided on breadth and enthusiasm.
⭐   ⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ OPTIMISE: SHOW YOU CAN OWN SOMETHING END TO END ⭐ and
⭐     ⭐⭐ ASK HARD QUESTIONS ABOUT RUNWAY AND WHY THEY ARE
⭐     HIRING — ⭐ they respect it, and you need the answer.

⭐ ④ ***CONSULTANCY / SERVICE*** — ⭐ client-facing communication
⭐   weighted heavily, ⭐ breadth over depth, ⭐⭐ AND OFTEN AN
⭐   APTITUDE OR PSYCHOMETRIC ROUND THAT IS PURE PATTERN
⭐   PRACTICE.
⭐   ⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ OPTIMISE: EXPLAINING TECHNICAL WORK TO A
⭐     NON-ENGINEER.

⭐ ⭐⭐ ⇒ PREPARATION IS NOT UNIFORM. ⭐ A month optimising DSA for
⭐   a scale-up whose decision hangs on the deep dive is a month
⭐   spent on the wrong axis, ⭐⭐ AND THE FIX COSTS ONE EMAIL TO
⭐   THE RECRUITER.
```

# Part 4 — Researching the company usefully

```
⭐ ***THE USELESS VERSION:*** ⭐ reading the About page and
⭐   saying "I'm passionate about your mission." ⭐⭐ EVERY
⭐   CANDIDATE DOES THIS AND IT CARRIES NO INFORMATION.

⭐ ***THE USEFUL VERSION — 45 MINUTES, FOUR SOURCES:***
⭐   ⭐ ① ⭐⭐ THEIR ***ENGINEERING BLOG***. ⭐ What did they build
⭐     and what went wrong? ⭐⭐ REFERENCING A SPECIFIC POST — AND
⭐     DISAGREEING WITH PART OF IT THOUGHTFULLY — IS THE
⭐     STRONGEST PREPARATION SIGNAL AVAILABLE.
⭐   ⭐ ② ***the job description, read as a symptom.*** ⭐ "Must
⭐     be comfortable with ambiguity" ⇒ ⭐⭐ NOBODY KNOWS WHAT THE
⭐     ROADMAP IS. ⭐ "Improve reliability of existing services"
⭐     ⇒ ⭐⭐ SOMETHING IS ON FIRE. ⭐ Both are fine; ⭐⭐ BOTH ARE
⭐     THINGS TO ASK ABOUT.
⭐   ⭐ ③ ***their actual product.*** ⭐ Use it. ⭐⭐ FIND ONE THING
⭐     THAT IS SLOW OR BROKEN. ⭐ "I noticed search takes about
⭐     two seconds on a cold load — is that a retrieval problem
⭐     or a rendering one?" ⭐⭐ THAT SINGLE QUESTION SEPARATES
⭐     YOU FROM EVERY OTHER CANDIDATE THAT DAY.
⭐   ⭐ ④ ⭐⭐ THEIR SCALE AND STAGE. ⭐ Headcount, funding,
⭐     customers. ⭐ It tells you which archetype you are in and
⭐     what "senior" means there (D487).

⭐ ***AND THE FOUR QUESTIONS TO ASK*** (Day 460), ⭐ which are
⭐   simultaneously good questions and a diagnostic:
⭐   ⭐ "What does your eval set look like?"
⭐   ⭐ "What's your cost per request?"
⭐   ⭐ "How do you roll back a prompt?"
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ "WHAT HAPPENS WHEN THE PROVIDER IS DOWN?"
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ PLUS ONE HUMAN ONE, ⭐ which does more work than people
⭐     expect: ⭐ "What surprised you most about working here?"
```

⭐   stop.

⭐ ***AND THE REJECTION PROTOCOL — ⭐⭐ WHICH IS A SKILL, NOT A
⭐   MOOD:***

⭐     particular round or signal that was weakest? ⭐⭐ IT WOULD

⭐     with no feedback after four good rounds is often a

# 💬 Interview

**1. "How do you prepare for a specific company?"**

⭐ First I ask the recruiter what the rounds are and what each focuses on, because preparation isn't
uniform — ⭐⭐ a month optimising DSA for a scale-up whose decision hangs on the project deep dive is a
month on the wrong axis, and the fix costs one email. ⭐ Then forty-five minutes on four sources: their
engineering blog, the job description read as a *symptom* rather than a wish list, their actual product,
and their scale. ⭐⭐ "Must be comfortable with ambiguity" usually means nobody knows the roadmap;
"improve reliability of existing services" usually means something is on fire. ⭐ Both are fine, and both
are things I'd ask about.

**2. "What's the highest-value thing in that research?"**

⭐ Using the product and finding one thing that's slow or broken. ⭐⭐ "I noticed search takes about two
seconds on a cold load — is that retrieval or rendering?" separates me from every other candidate that
day, because it proves I engaged with what they actually built rather than with their About page. ⭐ The
close second is referencing a specific engineering blog post *and* disagreeing with part of it
thoughtfully. ⭐⭐ Agreement is cheap; a considered disagreement shows I read it properly and have my own
model.

**3. "Who actually decides?"**

⭐ Not the person in the room — that's the thing that changed how I answer. ⭐⭐ Each interviewer writes a
structured debrief within an hour, usually before speaking to anyone else, and a hiring manager or
committee reads the written packet. ⭐ Most of them never met me. ⭐⭐ So my interviewer is a witness
rather than a judge: they can only write down what I actually said, and a brilliant thought I had but
didn't articulate doesn't exist in the packet. ⭐ Which is why I give them quotable sentences — "recall
went from 0.62 to 0.91 and two-thirds of that was free" is transcribable; "I improved retrieval quality"
isn't.

**4. "Does one bad round sink you?"**

⭐ Often, yes, and it's worth knowing rather than hoping. ⭐⭐ At most companies one strong "no hire"
outweighs three weak "hires", because the cost of a bad hire is modelled as much higher than a missed
good one. ⭐ So there's no round I can safely write off, ⭐⭐ and the round people usually lose is the one
they weren't worried about — the behavioural, or the "easy" coding round where they solved it silently
and gave the interviewer nothing to write down.

**5. "What do you do after an interview?"**

⭐ Within two hours I write an after-action record: the questions verbatim, the exact moment I ran out of
depth, what I wish I'd said, which answers made them lean in, and any red flags on their side. ⭐⭐ Memory
decays fast and selectively — I'll remember the round that went worst and forget why. ⭐ After three loops
that document is worth more than any interview book, because it records what *I* am actually asked and
where *I* actually stop. ⭐⭐ The red flags column matters too, and I write it before any offer arrives —
because an offer makes people generous about things they noticed and dismissed.

**6. "How do you handle rejection?"**

⭐ As a process, not a mood. ⭐⭐ I ask once, specifically: "was there a particular round or signal that was
weakest?" ⭐ Most companies decline, some don't, and one specific answer is worth ten generic ones. ⭐ I
never argue — the industry is small and referral networks outlive any single loop. ⭐⭐ I ask about the
cooldown, because reapplying in a year with a visibly better answer to last time's weak round is a strong
story. ⭐ And I separate signal from noise: ⭐⭐ a rejection with no feedback after four good rounds is
frequently a headcount decision rather than a verdict on me, and treating it as a verdict is both wrong
and expensive.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

⭐⭐ **Two minutes, out loud: "why do you want to work here?"** — the question everyone answers badly.

Don't say passion. **"Three things, and they're specific. First, I read your post on migrating the
ingestion pipeline to CDC — the part about the replication slot filling the disk during a consumer outage
is exactly the failure mode I'd have expected, and I'd be interested to know whether you ended up
alerting on slot lag or capping retention. Second, I use the product, and search takes about two seconds
on a cold load — I'd want to know whether that's retrieval or rendering, because those are completely
different problems and it's the sort of thing I'd enjoy owning."**

Then the honest third reason, which is about fit rather than flattery. **"And third — the job description
says 'improve reliability of existing services', which usually means something is on fire and someone
has decided to do something about it. That's the kind of work I'm best at and it's what my last year has
been: measuring things nobody had measured, then fixing the two that mattered. I'd rather join something
that has a known problem than something that hasn't found its problems yet."**

Notice what that answer does: **it proves you read their engineering writing, used their product, and
read the JD as information rather than as a list of requirements to echo back.** It also gives your
interviewer three quotable, specific sentences for the debrief they'll write in forty minutes — which is
the actual audience.
