# Day 184

**L-184** · ⚖️ **What actually transfers** — ⭐⭐ the twelve invariants under every web framework

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** SYNTHESIS

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: fifty-five days of framework material reduces to about twelve ideas, and
> those twelve are true in Rails, Spring, Express and Go — which is why they are the ones worth being
> able to derive rather than recall.**
>
> ⭐ Today is not new material. ⭐⭐ It is the compression, and it is the day that makes the rest
> portable. **If you can derive these twelve, you can pick up any framework in a week.**

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ The four about data

```
⭐⭐ 1. THE TRANSACTION BOUNDARY IS AN OPERATION, NOT A CALL.
⭐   ⭐ Per-`save()` ⇒ nothing is atomic. Per-request ⇒ locks held across I/O.
⭐   ⭐⭐ THE SERVICE OWNS IT, because only the service knows what "the
⭐        operation" means.
⭐   ⭐ SEEN IN: Day 139 (repositories), Day 164 (place_order), Day 172
⭐        (Django autocommit).
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ THE COROLLARY THAT DOES ALL THE WORK: **NEVER HOLD A LOCK ACROSS
⭐        I/O.** ⭐ It is why the hold exists, why `ATOMIC_REQUESTS` is wrong,
⭐        and why one slow provider does not have to serialise your service.

⭐⭐ 2. PUT THE GUARANTEE WHERE A BUG CANNOT REACH IT.
⭐   ⭐ a convention fails the first time someone is in a hurry; ⭐ a required
⭐        argument fails at import; ⭐⭐ a constraint cannot fail at all.
⭐   ⭐ SEEN IN: `CHECK (sold <= total)` · `UniqueConstraint` for idempotency ·
⭐        RLS · keyword-only `tenant_id` · `lazy="raise"`.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AND ITS TEST: "IF I FORGET, WHAT HAPPENS?" ⭐ Zero rows or an error
⭐        is a good design; someone else's rows is not.

⭐⭐ 3. AN ORM IS A CACHE OF ROWS WITH A LIFETIME, AND IT LIES ABOUT COST.
⭐   ⭐ attribute access can be a round trip ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE N+1 IS THE DEFAULT
⭐        OUTCOME, and it is invisible to a slow-query log because each query
⭐        is fast.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ SO EVERY ORM NEEDS THREE THINGS: eager loading declared at the
⭐        query, a way to make an unexpected load LOUD, and a query-count
⭐        ceiling in CI.
⭐   ⭐ SEEN IN: `selectinload`/`joinedload` · `select_related`/
⭐        `prefetch_related` · `assertNumQueries` — ⭐ and the join-vs-second-
⭐        query rule DERIVED from row multiplication, in both.

⭐⭐ 4. TWO VERSIONS OF YOUR CODE ARE ALWAYS LIVE.
⭐   ⭐⭐ EXPAND/CONTRACT IS THEREFORE THE ANSWER TO FOUR DIFFERENT QUESTIONS:
⭐        schema migrations, cache keys, event payloads, API versions.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ ONE SHAPE: add the new, write both, migrate readers, verify nothing
⭐        uses the old, remove. ⭐ Recognising that four problems have one
⭐        answer is worth more than remembering four answers.
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ The four about failure

```
⭐⭐ 5. EVERY REMOTE CALL HAS THREE OUTCOMES: YES, NO, AND NO ANSWER YET.
⭐   ⭐ a timeout means UNKNOWN, not failed. ⭐⭐ Systems that survive MODEL
⭐        THE THIRD OUTCOME (`payment_unknown`) and have a job that resolves it.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ Recording either of the other two is a real failure with real money.

⭐⭐ 6. AT-LEAST-ONCE IS THE ONLY DELIVERY GUARANTEE THAT EXISTS.
⭐   ⭐ the ack and the side effect are two operations; the process can die
⭐        between them. ⭐⭐ NO QUEUE, BROKER OR SCHEDULER REMOVES THAT WINDOW.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ SO IDEMPOTENCY IS NOT A FEATURE, IT IS THE PRICE OF ADMISSION —
⭐        and the phrase is **"exactly-once processing is at-least-once
⭐        delivery plus an idempotent consumer."**
⭐   ⭐ SEEN IN: idempotency keys · the outbox relay · Celery `acks_late` ·
⭐        webhook `event_id` · the scheduled-job period key.

⭐⭐ 7. TWO WRITES TO TWO SYSTEMS IS A BUG UNTIL PROVEN OTHERWISE.
⭐   ⭐ commit-then-enqueue loses the message; ⭐ enqueue-then-commit is worse
⭐        because the worker reads before you commit and it HIDES as
⭐        intermittent noise.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ MAKE THE TWO THINGS THAT MUST AGREE INTO ONE ATOMIC WRITE. ⭐ That
⭐        is the outbox, the idempotency claim, and the object-before-row
⭐        ordering for uploads — ⭐⭐ one idea, three names.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AND WHEN YOU CANNOT: CHOOSE THE ORDER THAT LEAVES GARBAGE, NOT A
⭐        DANGLING REFERENCE. ⭐ Orphaned data is cheap; a broken link is an
⭐        incident.

⭐⭐ 8. FAILURES ARE CORRELATED, AND YOUR RETRIES ARE LOAD.
⭐   ⭐ everything fails at once, so ⭐⭐ un-jittered backoff is a synchronised
⭐        herd aimed at something already struggling.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AND THE SHAPE TO NAME: **METASTABLE FAILURE** — the system stays
⭐        down after the cause is fixed, because the retries are now the load.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ jitter, retry BUDGETS, retries at one layer only, breakers outside
⭐        retries, and shedding load fast rather than queueing it all.
⭐   ⭐ SEEN IN: Days 142, 143, 152, 153, 158, 159 — ⭐⭐ correlated timing is
⭐        the recurring bug of the entire stage.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ The four about boundaries

```
⭐⭐ 9. AUTHORISATION BELONGS IN THE QUERY, NOT AFTER THE FETCH.
⭐   ⭐ fetch-then-check is correct until a second call site forgets, because
⭐        the fetch line still works perfectly alone.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ SCOPE IN THE `WHERE` CLAUSE: a wrong id returns nothing, so it is a
⭐        404, so you do not confirm existence — ⭐ and there is NO code path
⭐        that can return another user's row.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AND THE HIERARCHY: authentication asks WHO · roles ask MAY YOUR
⭐        KIND · **object-level asks MAY YOU DO THIS TO THIS ONE** — ⭐ and no
⭐        amount of role checking ever answers the third.
⭐   ⭐ SEEN IN: Day 118 · Day 165 · ⭐⭐ Day 176's `has_object_permission`
⭐        skipping `list`, which is that mistake with a framework's name on it.

⭐⭐ 10. THE OUTPUT SCHEMA IS A SECURITY CONTROL.
⭐   ⭐ `fields = "__all__"` and returning the ORM object directly both mean
⭐        ⭐⭐ A COLUMN SOMEONE ADDS IN SIX MONTHS APPEARS IN YOUR API WITH NO
⭐        REVIEW.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ separate input and output types, and ⭐⭐ ASSERT THE ABSENCE:
⭐        `assert "password_hash" not in body`. ⭐ A control you do not test is
⭐        a convention.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AND THE SAME IDEA INBOUND: mass assignment. `user` or `tenant_id`
⭐        accepted from a request body is a breach, not a bug.

⭐⭐ 11. EVERY BOUNDARY NEEDS A TIMEOUT, A SIZE LIMIT AND A CAP.
⭐   ⭐ a client chooses how much of you it consumes: body size, page size,
⭐        request rate, concurrency, query cost.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ AND THE CAP MUST BE ON **OCCUPANCY**, NOT ONLY ON RATE — ⭐ ten
⭐        60-second requests consume more than a thousand 5 ms ones, and no
⭐        rate limit sees it.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ WITHOUT A TIMEOUT, ONE SLOW DEPENDENCY CONSUMES EVERY WORKER
⭐        INCLUDING YOUR HEALTH CHECK — and then the orchestrator kills you.

⭐⭐ 12. MIDDLEWARE RUNS BEFORE ROUTING, SO PROTECTION GOES ON THE ROUTE.
⭐   ⭐ middleware cannot know the endpoint, so it decides by path prefix —
⭐        ⭐⭐ which is how a new route under a public prefix ships unprotected.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ default-deny at the router, an explicit greppable public list, and
⭐        ⭐⭐ A TEST THAT WALKS THE ROUTE TABLE. ⭐ True in FastAPI, Django,
⭐        Express and Spring.
```

# Part 4 — ⭐ Three that are about you, not the code

```
⭐⭐ THE OPERATIONAL INVARIANTS. ⭐ They are not framework features and they
⭐   decide whether you can run the thing:

⭐   ⭐⭐ **A DEAD SERVICE LOOKS PERFECT ON EVERY METRIC.** No errors, no
⭐        latency, no saturation. ⭐ So one of your alerts must be on ABSENCE,
⭐        and one of your panels must be BUSINESS metrics — ⭐⭐ because a bug
⭐        that makes every purchase return a fast, correct 422 is invisible to
⭐        everything technical.
⭐
⭐   ⭐⭐ **THE THING PROTECTING YOU CAN BECOME THE OUTAGE.** ⭐ A liveness
⭐        probe that checks the database restarts your fleet over a blip. A
⭐        rate limiter with no socket timeout hangs every request. ⭐⭐ A
⭐        readiness check on a third party removes all your capacity. ⭐ Ask of
⭐        every safety mechanism: what happens when IT fails?
⭐
⭐   ⭐⭐ **MEASURE BEFORE YOU BELIEVE — INCLUDING YOUR MEASUREMENT.** ⭐ A
⭐        closed-model load test cannot overload you; coordinated omission
⭐        makes p99 wrong by an order of magnitude in the flattering
⭐        direction; ⭐ a generator above 70% CPU is measuring itself.
⭐        ⭐⭐ Little's Law answers most capacity questions before any tool runs.
```

# Part 5 — ⭐⭐ Using this

```
⭐⭐ THE TEST OF WHETHER YOU OWN THESE: **PICK A FRAMEWORK YOU HAVE NEVER
⭐   USED AND ANSWER THE TWELVE QUESTIONS FOR IT.** ⭐ Rails, Spring Boot,
⭐   Express, Go's `net/http`. ⭐⭐ Every one has an answer, and finding it
⭐   takes an afternoon instead of a month — which IS the transferable skill.

⭐   ⭐ Where is the transaction boundary, and what is the default?
⭐   ⭐ What is the eager-loading mechanism, and how do I make a lazy load loud?
⭐   ⭐ How do I express a constraint the code cannot bypass?
⭐   ⭐ What does the router do before my handler runs?
⭐   ⭐ How do I scope a query so forgetting returns nothing?
⭐   ⭐ Where does the output schema live, and can a new column leak?
⭐   ⭐ What is the default timeout? (⭐⭐ Usually: none.)
⭐   ⭐ What is the migration story, and does it detect renames?
⭐   ⭐ How do I assert a query count in a test?
⭐   ⭐ What is the background-work story, and what is its delivery guarantee?
⭐
⭐⭐ AND THE INTERVIEW VALUE, WHICH IS THE REAL POINT:
⭐   ⭐ "I haven't used Rails, but I'd want to know where the transaction
⭐     boundary sits by default, whether the ORM makes lazy loads loud, and
⭐     what happens to an unprotected route" ⭐⭐ IS A BETTER ANSWER THAN
⭐     RECITING FEATURES OF A FRAMEWORK YOU HAVE USED.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ It demonstrates that you learned PRINCIPLES from tools, which is
⭐        exactly what fifty-five days were for.

⭐⭐ THE TWELVE, IN ONE BREATH — say these until they are automatic:
⭐   ⭐ 1. The service owns the transaction, and never holds a lock across I/O.
⭐   ⭐ 2. Put the guarantee where a bug cannot reach it.
⭐   ⭐ 3. The N+1 is the ORM's default outcome; make it loud and count queries.
⭐   ⭐ 4. Two versions are always live ⇒ expand/contract.
⭐   ⭐ 5. A timeout means "I don't know" ⇒ model the third outcome.
⭐   ⭐ 6. At-least-once is the only guarantee ⇒ idempotency is the price.
⭐   ⭐ 7. Two writes to two systems ⇒ make them one atomic write.
⭐   ⭐ 8. Failures are correlated ⇒ jitter, budgets, shed rather than queue.
⭐   ⭐ 9. Authorisation lives in the `WHERE` clause.
⭐   ⭐ 10. The output schema is a security control — assert the absence.
⭐   ⭐ 11. Every boundary needs a timeout, a limit and an occupancy cap.
⭐   ⭐ 12. Middleware runs before routing ⇒ protect the route, and test the
⭐        route table.
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Learning frameworks as feature lists | The features change; the twelve invariants don't. |
| Believing the framework prevents these bugs | None of them do. The race, the idempotency and the dual write are yours. |
| Treating expand/contract as a migration technique | It's the answer to schemas, cache keys, event payloads and API versions. |
| Thinking a queue gives exactly-once | Nothing does. The ack and the effect are two operations. |
| Checking ownership after the fetch | Correct until a second call site forgets, because the fetch works alone. |
| Returning ORM objects from an API | A column added in six months appears with no review. |
| Testing that a control exists but not that it works | Assert the *absence* of the field, not the presence of the serializer. |
| Capping rate but not occupancy | Ten slow requests outweigh a thousand fast ones, invisibly. |
| Protection in middleware | It runs before routing, so it can only match paths. |
| Alerting only on errors | A dead service has no errors. Alert on absence. |
| Not asking what happens when a safety mechanism fails | Liveness probes, readiness checks and limiters all become outages. |
| Trusting a load test you didn't scrutinise | Closed model and coordinated omission both flatter you. |

## Interview questions

**Q: You've used FastAPI and Django. What did you actually learn?**

> That about eighty percent of the difficulty was the same in both, and that the shared part
> compresses to roughly a dozen ideas. The service owns the transaction and never holds a lock across
> I/O. Put the guarantee where a bug can't reach it — a constraint, not a convention. The N+1 is the
> ORM's default outcome and it's invisible to a slow-query log, so you need eager loading, a way to
> make lazy loads loud, and a query-count ceiling in CI. Two versions of your code are always live,
> so expand/contract answers migrations, cache keys, event payloads and API versions with one shape.
> A timeout means "I don't know", so you model the third outcome. At-least-once is the only delivery
> guarantee, so idempotency is the price of admission. Those are true in Rails and Spring too, which
> is why they're the ones worth being able to derive rather than recall.

**Q: What does "put the guarantee where a bug can't reach it" mean?**

> That controls have a hierarchy of reliability. A convention — "always add the tenant filter" —
> fails the first time someone's in a hurry, and a reviewer can't see a clause that isn't there. A
> required keyword-only argument is better, because forgetting it is a `TypeError` at collection
> time. A database constraint is best, because it can't fail at all: `CHECK (sold <= total)` means my
> locking can have a bug and the database still refuses to oversell. Row-level security is the same
> idea for tenancy — the guarantee moves out of my discipline and into Postgres, and it fails
> *closed*, returning zero rows rather than someone else's. The test I apply is: if I forget, what
> happens? Zero rows or an error is a good design. Someone else's rows is not.

**Q: What's the most under-appreciated failure mode you learned?**

> That failures are correlated and your own retries become the load. When a dependency degrades it
> isn't one request failing, it's all of them, so un-jittered exponential backoff retries everything
> in lockstep at exactly the same instants — a synchronised herd aimed at something already
> struggling. And the shape that follows is metastable failure: the system stays down after the
> original cause is fixed, because the retries *are* the load now. That's what turns a five-minute
> blip into a four-hour incident. The defences are jitter, a retry budget as a percentage of base
> traffic rather than per call, retries at exactly one layer, breakers outside retries, and shedding
> load fast rather than queueing it — because a queue that grows faster than you drain it means you
> do a hundred percent of the work and deliver zero percent of the value.

**Q: You're asked to work in a framework you've never used. How do you start?**

> By answering about ten questions rather than reading a tutorial. Where's the transaction boundary
> and what's the default — because if it's autocommit, my multi-step operation isn't atomic no matter
> how it reads. What's the eager-loading mechanism and can I make a lazy load raise. How do I express
> a constraint the code can't bypass. What does the router do before my handler runs, because that
> tells me whether protection can live in middleware. How do I scope a query so forgetting returns
> nothing. Where does the output schema live and can a new column leak into it. What's the default
> timeout — usually none. And what's the background-work story and its delivery guarantee. Every
> framework has an answer to all of those, and finding them takes an afternoon rather than a month.
> That's the actual transferable skill: I learned principles from the tools, not the tools.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Three minutes: "What did you learn building backends?"**

Do not list frameworks. Say you built the same endpoint in two of them and found the difficulty was
mostly the same, then give the compression — about a dozen ideas that hold everywhere. Pick four and
give each in one line with the bug it prevents: the service owns the transaction and never holds a
lock across I/O; put the guarantee where a bug can't reach it; a timeout means "I don't know", so
model the third outcome; at-least-once is the only guarantee, so idempotency is the price of
admission. Then the operational one, because it's the one that sounds like experience: **a dead
service looks perfect on every technical metric, so one alert has to be on absence.** Close on the
transfer claim and make it concrete — **give me a framework I've never used and I'll ask ten
questions about it, and most of them are about defaults nobody documents.**
