# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ Timeouts: the hierarchy, and the ones you forgot

```
⭐⭐ WITHOUT A TIMEOUT, A SLOW DEPENDENCY IS NOT A SLOW ENDPOINT — IT IS AN
⭐   OUTAGE, AND HERE IS THE MECHANISM PEOPLE SKIP:

⭐   ⭐ payments is slow: 30 s instead of 200 ms.
⭐   ⭐ each request holds a worker for 30 s (Day 134's threadpool: 40 slots).
⭐        ↓
⭐   ⭐⭐ AT 2 rps ON THAT ONE ROUTE, ALL 40 SLOTS ARE OCCUPIED IN 20 SECONDS.
⭐        ↓
⭐   ⭐⭐ EVERY OTHER ENDPOINT NOW HANGS TOO — including `/healthz`.
⭐        ↓
⭐   ⭐ liveness fails ⇒ the pod is killed ⇒ its load moves to the others ⇒
⭐        ⭐⭐ THEY DIE FASTER. Day 149's cascade, triggered by a MISSING NUMBER.
⭐
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ ONE SLOW DEPENDENCY TOOK DOWN AN ENTIRE SERVICE THAT MOSTLY DOES
⭐        NOT USE IT. A timeout is not politeness — ⭐ it is the thing that
⭐        keeps a local failure local.
```

```python
# ⭐⭐ FOUR TIMEOUTS, NOT ONE — httpx makes the distinction explicit
timeout = httpx.Timeout(
    connect=2.0,     # ⭐ TCP + TLS. Short: a healthy host connects in ms.
    read=5.0,        # ⭐⭐ between BYTES, not total — see the trap below
    write=5.0,
    pool=1.0,        # ⭐⭐ waiting for a free CONNECTION from your own pool.
)                    #    ⭐ The one everyone omits, and it hides saturation
                     #    as "the dependency is slow" (Day 148).
client = httpx.AsyncClient(timeout=timeout,
                           limits=httpx.Limits(max_connections=100))
```

```
⭐⭐ THE TRAP: A READ TIMEOUT IS PER-CHUNK, NOT TOTAL.
⭐   ⭐ A server dribbling one byte every 4 seconds never trips a 5-second
⭐     read timeout — ⭐⭐ AND HOLDS YOUR WORKER FOREVER. ⭐ That is slowloris
⭐     (Day 103) aimed at you as a CLIENT.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ ALSO SET A TOTAL DEADLINE: `asyncio.timeout(10)` around the call.

⭐⭐ THE HIERARCHY — each layer's timeout must be SHORTER than its caller's:
⭐   ⭐ browser/client   30 s
⭐   ⭐ CDN / LB         25 s
⭐   ⭐ your API         20 s   ⭐⭐ (uvicorn/gunicorn worker timeout)
⭐   ⭐ your DB query    5 s    ⭐ `statement_timeout` — Day 138
⭐   ⭐ downstream HTTP  3 s
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ IF AN INNER TIMEOUT EXCEEDS AN OUTER ONE, THE OUTER GIVES UP FIRST
⭐        AND THE INNER WORK CONTINUES — ⭐ you burn a worker on a result
⭐        nobody will read, ⭐⭐ and you never see the inner error, so your logs
⭐        show a mystery 504 with no cause.

⭐⭐ AND THE FIVE TIMEOUTS PEOPLE FORGET ENTIRELY:
⭐   ⭐ DB CONNECTION ACQUIRE (pool wait) — Day 135
⭐   ⭐ DB STATEMENT (`statement_timeout`) — ⭐⭐ without it one bad query
⭐        holds a connection for an hour
⭐   ⭐ REDIS `socket_timeout` — ⭐ default is often NONE, and a hung Redis
⭐        blocks your whole cache path (Day 143)
⭐   ⭐ CELERY `task_time_limit` — Day 142
⭐   ⭐⭐ THE OVERALL REQUEST DEADLINE, propagated. ⭐ If 18 of your 20 seconds
⭐        are gone, ⭐⭐ DO NOT START A 5-SECOND CALL. Pass the remaining budget
⭐        down and fail immediately — that is deadline propagation, and it is
⭐        what stops work continuing after the client has left.
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ Retries: when they help, and when they are the outage

```
⭐⭐ THE RULE THAT COMES FIRST, BECAUSE IT IS A CORRECTNESS RULE:
⭐   ⭐ ONLY RETRY WHAT IS SAFE TO REPEAT.
⭐   ⭐ GET/PUT/DELETE are idempotent by contract. ⭐⭐ POST IS NOT.
⭐   ⭐⭐ AND THE TIMEOUT CASE IS THE WHOLE PROBLEM: a timeout does NOT mean
⭐        "it didn't happen". ⭐ It means YOU DON'T KNOW. The charge may have
⭐        succeeded and the response was lost.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ SO RETRYING A TIMED-OUT POST CAN DOUBLE-CHARGE. ⭐ The fix is
⭐        Day 122's: an idempotency key, so the retry is SAFE BY CONSTRUCTION
⭐        rather than by hope.
⭐
⭐   ⭐⭐ RETRY: connect errors, 502/503/504, `Retry-After`, connection reset.
⭐   ⭐⭐ NEVER RETRY: 400, 401, 403, 404, 422. ⭐ A malformed request stays
⭐        malformed — ⭐⭐ retrying a 4xx is pure load with a zero success rate.
⭐   ⭐ 429: retry, but ⭐⭐ ONLY after `Retry-After`. Ignoring it and retrying
⭐        immediately is how you get IP-banned.
```

```
⭐⭐ THE RETRY STORM — THE FAILURE MODE THAT MAKES RETRIES DANGEROUS:

⭐   ⭐ downstream degrades: 50% of requests fail.
⭐   ⭐ every client retries 3×.
⭐        ↓
⭐   ⭐⭐ OFFERED LOAD RISES TO ~2.5× AT THE MOMENT CAPACITY FELL.
⭐        ↓
⭐   ⭐ more failures ⇒ more retries ⇒ ⭐⭐ 100% FAILURE, AND IT STAYS DOWN
⭐        EVEN AFTER THE ORIGINAL CAUSE IS GONE — because your retries are now
⭐        the load. ⭐ THAT is a metastable failure: the system cannot recover
⭐        without shedding load, even though nothing is broken any more.
⭐
⭐⭐ THREE DEFENCES, AND YOU NEED ALL THREE:
⭐  1. ⭐ EXPONENTIAL BACKOFF **WITH JITTER** (Day 142). ⭐⭐ Without jitter,
⭐       every client retries at the same instant — a synchronised herd.
⭐  2. ⭐⭐ A RETRY BUDGET — cap retries at ~10% of BASE TRAFFIC, not per call.
⭐       ⭐ "3 attempts each" is unbounded in aggregate; a budget is not.
⭐       ⭐⭐ THIS IS THE ONE ALMOST NOBODY IMPLEMENTS, AND IT IS THE ONLY ONE
⭐       THAT BOUNDS TOTAL LOAD.
⭐  3. ⭐⭐ RETRY AT ONE LAYER ONLY. ⭐ 3 retries in the SDK × 3 in your client
⭐       × 3 in the gateway = ⭐⭐ 27 REQUESTS FOR ONE LOGICAL CALL. ⭐ Retry
⭐       amplification is multiplicative through a call stack, and every layer
⭐       thinks it is being helpful.
```

```python
@retry(
    retry=retry_if_exception_type((httpx.ConnectError, httpx.ReadTimeout)),
    wait=wait_exponential_jitter(initial=0.1, max=5),   # ⭐⭐ jitter
    stop=stop_after_attempt(3),
    reraise=True,
)
async def fetch_rate(client, cur: str) -> Decimal: ...
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Circuit breakers: failing fast on purpose

```
⭐⭐ THE PROBLEM RETRIES CANNOT SOLVE: IF A DEPENDENCY IS DOWN, EVERY CALL
⭐   WAITS THE FULL TIMEOUT BEFORE FAILING. ⭐ 3 s × every request = your
⭐   workers are all blocked waiting for something you ALREADY KNOW is broken.

⭐   ⭐⭐ CLOSED ──(failure rate > 50% over N)──> OPEN
⭐        │                                        │
⭐        │                                        │ (after 30 s)
⭐        └──(probe succeeds)── HALF-OPEN <────────┘
⭐                                 │
⭐                                 └──(probe fails)──> OPEN
⭐
⭐   ⭐ CLOSED     — normal. Count failures.
⭐   ⭐⭐ OPEN      — FAIL IMMEDIATELY WITHOUT CALLING. ⭐ Microseconds, not
⭐        3 seconds. ⭐⭐ This is the entire value: you stop spending YOUR
⭐        capacity on a dependency that cannot serve you, and you stop adding
⭐        load to something that is trying to recover.
⭐   ⭐ HALF-OPEN  — let ONE request through. ⭐⭐ Success ⇒ close. Failure ⇒
⭐        open again. ⭐ Letting them ALL through at once re-kills a service
⭐        that had just come back — which is why half-open exists at all.

⭐⭐ THE FOUR THINGS PEOPLE GET WRONG:
⭐  1. ⭐⭐ ONE BREAKER PER DEPENDENCY, NOT PER SERVICE. A shared breaker means
⭐       a broken search backend opens the circuit for payments.
⭐  2. ⭐⭐ TRIP ON A *RATE*, NOT A COUNT. "5 failures" trips on a quiet
⭐       service after five unlucky minutes. ⭐ Use a percentage over a window,
⭐       ⭐⭐ AND a minimum volume — 100% of 2 requests is not a signal.
⭐  3. ⭐ DO NOT COUNT 4xx AS FAILURES. ⭐⭐ A 404 means the dependency is
⭐       WORKING. Counting client errors opens the breaker during normal
⭐       operation.
⭐  4. ⭐⭐ IN A MULTI-PROCESS SERVICE, THE BREAKER IS PER PROCESS (Day 111) —
⭐       ⭐ so N workers each learn independently. ⭐ That is usually fine and
⭐       occasionally not; a shared breaker in Redis costs a round trip on
⭐       every call, ⭐⭐ which is a poor trade for the thing that is supposed
⭐       to make you fast.
```

```
⭐⭐ AND THE QUESTION THE BREAKER FORCES YOU TO ANSWER — this is the real
⭐   design work, not the state machine:
⭐   ⭐ "WHAT DO I RETURN WHEN THE CIRCUIT IS OPEN?"
⭐        ⭐ stale cache (Day 143)          ⭐⭐ usually the best answer
⭐        ⭐ a default / empty result       ⭐ recommendations → show popular
⭐        ⭐ a queued promise               ⭐ accept the write, process later
⭐        ⭐ 503 + `Retry-After`            ⭐ honest, when nothing else works
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ GRACEFUL DEGRADATION IS A PRODUCT DECISION, NOT A TECHNICAL ONE.
⭐        ⭐ "Should checkout work without the recommendation service?" has an
⭐        obvious answer, and only the product owner can give it for the ten
⭐        cases where it is not obvious. ⭐⭐ Ask before the incident.
```

# Part 4 — ⭐ Bulkheads, load shedding, and fallbacks

```
⭐⭐ BULKHEADS — isolate resources so one dependency cannot consume all of
⭐   them. ⭐ Named after ship compartments: a hull breach floods ONE.
⭐   ⭐ a SEPARATE connection pool per dependency (not one shared pool)
⭐   ⭐ a SEPARATE thread pool / semaphore per downstream
⭐   ⭐ separate Celery queues by latency class (Day 142)
⭐   ⭐⭐ separate DEPLOYMENTS for critical vs non-critical endpoints
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ WITHOUT A BULKHEAD, PART 1'S CASCADE IS UNAVOIDABLE — a shared
⭐        worker pool means EVERY dependency competes for the SAME slots, so
⭐        one slow one starves all of them. ⭐ A `Semaphore(20)` around the
⭐        payments client caps its blast radius at 20 workers.

⭐⭐ LOAD SHEDDING — the counterintuitive one:
⭐   ⭐ when overloaded, ⭐⭐ REJECT REQUESTS *FAST* WITH 503 RATHER THAN
⭐     QUEUEING THEM ALL AND SERVING EVERY ONE SLOWLY.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ WHY: a queue that grows faster than you drain it means EVERY
⭐        request eventually times out — ⭐ so you do 100% of the work and
⭐        deliver 0% of the value. ⭐⭐ Serving 70% and rejecting 30% quickly
⭐        beats failing 100% slowly, every time.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Shed by PRIORITY: reject bulk/analytics before checkout.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ AND DROP REQUESTS THE CLIENT HAS ALREADY ABANDONED. Check
⭐        `await request.is_disconnected()` before expensive work — ⭐ work for
⭐        a client that left is pure waste that deepens the overload.

⭐ ⭐⭐ THE FALLBACK RULE PEOPLE LEARN THE HARD WAY: A FALLBACK PATH THAT IS
⭐   NEVER EXERCISED DOES NOT WORK. ⭐ It is code that runs only during an
⭐   incident, which is the worst possible time to discover a typo in it.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ TEST IT: a scheduled chaos test, or a flag that forces the fallback
⭐        for 1% of traffic permanently. ⭐⭐ That 1% is your proof.
```

# Part 5 — ⭐⭐ Putting it together, in order

```
⭐⭐ THE ORDER OF DEFENCES AROUND A REMOTE CALL, OUTERMOST FIRST — and the
⭐   ORDER MATTERS, so be able to justify it:

⭐   ┌─ TIMEOUT (per attempt + total deadline)   ⭐⭐ FIRST. Without it nothing
⭐   │                                              else can bound anything.
⭐   ├─ BULKHEAD (semaphore)                     ⭐ caps concurrent blast radius
⭐   ├─ CIRCUIT BREAKER                          ⭐⭐ OUTSIDE the retry, so an
⭐   │                                              open circuit prevents ALL
⭐   │                                              attempts, not just the first
⭐   ├─ RETRY (backoff + jitter + budget)        ⭐ only for safe operations
⭐   └─ FALLBACK (stale cache / default / 503)   ⭐ what the user actually gets
⭐
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE COMMON MISTAKE IS BREAKER INSIDE RETRY: then each retry checks
⭐        a breaker that is already open and you still make three calls'
⭐        worth of decisions. ⭐ Breaker outside means one fast rejection.

⭐⭐ AND THE THREE PRINCIPLES THAT GENERATE ALL OF THIS:
⭐  1. ⭐⭐ EVERY REMOTE CALL HAS THREE OUTCOMES: yes, no, and NO ANSWER YET.
⭐       ⭐ The third is where timeouts, idempotency and reconciliation live.
⭐  2. ⭐⭐ FAILURES ARE CORRELATED, NOT INDEPENDENT. ⭐ Everything fails at
⭐       once, which is why jitter, budgets and load shedding exist — ⭐⭐ your
⭐       intuition from single-request thinking is wrong at fleet scale.
⭐  3. ⭐⭐ THE SYSTEM MUST BE ABLE TO RECOVER WITHOUT HUMAN ACTION. ⭐ A
⭐       system that stays down after the cause is fixed — because retries
⭐       became the load — ⭐⭐ is metastable, and that is the failure mode
⭐       that turns a 5-minute blip into a 4-hour incident.
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| No timeout on an HTTP call | One slow dependency consumes every worker, including the health check. |
| One `timeout=5` for everything | Connect, read, write and **pool** are different failures. Pool waits hide your own saturation. |
| Relying on the read timeout alone | It's per-chunk. A dribbling server holds you forever. Add a total deadline. |
| Inner timeout longer than outer | The outer gives up first, the inner keeps burning a worker, and you never see the cause. |
| No `statement_timeout` | One bad query holds a connection for an hour. |
| Redis with no `socket_timeout` | Often unset by default; a hung Redis blocks the whole cache path. |
| Retrying a timed-out POST | A timeout means "unknown", not "didn't happen" — you can double-charge. Use an idempotency key. |
| Retrying 4xx | Malformed stays malformed. Pure load, zero success rate. |
| Ignoring `Retry-After` on a 429 | The fastest route to an IP ban. |
| Backoff without jitter | Synchronised herd; you keep the failing service down. |
| Per-call retry limits with no budget | Unbounded in aggregate. Cap retries at ~10% of base traffic. |
| Retries at three layers | 3×3×3 = 27 requests per logical call. Retry at one layer only. |
| One circuit breaker for all dependencies | A broken search backend opens the circuit for payments. |
| Tripping on a failure count | Five failures on a quiet service is noise. Use a rate, with a minimum volume. |
| Counting 4xx toward the breaker | A 404 means the dependency is working. |
| Circuit breaker inside the retry | An open circuit should stop all attempts, not be re-checked three times. |
| No answer for "what do we return when open?" | The state machine is the easy part; degradation is a product decision. |
| Shared pool for all dependencies | No bulkhead, so Part 1's cascade is unavoidable. |
| Queueing everything under overload | You do 100% of the work and deliver 0% of the value. Shed fast. |
| An untested fallback path | Code that runs only during incidents, discovered broken during one. |

## Interview questions

**Q: Why is a missing timeout an outage rather than a slow endpoint?**

> Because workers are a fixed resource. If payments goes from 200 ms to 30 seconds, each request on
> that route holds a worker for 30 seconds; at two requests per second, all forty threadpool slots
> are gone in twenty seconds. Now every endpoint hangs, including the health check — so liveness
> fails, the pod gets killed, its traffic moves to the remaining pods and they saturate faster. One
> slow dependency takes down a service that mostly doesn't use it. A timeout is what keeps a local
> failure local. And I'd set four of them, not one: connect, read, write and pool — pool being the
> one people omit, which matters because waiting for your own connection looks exactly like the
> dependency being slow.

**Q: Is it safe to retry?**

> Only if the operation is safe to repeat, and the hard case is a timeout — a timeout doesn't mean it
> didn't happen, it means you don't know. The charge may have succeeded and the response was lost, so
> retrying a timed-out POST can double-charge. That's what idempotency keys are for: they make the
> retry safe by construction instead of by hope. Beyond correctness there's the retry storm: if a
> downstream degrades to fifty percent failure and everyone retries three times, offered load rises
> to about two and a half times at the exact moment capacity dropped, and the system can stay down
> after the original cause is gone because the retries *are* the load. That's a metastable failure. So
> I need jitter, a retry budget capped as a percentage of base traffic rather than per-call, and
> retries at exactly one layer — three in the SDK times three in the client times three in the
> gateway is twenty-seven requests for one logical call.

**Q: What does a circuit breaker do that retries don't?**

> It stops you from spending your own capacity discovering something you already know. If a
> dependency is down, every call waits the full timeout before failing, so all your workers are
> blocked on a known-broken service. An open circuit fails in microseconds instead of three seconds,
> which protects you and also stops you adding load to something trying to recover. Half-open is the
> subtle part: when the timer expires you let exactly *one* request through, because letting them all
> through at once re-kills a service that had just come back. The mistakes I'd watch for are one
> breaker shared across dependencies, tripping on a count rather than a rate with a minimum volume,
> and counting 4xx as failures — a 404 means the dependency is working fine.

**Q: What do you return when the circuit is open?**

> That's the actual design question, and it's a product decision rather than a technical one. Options
> are a stale cache value, which is usually best; a sensible default — recommendations fall back to
> "popular items"; accepting the write into a queue and processing it later; or an honest 503 with
> `Retry-After`. What matters is asking "should checkout still work if recommendations are down?"
> *before* the incident, because during one you'll get the answer wrong. And I'd make sure the
> fallback is actually exercised — a path that only runs during incidents is a path discovered broken
> during an incident, so I'd force it for one percent of traffic permanently as proof.

**Q: Your service is overloaded. Do you queue requests or reject them?**

> Reject, quickly, and by priority. If the queue grows faster than you drain it, every request
> eventually exceeds its client's timeout, so you do a hundred percent of the work and deliver zero
> percent of the value — plus you've burned the capacity that could have served the requests you
> could actually finish. Serving seventy percent and rejecting thirty percent fast beats failing a
> hundred percent slowly. I'd shed bulk and analytics traffic before checkout, and I'd also check
> whether the client has already disconnected before doing expensive work, because work for a client
> that left is pure waste that deepens the overload.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes: "How do you stop one failing dependency from taking down your service?"**

Start with the cascade, concretely — slow dependency, saturated workers, failing health check, dead
pod, more load on the survivors — because the mechanism is what makes the rest sound necessary. Then
walk the defences in nesting order and justify each one's position: timeouts first because nothing
else bounds anything without them, a bulkhead to cap blast radius, the breaker *outside* the retry so
an open circuit stops all attempts, retries only for safe operations with jitter and a budget, and a
fallback at the end. Then raise the counterintuitive pair: **retries are how a degraded dependency
becomes a total outage**, and **shedding load fast beats serving everything slowly**. Close on the
three-outcome principle — yes, no, and no answer yet — and note that the third outcome is where
idempotency keys earn their keep.
