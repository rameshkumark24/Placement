# Day 450 🔏

**P-450** · 🔏 **Data privacy** — ⭐⭐ your traces are the most sensitive store you own

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: an LLM feature scatters your users' data across seven stores you did not
> design as data stores, and the worst of them is observability — full prompts, longer retention than
> your database, and read access for the whole engineering team.**

```

⭐   LEGAL CONVERSATION.***

⭐ ① ***THE PROVIDER'S API*** — ⭐ leaves your network. ⭐ Part 3.
⭐ ② ***THE PROVIDER'S ABUSE-MONITORING LOGS*** — ⭐⭐ RETAINED

⭐   days. ⭐ A separate question from training. ⭐ Part 3.
⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ ***YOUR TRACES.*** ⭐ The full prompt — which means the

⭐   USERS ANYWHERE IN YOUR SYSTEM. ⭐ Part 2.

⭐ ⑦ ***PROMPT AND ANSWER CACHES*** — ⭐⭐ INCLUDING THE
⭐   PROVIDER'S PROMPT CACHE, WHICH HOLDS YOUR CONTEXT SERVER
⭐   SIDE FOR ITS TTL.

⭐⭐ AND THE ONE THAT CATCHES PEOPLE OUT: ⭐ ***A DELETION
⭐   REQUEST MUST REACH ALL SEVEN.*** ⭐ Most implementations

⭐   HONESTLY BELIEVES THE DATA IS GONE.

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ Why observability is the worst of the seven

```
⭐ ***THINK ABOUT WHAT A TRACE CONTAINS*** for one support
⭐   conversation:
⭐   ⭐ the user's message — ⭐ possibly a medical detail, a
⭐     financial problem, a complaint about a colleague;
⭐   ⭐ ***every retrieved chunk*** — ⭐⭐ SO DOCUMENTS THE
⭐     ENGINEER READING THE TRACE MAY HAVE NO RIGHT TO SEE;
⭐   ⭐ the memory block — ⭐ accumulated personal facts;
⭐   ⭐ tool results — ⭐ account balances, addresses, order
⭐     history.

⭐ ***NOW COMPARE THE CONTROLS ON EACH SIDE:***
⭐  ┌──────────────────┬─────────────────┬──────────────────┐
⭐  │                  │ PRIMARY DATABASE│ TRACE STORE      │
⭐  ├──────────────────┼─────────────────┼──────────────────┤
⭐  │ access control   │ ⭐ RLS, roles,   │ ⭐⭐ "ENGINEERING" │
⭐  │                  │ audited         │ — everyone       │
⭐  │ retention        │ ⭐ policy-driven │ ⭐⭐ 30–90 DAYS OR │
⭐  │                  │                 │ FOREVER, UNSET   │
⭐  │ encryption/field │ ⭐ often         │ ⭐ rarely         │
⭐  │ deletion path    │ ⭐ exists        │ ⭐⭐ USUALLY NONE  │
⭐  │ third party?     │ ⭐ no            │ ⭐⭐ OFTEN A SAAS  │
⭐  └──────────────────┴─────────────────┴──────────────────┘

⭐⭐ ⇒ YOU BUILT A SECOND COPY OF YOUR MOST SENSITIVE DATA WITH
⭐   WEAKER CONTROLS, AND YOU DID IT WHILE IMPROVING
⭐   OBSERVABILITY, WHICH IS A GOOD THING TO BE DOING. ⭐ That is
⭐   why it goes unnoticed.

⭐ ***THE FIVE FIXES:***
⭐   ⭐ ① ⭐⭐ REDACT ON THE WAY ***IN***, NOT ON QUERY — ⭐ once
⭐     it is written, it is written.
⭐   ⭐ ② store ***references*** to retrieved chunks (doc ids +
⭐     hashes), ⭐⭐ NOT THE CHUNK TEXT — ⭐ you keep full
⭐     debuggability and drop most of the sensitivity;
⭐   ⭐ ③ short retention by default — ⭐ 7–14 days for full
⭐     payloads, ⭐ longer for ***metrics only***;
⭐   ⭐ ④ ⭐⭐ ACCESS CONTROL AND AUDIT ON TRACE READS. ⭐ "Who
⭐     read this customer's conversation?" must be answerable.
⭐   ⭐ ⑤ a deletion path keyed by user id, ⭐⭐ TESTED, NOT
⭐     ASSUMED.
```

# Part 3 — What the provider terms actually say

```
⭐ ***THREE SEPARATE QUESTIONS PEOPLE COLLAPSE INTO ONE:***

⭐ ① ***"DO YOU TRAIN ON MY DATA?"***
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ ON ENTERPRISE/BUSINESS API TIERS THE MAJOR PROVIDERS
⭐     DEFAULT TO ***NO***. ⭐ On consumer chat products the
⭐     default often differs. ⭐⭐ THE API AND THE CHAT PRODUCT
⭐     ARE DIFFERENT PRODUCTS WITH DIFFERENT TERMS — ⭐ people
⭐     quote one and use the other.

⭐ ② ***"DO YOU RETAIN IT AT ALL?"*** ⭐⭐ USUALLY YES, BRIEFLY,
⭐   FOR ABUSE MONITORING — ⭐ commonly ~30 days.
⭐   ⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ ZERO-DATA-RETENTION IS TYPICALLY A ***SEPARATE
⭐     AGREEMENT YOU ASK FOR***, not a default. ⭐ If you handle
⭐     regulated data, this is the question to ask on the first
⭐     call.

⭐ ③ ***"WHERE IS IT PROCESSED?"*** ⭐ Residency and cross-border
⭐   transfer. ⭐⭐ CHECK THE ***SUBPROCESSOR LIST***, NOT JUST THE
⭐   PROVIDER — ⭐ your provider has providers, and your DPA has
⭐   to cover the chain.

⭐ ***AND THE ONES TO CHECK BEFORE SIGNING:***
⭐   ⭐ a ***DPA***, and whether they will sign yours;
⭐   ⭐ SOC 2 / ISO 27001, and ⭐⭐ WHETHER IT COVERS THE SPECIFIC
⭐     API YOU USE;
⭐   ⭐ breach notification timelines;
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ WHAT HAPPENS TO YOUR FINE-TUNED WEIGHTS IF YOU LEAVE
⭐     (Day 451);
⭐   ⭐ whether prompt caching changes any of the above.
```

# Part 4 — Redaction, and its honest limits

```
⭐ ***WHAT WORKS — STRUCTURED IDENTIFIERS:***
⭐   ⭐ card numbers (with a Luhn check), NI/SSN, IBANs, phone
⭐     numbers, emails, postcodes, API keys.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ REGEX + CHECKSUM IS EXACT, FREE AND HAS ALMOST NO
⭐     FALSE POSITIVES. ⭐ Do this unconditionally.
⭐   ⭐ ***TOKENISE RATHER THAN DELETE:*** ⭐ replace with
⭐     `[EMAIL_1]`, keep the mapping ***locally***, and
⭐     ⭐⭐ RE-SUBSTITUTE ON THE WAY OUT. ⭐ The model can reason
⭐     about "the customer" without ever seeing the address.

⭐ ***WHAT WORKS PARTIALLY — NAMES AND FREE TEXT:***
⭐   ⭐ NER (Presidio, spaCy) catches most names; ⭐⭐ IT MISSES
⭐     UNUSUAL ONES AND OVER-FIRES ON ORDINARY WORDS. ⭐ Measure
⭐     both errors (Day 448).

⭐ ***WHAT DOES NOT WORK — AND SAY THIS PLAINLY:***
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ REMOVING NAMES DOES NOT ANONYMISE. ⭐ "The junior
⭐     developer on the payments team who was off sick in March"
⭐     identifies exactly one person. ⭐⭐ RE-IDENTIFICATION FROM
⭐     CONTEXT IS THE NORM IN CONVERSATIONAL DATA, NOT THE
⭐     EXCEPTION.
⭐   ⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ SO WHAT YOU HAVE IS ***PSEUDONYMISED***, NOT
⭐     ANONYMISED — ⭐ which means it is ***still personal data***
⭐     under GDPR and every downstream obligation still applies.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ TEAMS THAT SAY "IT'S ANONYMISED, SO WE CAN KEEP IT
⭐     FOREVER" ARE ALMOST ALWAYS WRONG, AND IT IS AN EXPENSIVE
⭐     KIND OF WRONG.
```

# Part 5 — Erasure, and the eval-set problem

```
⭐ ***"DELETE MY DATA" — THE CHECKLIST ACROSS ALL SEVEN:***
⭐   ⭐ ① primary records ✅ ⭐ everyone does this one
⭐   ⭐ ② ⭐⭐ VECTOR INDEX — by `doc_id` (D430/434)

# 💬 Interview

**1. "Where does customer data go in your LLM feature?"**

⭐ Seven places, and I'd draw them before any legal conversation because that map *is* the legal
conversation. ⭐⭐ The provider's API, the provider's abuse-monitoring logs, my traces, my eval sets, the
vector index, the memory store, and the caches — including the provider's prompt cache, which holds my
context server-side for its TTL. ⭐ Most teams reach exactly one of those with a deletion request, the
primary database, and honestly believe the data is gone. ⭐⭐ The one I'd flag hardest is traces.

**2. "Why traces specifically?"**

⭐ Because a trace contains the full prompt, which means the user's message, every retrieved chunk, the
memory block and every tool result — ⭐⭐ the most complete picture of a user anywhere in the system.
⭐ And the controls are inverted relative to the database: the database has row-level security, roles and
audit; the trace store has "engineering" as its access group, retention that's either 90 days or unset,
usually no field-level deletion path, and it's frequently a third-party SaaS. ⭐⭐ So you've built a
second copy of your most sensitive data with weaker controls, and you did it while improving
observability, which is why nobody notices. ⭐ My fixes are redact on the way in rather than on query,
store chunk **references** rather than chunk text — which keeps full debuggability and drops most of the
sensitivity — short retention for payloads with longer retention for metrics only, and audit on trace
reads so "who read this customer's conversation" is answerable.

**3. "The provider says they don't train on your data. Is that enough?"**

⭐ It answers one of three questions. ⭐⭐ Training is one thing; **retention** is another — most
providers still keep requests briefly for abuse monitoring, commonly around thirty days, and
zero-data-retention is usually a separate agreement you have to ask for rather than a default. ⭐ And
processing **location** is a third, where the thing to check is the subprocessor list rather than just
the provider, because your provider has providers and your DPA has to cover the chain. ⭐⭐ I'd also be
careful that people don't quote the consumer chat product's terms while using the API, or vice versa —
they're different products with different defaults.

**4. "How do you redact?"**

⭐ Deterministically where I can, and I'm honest about where I can't. ⭐⭐ Structured identifiers — card
numbers with a Luhn check, national insurance numbers, IBANs, emails, phones, API keys — are regex plus
checksum: exact, free, almost no false positives, done unconditionally. ⭐ And I tokenise rather than
delete: replace with `[EMAIL_1]`, keep the mapping locally, re-substitute on the way out, so the model
reasons about "the customer" without ever seeing the address. ⭐ Names in free text need NER, which
misses unusual names and over-fires on ordinary words, so both error rates get measured. ⭐⭐ What I
won't claim is anonymisation: "the junior developer on the payments team who was off sick in March"
identifies exactly one person, so re-identification from context is the norm in conversational data.
⭐ What I have is pseudonymised data, which is still personal data, and teams that say "it's anonymised
so we can keep it forever" are usually wrong in an expensive way.

**5. "What's the awkward one?"**

⭐⭐ Eval sets. ⭐ The best eval cases are production failures, and the harness has to outlive every
framework and model change — so my most valuable engineering asset is a git repo full of real customer
conversations with no retention policy and no deletion path. ⭐⭐ The fix is to synthesise from the
failure rather than store the failure: keep the *shape* — the question pattern, the retrieval condition,
the expected behaviour — with names, ids and specifics replaced. ⭐ Slightly less faithful, and it's the
difference between an asset and a liability. ⭐⭐ It has to happen at capture time; retrofitting it is
miserable.

**6. "How do you know erasure actually works?"**

⭐ I test it, which is the step that turns a policy document into an engineering control. ⭐⭐ Create a
user, exercise every feature so data lands in all seven stores, run the erasure, then grep every store
for a canary string. ⭐ It fails the first several times, and the failures are always the same ones —
the trace store, because nobody put a user id on the trace, and the embedding cache, because it's keyed
by content hash rather than by anything user-shaped. ⭐⭐ Both of those are design decisions you have to
make before you need them.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

⭐⭐ **Three minutes, out loud: "how do you handle privacy in an LLM feature?"**

Start with the map, because it's the thing most teams have never drawn. **Adding an LLM feature scatters
customer data across seven stores you didn't design as data stores: the provider's API, the provider's
abuse logs, my traces, my eval sets, the vector index, the memory store, and the caches — including the
provider's prompt cache, which holds my context server-side for its TTL. A deletion request has to reach
all seven. Most implementations reach one, the primary database, and the team honestly believes the data
is gone.**

Then name the worst one, and why it's counterintuitive. **The trace store. A trace holds the full
prompt, which means the user's message, every retrieved chunk, the memory block and every tool result —
the most complete picture of a user anywhere in the system. And the controls are inverted: the database
has row-level security, roles and audit; the trace store has "engineering" as its access group,
retention that's ninety days or unset, no field-level deletion, and it's often a third-party SaaS. So
we built a second copy of the most sensitive data with the weakest controls, and we did it while
improving observability, which is why it goes unnoticed. We fixed it by redacting on the way in, storing
chunk references instead of chunk text — full debuggability, most of the sensitivity gone — short
payload retention with longer metrics retention, and audit on trace reads.**

Then be precise about the provider question. **"They don't train on our data" answers one of three
questions. Retention is separate — most providers keep requests around thirty days for abuse monitoring,
and zero-data-retention is usually an agreement you ask for, not a default. And processing location is a
third, where you check the subprocessor list rather than just the provider, because your provider has
providers.**

Close on the two honest admissions, because they're what a reviewer is listening for. **First, we don't
have anonymised data, we have pseudonymised data. Stripping names doesn't help when "the junior
developer on the payments team who was off sick in March" identifies exactly one person — re-identification
from context is the norm in conversational data. So it's still personal data and every obligation still

engineering asset and a genuine liability. We synthesise from the failure rather than storing it — keep
the shape, drop the specifics — at capture time, because retrofitting it is miserable. And we test
erasure: seed a canary through every feature, erase the user, grep all seven stores. It fails the first
few times, always on the trace store because nobody put a user id on the trace, and the embedding cache
because it's keyed by content hash.**
