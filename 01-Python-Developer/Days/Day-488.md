# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ Negotiation, mechanically

```
⭐ ***THE THING TO INTERNALISE FIRST:*** ⭐ the offer is the
⭐   moment your leverage is at its ***maximum*** and it decays
⭐   from there.
⭐   ⭐ They have spent five to fifteen thousand pounds of
⭐     engineering time on your loop. ⭐⭐ A RECRUITER WHOSE
⭐     CANDIDATE DECLINES RESTARTS A SIX-WEEK PROCESS. ⭐ Nobody
⭐     rescinds over a polite negotiation, ⭐⭐ AND THE FEAR THAT
⭐     THEY MIGHT IS THE SINGLE MOST EXPENSIVE BELIEF IN THIS
⭐     ENTIRE PROCESS.

⭐ ***THE FIVE RULES:***
⭐   ⭐ ① ⭐⭐ ***DO NOT NAME A NUMBER FIRST.***
⭐     ⭐ "What's your expectation?" → ⭐⭐ "I'D RATHER HEAR THE
⭐       BAND FOR THE LEVEL — WHAT RANGE IS BUDGETED?"
⭐     ⭐ If pressed twice, ⭐⭐ GIVE A RESEARCHED RANGE WITH THE
⭐       BOTTOM AT YOUR TARGET, ⭐ because the bottom is what you
⭐       will be offered.
⭐     ⭐ ⭐⭐ IN SOME JURISDICTIONS THEY MUST PUBLISH THE BAND —
⭐       ASK; IT IS FREE.
⭐   ⭐ ② ⭐⭐ ***NEGOTIATE ONCE, ON EVERYTHING, TOGETHER.***
⭐     ⭐ Salary, sign-on, equity, level, start date — one
⭐     conversation. ⭐⭐ SERIAL SMALL ASKS EXHAUST GOODWILL AND
⭐     MAKE YOU LOOK DISORGANISED.
⭐   ⭐ ③ ***give a reason that is not "I want more".***
⭐     ⭐ ⭐⭐ COMPETING OFFER > MARKET DATA > SCOPE OF THE ROLE >
⭐       WHAT YOU ARE LEAVING BEHIND. ⭐ A reason lets the
⭐       recruiter argue your case internally, ⭐⭐ WHICH IS
⭐       LITERALLY THEIR JOB.
⭐   ⭐ ④ ⭐⭐ ***ASK FOR A SPECIFIC NUMBER, NOT "MORE".***
⭐     ⭐ "Could you get to £X?" is actionable. ⭐⭐ "IS THERE ANY

⭐       ENDED PUSH, ⭐ because it converts their concession into
⭐       a closed deal rather than another round.

⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ EQUITY REFRESH POLICY ⭐ — ask; ⭐ the initial grant

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ Reading the offer honestly

```
⭐ ***THE ARITHMETIC PEOPLE GET WRONG:***
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ EQUITY AT A PRIVATE COMPANY IS NOT MONEY. ⭐ It is a
⭐     lottery ticket with a strike price and a cliff, ⭐⭐ AND
⭐     THE "£60,000 OF EQUITY" IN THE EMAIL IS THE COMPANY'S
⭐     LAST PREFERRED-SHARE VALUATION MULTIPLIED BY YOUR COMMON
⭐     SHARES — ⭐ two things that are not the same asset.
⭐   ⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ ASK FOUR QUESTIONS: ⭐ what percentage of the company
⭐     is this? ⭐ what was the last preferred price and when?
⭐     ⭐ what is the strike? ⭐⭐ AND WHAT HAPPENS IF I LEAVE
⭐     AFTER TWO YEARS — HOW LONG DO I HAVE TO EXERCISE?
⭐     ⭐ ⭐⭐ THAT LAST ONE IS THE ONE NOBODY ASKS, ⭐ and a 90-day
⭐       exercise window on options you cannot afford to buy
⭐       means the equity was never yours.
⭐   ⭐ ***a four-year vest with a one-year cliff*** means
⭐     ⭐⭐ LEAVING AT ELEVEN MONTHS YIELDS ZERO.

⭐ ***COMPARE ***TOTAL*** AND ***RISK-ADJUSTED***:***
⭐   ⭐ base + realistic bonus + ⭐⭐ EQUITY DISCOUNTED FOR THE
⭐     PROBABILITY IT IS WORTH ANYTHING ⭐ + pension +
⭐     ⭐ commuting cost + the value of the days you get back.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ A REMOTE ROLE AT £5,000 LESS CAN BE WORTH MORE THAN
⭐     THE OFFICE ONE ONCE YOU PRICE TEN HOURS A WEEK AND THE
⭐     TRAVEL.

⭐ ***AND THE EXPLODING OFFER:***
⭐   ⭐ "You have 48 hours." ⭐⭐ THIS IS ALMOST ALWAYS PRESSURE
⭐     RATHER THAN POLICY.
⭐   ⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ "I'M GENUINELY INTERESTED AND I WANT TO DECIDE
⭐     PROPERLY. ⭐ I have a final round elsewhere on Thursday —
⭐     could we speak Friday?" ⭐⭐ REASONABLE COMPANIES AGREE.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AND IF THEY REFUSE FLATLY, YOU HAVE LEARNED SOMETHING
⭐     REAL ABOUT HOW THEY TREAT PEOPLE UNDER TIME PRESSURE — ⭐
⭐     which is a preview, not an exception.

⭐   SURVIVE THAT.***

⭐   HAPPINESS AT EIGHTEEN MONTHS:

# 💬 Interview

**1. "What are your compensation expectations?"**

⭐ I'd turn it around once, warmly. ⭐⭐ "I'd rather hear the band for the level first — what range is
budgeted for this role?" ⭐ Many places have to publish it if asked, and it costs nothing to ask. ⭐⭐ If
they press a second time I'd give a researched range with my target at the *bottom*, because the bottom
of any range I state is what I'll be offered. ⭐ What I wouldn't do is name a number first out of
politeness — that's the moment most of the negotiation is decided, before it's started.

**2. "There's no flexibility on the number."**

⭐ Then I'd negotiate the rest, in one conversation rather than serially. ⭐⭐ Sign-on bonuses often come
from a different budget and are the path of least resistance. Start date is free to them and valuable to
me. ⭐ Level is harder and worth more than the money long-term. ⭐⭐ And I'd ask about the equity *refresh*
policy rather than the initial grant, because whether it renews matters more than what it starts at.
⭐ I'd also make it easy to say yes: ⭐⭐ "if you can get to X, I'll sign today" converts their concession
into a closed deal rather than another round of approvals.

**3. "How do you value the equity?"**

⭐ At a private company I treat it as a lottery ticket, not as money — and I'd ask four questions to size
the ticket. ⭐⭐ What percentage of the company is this, what was the last preferred price and when, what's
the strike, and what happens if I leave after two years — how long do I have to exercise? ⭐ That last one
is the one almost nobody asks, and it matters most: ⭐⭐ a ninety-day exercise window on options I can't
afford to buy means the equity was never really mine. ⭐ Then I compare offers on risk-adjusted total,
including commute and the days I get back — a remote role at five thousand less can be worth more once
you price ten hours a week.

**4. "How do you decide between two offers?"**

⭐ Four questions, in an order that's deliberately not the intuitive one. ⭐⭐ Will I work on problems
bigger than the ones I've solved — that's scope, and it determines my *next* job as well as this one.
⭐ Do I want to be managed by this person, which is the highest-variance factor in the whole decision and
I've had forty-five minutes to judge it, so I'd ask to speak to someone on the team without the manager
present. ⭐⭐ Will I learn things that are portable, because three years of a proprietary internal
framework is three years of non-transferable experience. ⭐ And is the compensation acceptable — fourth,
⭐⭐ because putting it first is the most common way people end up unhappy with a genuinely good offer.
⭐ Then I re-read the red flags I wrote down after each loop, before I had an offer and was therefore
honest.

**5. "They've given you 48 hours."**

⭐ That's pressure rather than policy, almost always. ⭐⭐ I'd say I'm genuinely interested, that I want to
decide properly, and ask for a specific later date — "I have a final round Thursday, could we speak
Friday?" ⭐ Reasonable companies agree, because they'd rather have a considered yes than a rushed one that
unravels in month three. ⭐⭐ And if they refuse flatly, I've learned something real about how they treat
people under time pressure — that's a preview of the job, not an exception to it.

**6. "What would you do in your first ninety days?"**

⭐ Ship something real in week one, however small, because it proves the pipeline works end to end and
buys credibility for the questions I'm about to ask. ⭐⭐ Then write down everything confusing, because I
have about three weeks of useful naivety before I stop noticing — that list is the most valuable document
a new joiner produces and almost nobody keeps it. ⭐ I'd ask "why is it like this?" before "this is
wrong", because there's usually a reason and the fastest way to lose a team is proposing to rewrite
something whose history you don't know. ⭐⭐ By month two, take a feature end to end and measure something
nobody has measured. ⭐ By month three, volunteer for the thing nobody wants — the flaky test suite, the
forty-minute deploy, the service with no runbook. ⭐⭐ Unglamorous, uncontested, and it makes me the person
who owns something.

**7. "What did four hundred and eighty-eight days actually give you?"**

⭐ Not seniority — scope does that, and scope needs real problems with real consequences. ⭐⭐ What it did
was remove *knowledge* as my constraint. ⭐ I don't have gaps I'm hiding: I can build a service, persist
data properly, deploy it, reason about it distributed, and build and measure an LLM system on top.
⭐⭐ From here what limits me is the size of the problem I get handed — which is the constraint I wanted,
and the only one a job can actually fix. ⭐ The habits are the part I'd defend hardest: measure before
arguing, break it deliberately, name the price of every decision, assume the dangerous failure is the one
that returns 200, and say what it can't do before being asked.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

⭐⭐ **The last one. Three minutes, out loud: "tell me about yourself."** — the question you will be asked
more than any other, and the one you can now answer completely.

Lead with what you are, in one sentence with a number in it. **"Backend engineer, Python — FastAPI,
Postgres, containers, AWS. The last stretch of my work has been AI engineering, and the reason I'd lead
with that is that most people who say it mean they've called an API. I can tell you my retrieval system's
recall, its abstention rate on questions it can't answer, and its cost per request."**

Then the project, told as a problem rather than a build. **"It started as 'support costs too much', not
as 'build a RAG system'. I spent the first week reading tickets instead of writing code, and found a
third of the questions were aggregations — counts and totals — which a vector index fundamentally cannot
answer. So the thing I delivered included the finding that a third of the traffic shouldn't use the
technology I was hired to use. Recall on the retrieval half went from 0.62 to 0.91, and two-thirds of
that gain was free."**

Then what it cost and what it can't do, unprompted. **"What it cost: permission revocation takes up to
thirty seconds, because ACL changes propagate through an outbox rather than resolving live — I chose that
for latency and I can name the conditions that would change my mind. What it can't do: nine percent of
questions have no correct chunk retrieved, so no prompt fixes those. Full rebuild is thirty-eight
minutes, which is our worst-case recovery time."**

Close on the thing that generalises, and stop. **"The habit underneath all of it is that I measure before
I argue. I spent two weeks arguing about chunk sizes and got nowhere, then built a thirty-question eval
set in an afternoon and had the answer in ninety minutes — and it wasn't the chunk size, it was the
splitting strategy. That's the way I'd want to work on whatever you'd hand me: find the number first,
then decide."**
