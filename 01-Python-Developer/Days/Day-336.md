# Day 336 🌊

**P-336** · 🌊 **Distributed rate limiting and backpressure** — ⭐⭐ the half nobody prepares

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: rate limiting protects you from clients, and backpressure protects you
> from yourself — and a system without backpressure does not fail by refusing work, it fails by
> accepting all of it, queueing until memory or patience runs out, and then losing everything at
> once.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ Three words that get confused

```
⭐⭐ ***RATE LIMITING*** — a policy about ***who*** may do how
⭐   much. ⭐ Fairness, monetisation, abuse prevention.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ "100 requests/minute per API key."
⭐⭐ ***LOAD SHEDDING*** — dropping work you have capacity
⭐   problems with, ***right now***. ⭐ Not about fairness — it
⭐   is about survival.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ "We're at 95% and rejecting the lowest-priority 20%."
⭐⭐ ***BACKPRESSURE*** — telling the ***producer*** to slow
⭐   down, and having that signal actually propagate.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ "Stop reading from the socket. Stop polling the topic."

⭐⭐ THE DIFFERENCE THAT MATTERS:
⭐   ⭐ Rate limiting is ***configured in advance*** from policy.
⭐   ⭐ Shedding and backpressure are ***reactions to measured
⭐     state***.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ MOST SYSTEMS HAVE THE FIRST AND NEITHER OF THE
⭐     OTHERS — ⭐ which is why they degrade into total failure
⭐     rather than partial service.
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ Distributed rate limiting: the three architectures

```
⭐⭐ ① ***CENTRAL COUNTER*** (Redis).
⭐   ⭐ Every request does an atomic Lua op against one Redis.
⭐   ⭐ ✔ Exact. ✔ Simple. ✔ One place to reason about.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ ⭐⭐ A NETWORK HOP ON EVERY REQUEST, AND A HARD
⭐     DEPENDENCY: if Redis is down, do you fail open or
⭐     closed? (Day 314 — and the answer is per-limiter.)
⭐   ⭐ ✘ ⭐⭐ THE LIMIT KEY IS A HOT KEY BY DEFINITION. One
⭐     large customer is one Redis key at 40k ops/s.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Right up to a few hundred thousand rps with a sharded
⭐     Redis — ⭐⭐ WHICH IS MOST COMPANIES, FOREVER.

⭐⭐ ② ***LOCAL LIMITS, DIVIDED*** — each of N pods enforces
⭐   limit/N locally.
⭐   ⭐ ✔ Zero coordination, zero latency.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ ⭐⭐ WRONG WHENEVER TRAFFIC IS UNEVEN, WHICH IS ALWAYS.
⭐     ⭐ A client with a keep-alive connection hits one pod and
⭐     gets 1/N of its allowance.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ ⭐⭐ AND IT BREAKS DURING AUTOSCALING AND DEPLOYS,
⭐     BECAUSE N CHANGES UNDERNEATH YOU.

⭐⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ ***LOCAL DECISIONS, GLOBAL TRUTH*** — the real answer
⭐   at scale.
⭐   ⭐ Each pod keeps a local token budget and enforces
⭐     instantly.
⭐   ⭐ Asynchronously (every ~100 ms) it reports usage to a
⭐     central service and receives a new allocation.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ EXACT ENOUGH, NO PER-REQUEST HOP, AND IT DEGRADES
⭐     GRACEFULLY: IF THE CENTRAL SERVICE IS UNREACHABLE, PODS
⭐     KEEP ENFORCING THEIR LAST ALLOCATION.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ This is how Envoy's global rate limiting, Stripe's
⭐     limiter and most large gateways work. ⭐⭐ NAMING THIS
⭐     PATTERN IS A STRONG SIGNAL — most candidates stop at ①.
```

```lua
-- ⭐⭐ THE ATOMIC TOKEN BUCKET — WHY IT MUST BE ONE SCRIPT
-- ⭐ GET-then-SET across a network is check-then-act (Day 310)
-- ⭐⭐ WITH THE RACE WINDOW SET TO THE ROUND-TRIP TIME.
local tokens = tonumber(redis.call('HGET', KEYS[1], 'tokens') or ARGV[1])
local last   = tonumber(redis.call('HGET', KEYS[1], 'ts') or ARGV[4])
local delta  = math.max(0, ARGV[4] - last)
tokens = math.min(ARGV[1], tokens + delta * ARGV[2])   -- ⭐ lazy refill
if tokens < tonumber(ARGV[3]) then                      -- ⭐ cost
  return {0, tokens}
end
redis.call('HSET', KEYS[1], 'tokens', tokens - ARGV[3], 'ts', ARGV[4])
redis.call('EXPIRE', KEYS[1], 3600)                     -- ⭐⭐ OR IT LEAKS
return {1, tokens - ARGV[3]}
```

# Part 3 — ⭐ Limiting the right thing

```
⭐⭐ THE DIMENSION IS THE DESIGN (Day 314, at scale):
⭐   ⭐ Per API key ⇒ the billing boundary. ⭐ The default.
⭐   ⭐ Per user ⇒ fairness inside one customer.
⭐   ⭐ Per IP ⇒ ⭐⭐ ONLY FOR UNAUTHENTICATED ENDPOINTS, and
⭐     remember Day 324's NAT problem — an office is one IP.
⭐   ⭐ Per endpoint ⇒ ⭐⭐ ESSENTIAL. `/search` and `/health`
⭐     cost different amounts; one global limit prices them the
⭐     same and is therefore wrong for both.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ PER ***COST***, NOT PER REQUEST — this is the mature
⭐     answer. ⭐ A GraphQL query, a report, a bulk endpoint:
⭐     charge tokens proportional to work. ⭐ Day 314 put `cost`
⭐     in the interface on day one for exactly this.

⭐⭐ AND THE TWO LIMITERS THAT PROTECT DIFFERENT THINGS:
⭐   ⭐ ***CONCURRENCY LIMIT*** (how many at once) protects
⭐     ⭐⭐ MEMORY AND CONNECTION POOLS.
⭐   ⭐ ***RATE LIMIT*** (how many per second) protects
⭐     ⭐⭐ THROUGHPUT AND COST.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ A CONCURRENCY LIMIT IS OFTEN MORE USEFUL AND IS
⭐     ALMOST NEVER IMPLEMENTED. ⭐ Little's Law says they are
⭐     related — L = λW — ⭐ but only the concurrency limit
⭐     bounds your resource usage when W blows up.

⭐⭐ AND THE RESPONSE CONTRACT:
⭐   ⭐ `429` + ⭐⭐ `Retry-After` ⭐ + `X-RateLimit-Limit/
⭐     Remaining/Reset`.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ WITHOUT `Retry-After`, EVERY CLIENT RETRIES
⭐     IMMEDIATELY AND YOUR LIMITER BECOMES A BUSY-WAIT LOOP
⭐     ATTACHED TO YOUR INFRASTRUCTURE. ⭐ And jitter it, or all
⭐     of them come back in the same millisecond.
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ Backpressure: the half nobody prepares

```
⭐⭐ THE FAILURE WITHOUT IT, IN FIVE STEPS:
⭐   ① The consumer slows (a dependency got slow).
⭐   ② ⭐ The queue grows. Everything still "works".
⭐   ③ ⭐ Latency climbs. ⭐⭐ AND CLIENTS START TIMING OUT AND
⭐      RETRYING, WHICH ***INCREASES*** THE ARRIVAL RATE.
⭐   ④ ⭐⭐ MEMORY FILLS WITH QUEUED WORK — in-flight requests,
⭐      prefetched messages, buffered rows.
⭐   ⑤ ⭐⭐ OOMKILL. ⭐ Everything in memory is lost at once,
⭐      including work that was nearly done.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE SYSTEM DID NOT DEGRADE. IT ACCEPTED EVERY
⭐     REQUEST RIGHT UP TO THE MOMENT IT LOST ALL OF THEM.

⭐⭐ THE PRINCIPLE:
⭐   ***AN UNBOUNDED QUEUE IS A CRASH WITH A DELAY. EVERY QUEUE
⭐      MUST HAVE A BOUND AND A DEFINED BEHAVIOUR AT THAT
⭐      BOUND.***
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ And the behaviour is a choice: block the producer,
⭐     drop the newest, drop the ***oldest*** (often correct
⭐     for live data — ⭐⭐ A STALE METRIC IS WORTH LESS THAN A
⭐     FRESH ONE), or shed with a 503.

⭐⭐ WHERE PYTHON HIDES UNBOUNDED QUEUES — FIND ALL SIX:
⭐   ⭐ `asyncio.Queue()` with no `maxsize` ⇒ ⭐⭐ UNBOUNDED.
⭐   ⭐ `asyncio.create_task()` in a loop with nothing awaiting
⭐     them ⇒ ⭐⭐ AN UNBOUNDED TASK QUEUE WEARING NO NAME AT
⭐     ALL. ⭐ Use a `TaskGroup` plus a `Semaphore`.
⭐   ⭐ `ThreadPoolExecutor` ⇒ ⭐⭐ ITS INTERNAL WORK QUEUE IS
⭐     UNBOUNDED. `max_workers` bounds ***concurrency***, not
⭐     ***backlog***.
⭐   ⭐ Kafka `max.poll.records` prefetch ⇒ bounded, but often
⭐     far larger than you think.
⭐   ⭐ Celery ⇒ ⭐⭐ PREFETCH MULTIPLIER × CONCURRENCY messages
⭐     held in worker memory (Day 262).
⭐   ⭐ A cursor-less `session.execute(select(...)).all()` ⇒
⭐     ⭐⭐ THE WHOLE RESULT SET IN RAM. ⭐ `yield_per` or
⭐     `stream_results`.
```

```python
# ⭐⭐ BACKPRESSURE THAT ACTUALLY PROPAGATES
queue: asyncio.Queue[Job] = asyncio.Queue(maxsize=1000)   # ⭐ BOUND

async def ingest(job: Job) -> Response:
    try:
        queue.put_nowait(job)                 # ⭐⭐ NEVER `await put`
    except asyncio.QueueFull:                 # ⭐   AT AN HTTP EDGE
        raise HTTPException(503, headers={"Retry-After": "5"})
    return Response(status_code=202)
# ⭐⭐ `await queue.put()` WOULD BLOCK THE REQUEST, WHICH JUST
# ⭐   MOVES THE UNBOUNDED QUEUE INTO YOUR CONNECTION TABLE.
# ⭐⭐ REFUSING IS THE SIGNAL. BLOCKING IS HIDING THE SIGNAL.
```

# Part 5 — ⭐⭐ Deadline propagation, and work nobody will read

```
⭐⭐ THE WASTE THAT COMPOUNDS UNDER LOAD:
⭐   ⭐ Client times out at 2 s and retries.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ YOUR SERVICE KEEPS WORKING ON THE ABANDONED REQUEST
⭐     FOR ANOTHER 8 SECONDS.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ UNDER OVERLOAD, A GROWING FRACTION OF YOUR CAPACITY
⭐     IS SPENT ON RESULTS NOBODY WILL EVER RECEIVE — ⭐ which
⭐     makes the overload worse, which abandons more requests.

⭐⭐ THE FIX — ***A DEADLINE, NOT A TIMEOUT***:
⭐   ⭐ The caller sends an ***absolute*** deadline
⭐     (`X-Deadline` / gRPC does this natively).
⭐   ⭐ Every hop passes on ***the remaining budget***.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ ANY HOP WITH INSUFFICIENT TIME LEFT ***FAILS
⭐     IMMEDIATELY WITHOUT DOING THE WORK.***
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE DIFFERENCE: A TIMEOUT IS PER-HOP AND
⭐     ACCUMULATES (5 hops × 2 s = 10 s); A DEADLINE IS
⭐     END-TO-END AND SHRINKS.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ It also fixes the timeout-ordering rule from Day 325
⭐     automatically — the budget can only decrease.

⭐⭐ IN PYTHON: a `ContextVar` holding the deadline, set at the
⭐   edge, read by every client call and by long loops.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AND CHECK IT ***BEFORE*** EXPENSIVE WORK, NOT ONLY
⭐     AROUND I/O — a 4-second CPU-bound loop should ask
⭐     whether anyone is still waiting.
```

# Part 6 — ⭐ Adaptive limits, which beat configured ones

```
⭐⭐ THE PROBLEM WITH A CONFIGURED CONCURRENCY LIMIT:
⭐   ⭐ You set `max_concurrent = 50` after a load test.
⭐   ⭐ Then the database gets slower, the instance type
⭐     changes, or a neighbour gets noisy.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ 50 IS NOW WRONG, AND IT IS WRONG IN THE DIRECTION
⭐     THAT HURTS. ⭐ A static limit is a measurement with an
⭐     expiry date.

⭐⭐ ***ADAPTIVE CONCURRENCY*** — TCP congestion control, for
⭐   your service:
⭐   ⭐ Track the ***minimum*** observed latency (the
⭐     no-queueing latency).
⭐   ⭐ While current latency ≈ minimum ⇒ ⭐ raise the limit.
⭐   ⭐ When latency rises above it ⇒ ⭐⭐ QUEUEING HAS STARTED;
⭐     LOWER THE LIMIT.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THIS IS LITTLE'S LAW USED AS A CONTROL LOOP: HOLD
⭐     L AT THE POINT WHERE W STOPS BEING FLAT. ⭐ Netflix's
⭐     `concurrency-limits` and Envoy's adaptive concurrency
⭐     do exactly this.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Naming Vegas/gradient algorithms here is a genuine
⭐     differentiator, but the ***idea*** is what scores.

⭐⭐ AND THE PRIORITY RULE THAT MAKES SHEDDING HUMANE:
⭐   ***SHED IN PRIORITY ORDER, AND DECIDE THE ORDER BEFORE THE
⭐      INCIDENT.***
⭐   ⭐ Health checks and payments: never shed.
⭐   ⭐ Interactive reads: shed last.
⭐   ⭐ Bulk exports, prefetch, analytics, recommendation
⭐     refresh: ⭐⭐ SHED FIRST, AND NOBODY NOTICES.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Without a priority tag on the request, your only
⭐     option is to shed uniformly, ⭐⭐ WHICH MEANS DROPPING
⭐     PAYMENTS AT THE SAME RATE AS THUMBNAIL REFRESHES.
```

# Part 7 — ⭐ Putting it together at the edges

```
⭐⭐ THE FOUR PLACES A REQUEST MUST BE ABLE TO BE REFUSED:
⭐   ① ⭐ THE CDN/EDGE — volumetric abuse, per-IP.
⭐   ② ⭐ THE GATEWAY — per-key quotas, per-endpoint cost.
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ THE SERVICE — adaptive concurrency, priority
⭐        shedding, deadline checks.
⭐   ④ ⭐⭐ THE DEPENDENCY CLIENT — a bulkhead: a bounded pool
⭐        per downstream, so a slow dependency cannot consume
⭐        every worker (Day 339).

⭐⭐ AND THE TEST THAT PROVES ANY OF IT WORKS:
⭐   ***DRIVE 3× YOUR CAPACITY AT THE SYSTEM AND MEASURE
⭐      SUCCESSFUL THROUGHPUT AND p99 ***OF THE ACCEPTED
⭐      REQUESTS.***
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ A HEALTHY SYSTEM: THROUGHPUT PLATEAUS, p99 STAYS
⭐     FLAT, AND THE EXCESS GETS A FAST 429/503.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ AN UNHEALTHY ONE: THROUGHPUT ***COLLAPSES*** BELOW
⭐     ITS OWN CAPACITY WHILE LATENCY EXPLODES — ⭐ congestive
⭐     collapse, and it is the graph you are trying to never
⭐     see in production.
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ Any unbounded queue | It's a crash with a delay. Every queue needs a bound and a behaviour at that bound |
| ⭐⭐ `await queue.put()` at an HTTP edge | You moved the unbounded queue into the connection table. Refuse instead |
| ⭐⭐ `create_task` in a loop | An unbounded task queue with no name. TaskGroup plus a semaphore |
| ⭐ Believing `max_workers` bounds backlog | It bounds concurrency; the executor's internal queue is unbounded |
| ⭐⭐ Per-hop timeouts instead of deadlines | Five hops at two seconds is ten seconds, and abandoned work keeps burning capacity |
| ⭐ 429 without `Retry-After` | Every client retries immediately. Your limiter becomes a busy-wait loop |
| ⭐⭐ Local limits divided by N | Traffic is never even, and N changes during deploys and autoscaling |
| ⭐ One global rate limit for all endpoints | `/search` and `/health` cost different amounts; the limit is wrong for both |
| ⭐⭐ No concurrency limit, only a rate limit | Only concurrency bounds memory and pool usage when latency blows up |
| ⭐ Static concurrency limits | A measurement with an expiry date. Adaptive limits track reality |
| ⭐⭐ Shedding uniformly | You drop payments at the same rate as thumbnail refreshes. Tag priority in advance |
| ⭐ `.all()` on a large query | The whole result set in RAM. `yield_per` / `stream_results` |

## Interview questions

**1. "How do you rate limit across 50 pods?"**
> ⭐ Three options. A central Redis counter with an atomic Lua script is exact and fine to hundreds of
> thousands of rps — ⭐⭐ **the script must be one operation, because GET-then-SET is check-then-act
> with the race window set to the round-trip time.** ⭐ Dividing the limit locally is wrong because
> traffic is never even and N changes during deploys. ⭐⭐ At real scale it's **local decisions with
> global truth**: each pod enforces from a local budget and asynchronously reconciles with a central
> allocator, which means no per-request hop and graceful degradation when the allocator is unreachable.

**2. "What is backpressure and why does its absence kill systems?"**
> ⭐ It's the signal telling the producer to slow down, and it has to actually propagate. ⭐⭐ Without
> it the failure isn't refusal, it's acceptance: **the queue grows, latency climbs, clients time out
> and retry which increases arrival rate, memory fills, and the process is OOMKilled — losing
> everything at once, including work that was nearly finished.** ⭐ The principle is that an unbounded
> queue is a crash with a delay, so every queue needs a bound and a defined behaviour at that bound.

**3. "Where do unbounded queues hide in a Python service?"**
> ⭐ Six places: `asyncio.Queue()` with no maxsize; `create_task` in a loop, which is an unbounded task
> queue with no name; ⭐⭐ `ThreadPoolExecutor`, where `max_workers` bounds concurrency but **the
> internal work queue is unbounded**; Kafka prefetch; Celery's prefetch multiplier times concurrency
> held in worker memory; and `.all()` on a large query, which puts the whole result set in RAM.

**4. "Timeouts or deadlines?"**
> ⭐⭐ Deadlines. **A timeout is per-hop and accumulates — five hops at two seconds is ten seconds — a
> deadline is end-to-end and shrinks.** ⭐ The caller sends an absolute deadline, every hop passes on
> the remaining budget, and any hop without enough time left fails immediately rather than doing work
> nobody will read. ⭐ Under overload, that abandoned work is a growing fraction of your capacity, which
> makes the overload worse.

**5. "How do you pick a concurrency limit?"**
> ⭐ I'd rather not pick one — ⭐⭐ **a static limit is a measurement with an expiry date**, wrong as
> soon as the database slows or the instance type changes. ⭐ Adaptive concurrency tracks the minimum
> observed latency as the no-queueing baseline, raises the limit while latency stays flat, and lowers
> it the moment latency rises. ⭐⭐ That's Little's Law used as a control loop: hold L at the point
> where W stops being flat.

**6. "How do you know your shedding works?"**
> ⭐⭐ Drive three times capacity and measure successful throughput and p99 **of the accepted requests**.
> ⭐ Healthy looks like a plateau: throughput flat, p99 flat, the excess getting a fast 429 with a
> jittered `Retry-After`. ⭐⭐ Unhealthy is congestive collapse — **throughput falls below your own
> capacity while latency explodes** — and that's the graph the whole day exists to prevent. ⭐ I'd also
> check that shedding is in priority order, decided before the incident, or I'm dropping payments at
> the same rate as thumbnail refreshes.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes.** Separate the three words first: **rate limiting is policy about who, shedding is
survival right now, and backpressure is telling the producer to slow down** — and note that most
systems have the first and neither of the others, which is why they fail totally rather than partially.
On distribution, go past the obvious: a central Redis counter is exact and enough for most companies
forever, **but the operation has to be one atomic script, because get-then-set is check-then-act with
the race window set to the network round trip** — and at real scale the pattern is local decisions with
global truth, so there's no per-request hop and it degrades gracefully. Then the half nobody prepares.
Tell the failure as a sequence: **the consumer slows, the queue grows, clients time out and retry which
increases arrival rate, memory fills, and the process is killed — losing everything at once.** Give the
principle: **an unbounded queue is a crash with a delay**, and name Python's hiding places, especially
the executor whose `max_workers` bounds concurrency while its internal queue is unbounded. Then
deadlines over timeouts, because **a timeout accumulates across hops and a deadline shrinks**, so no
hop does work nobody will read. Close on the two things that make it real: **adaptive concurrency,
which is Little's Law as a control loop**, and the three-times-capacity test — healthy is a plateau
with fast rejections, unhealthy is congestive collapse, where throughput falls below your own capacity
while latency explodes.
