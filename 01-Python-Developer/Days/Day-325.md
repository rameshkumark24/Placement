# Day 325 ⚖️

**P-325** · ⚖️ **Load balancing** — L4 vs L7, and ⭐⭐ the health-check death spiral

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: a load balancer's job is not to spread load evenly, it is to keep traffic
> away from backends that cannot serve it — and the single most common way to build an outage is a
> health check that turns "slow" into "dead" and then removes every backend you have.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐ Where load balancers actually sit

```
⭐⭐ THERE ARE USUALLY THREE, AND CANDIDATES ONLY DRAW ONE:
⭐   ① ⭐ DNS / GLOBAL — routes to a ***region***. Round-robin
⭐        DNS, GeoDNS, or anycast.
⭐        ⇒ ⭐⭐ DNS IS A TERRIBLE LOAD BALANCER: clients cache
⭐          records past the TTL, and it cannot see health in
⭐          any useful time frame. ⭐ Day 385's failover trap is
⭐          exactly this.
⭐   ② ⭐⭐ EDGE / L7 — ALB, Nginx, Envoy. ⭐ This is the one
⭐        people mean. TLS terminates here.
⭐   ③ ⭐ INTERNAL — service-to-service. Often client-side
⭐        (Day 341) or a mesh sidecar.

⭐⭐ AND THE ONE PEOPLE FORGET ENTIRELY:
⭐   ④ ⭐⭐ THE DATABASE CONNECTION POOL IS A LOAD BALANCER. It
⭐     picks a backend connection, it has a queue, it has a
⭐     timeout, ⭐ and it saturates exactly like one (Day 322).
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ L4 vs L7 — what each one can and cannot know

```
⭐⭐ L4 (TRANSPORT): sees IP:port. Forwards packets or proxies
⭐   TCP. ⭐ Does not read your request.
⭐   ⭐ ✔ Very fast, very cheap, protocol-agnostic.
⭐   ⭐ ✔ Preserves the connection end to end (with DSR, even
⭐     the return path skips the balancer).
⭐   ⭐ ✘ ⭐⭐ CANNOT RETRY A FAILED REQUEST — it never saw a
⭐     request, only bytes.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ Cannot route by path, header, cookie or method.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ ⭐⭐ WITH HTTP/2 AND gRPC IT LOAD-BALANCES ***BADLY***:
⭐     many requests share one long-lived connection, so an L4
⭐     balancer pins all of them to one backend. ⭐ This is the
⭐     classic "why is one pod at 90% CPU and the rest idle?"

⭐⭐ L7 (APPLICATION): terminates the connection, parses HTTP.
⭐   ⭐ ✔ Route by path/host/header. Canary by header (Day 283).
⭐   ⭐ ✔ ⭐⭐ RETRY IDEMPOTENT REQUESTS ON ANOTHER BACKEND.
⭐   ⭐ ✔ Per-request balancing, which fixes the gRPC problem.
⭐   ⭐ ✔ TLS termination, compression, WAF, rate limiting.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ More CPU, more latency (~1 ms), one more thing to run.

⭐⭐ THE ANSWER TO "WHICH?" — ***L7 AT THE EDGE, L4 FOR
⭐   NON-HTTP AND FOR EXTREME THROUGHPUT.*** ⭐ And if the
⭐   interviewer says gRPC, ⭐⭐ SAY L7 OR A MESH IMMEDIATELY AND
⭐   EXPLAIN THE CONNECTION-PINNING REASON — it is a strong
⭐   signal that you have actually run one.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Six algorithms, and the two that matter

```
⭐ ① ROUND ROBIN — simple, assumes identical backends and
⭐      identical requests. ⭐ Both assumptions are false.
⭐ ② WEIGHTED ROUND ROBIN — for heterogeneous machines, or for
⭐      shifting traffic during a canary.
⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ LEAST CONNECTIONS — send to the backend with the
⭐      fewest in flight. ⭐⭐ THE RIGHT DEFAULT FOR VARIABLE
⭐      REQUEST COSTS, which is every real API.
⭐      ⇒ ⭐ Because "in-flight count" ***is*** Little's Law's L
⭐        measured per backend — ⭐⭐ IT IS A LIVE MEASURE OF
⭐        HOW BUSY THAT BOX ACTUALLY IS, not a guess.
⭐ ④ LEAST RESPONSE TIME — least-connections weighted by
⭐      latency. Better, and easier to make oscillate.
⭐ ⑤ IP HASH / CONSISTENT HASH — same client → same backend.
⭐      ⇒ ⭐ Useful for cache locality; ⭐⭐ AND DAY 324'S NAT
⭐        PROBLEM IS WAITING FOR YOU.
⭐ ⑥ ⭐⭐ POWER OF TWO CHOICES — pick two backends at random,
⭐      send to the less loaded of the two.
⭐      ⇒ ⭐⭐ NEARLY AS GOOD AS LEAST-CONNECTIONS, WITH NO
⭐        GLOBAL STATE, WHICH IS WHY DISTRIBUTED BALANCERS AND
⭐        SERVICE MESHES USE IT.
⭐      ⇒ ⭐ Naming this one is a genuine differentiator.
```

```
⭐⭐ WHY ROUND ROBIN GOES WRONG — THE CONCRETE FAILURE:
⭐   ⭐ 3 backends. Requests cost 10 ms, except 1-in-50 that
⭐     costs 3 s (a report).
⭐   ⭐ Round robin hands pod A three slow ones in a row.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ POD A NOW HAS A QUEUE, AND ROUND ROBIN KEEPS FEEDING
⭐     IT AT EXACTLY THE SAME RATE, BECAUSE IT IS NOT LOOKING.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Least-connections notices in one request.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE GENERAL LAW: ***A BALANCER THAT DOES NOT MEASURE
⭐     THE BACKEND CANNOT PROTECT IT.***
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ Health checks, and the death spiral

```
⭐⭐ THREE CHECKS, THREE DIFFERENT QUESTIONS (Day 282, sharpened):
⭐   ⭐ LIVENESS — "is this process wedged?" ⇒ RESTART.
⭐   ⭐ READINESS — "should traffic come here now?" ⇒ REMOVE
⭐     FROM ROTATION.
⭐   ⭐ STARTUP — "has it finished booting?" ⇒ don't judge yet.

⭐⭐ THE RULE THAT PREVENTS THE OUTAGE:
⭐   ***A LIVENESS PROBE MUST NEVER CHECK A DEPENDENCY.***
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ If liveness queries Postgres, then a 30-second database
⭐     blip restarts ***every pod simultaneously***, and now you
⭐     have a cold cache, a thundering herd of reconnections,
⭐     and a database that was going to recover on its own.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ YOU CONVERTED A 30-SECOND DEGRADATION INTO A
⭐     20-MINUTE OUTAGE, AND THE POST-MORTEM WILL BLAME THE
⭐     DATABASE.
```

```
⭐⭐ THE HEALTH-CHECK DEATH SPIRAL — LEARN THIS AS A STORY:
⭐   ① Traffic rises. All backends slow to 4 s p99.
⭐   ② ⭐ The health check has a 2-second timeout.
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ HEALTH CHECKS START TIMING OUT — ***ON HEALTHY
⭐        BACKENDS.*** They are slow, not broken.
⭐   ④ ⭐ The LB removes backend A. Its traffic goes to B and C.
⭐   ⑤ ⭐⭐ B AND C ARE NOW SLOWER, SO ***THEY*** FAIL THEIR
⭐        CHECKS.
⭐   ⑥ ⭐⭐ THE LB REMOVES EVERY BACKEND. 100% ERRORS. ⭐ THE
⭐        FLEET IS FINE.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE LOAD BALANCER DID NOT DETECT AN OUTAGE. ⭐⭐ IT
⭐     ***CAUSED*** ONE.

⭐⭐ THE FOUR DEFENCES — ALL FOUR, THEY ARE COMPLEMENTARY:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ ***FAIL-OPEN / PANIC MODE***: if more than ~50% of
⭐        backends are unhealthy, ⭐ IGNORE HEALTH ENTIRELY and
⭐        load-balance across all of them. ⭐⭐ A DEGRADED
⭐        BACKEND BEATS NO BACKEND. Envoy calls this the panic
⭐        threshold; ALB calls it fail-open. ⭐ Naming it is a
⭐        strong signal.
⭐   ② ⭐ Health-check timeout ***generous*** relative to p99;
⭐        require N consecutive failures.
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ THE HEALTH ENDPOINT MUST BE CHEAP AND MUST NOT
⭐        QUEUE BEHIND USER TRAFFIC. ⭐ In a saturated gunicorn
⭐        worker pool, `/healthz` waits in the same queue as
⭐        everything else — ⭐⭐ SO IT REPORTS "DEAD" PRECISELY
⭐        WHEN THE POD IS MERELY BUSY.
⭐   ④ ⭐ Shed load ***inside*** the app (Day 287, Day 336) so
⭐        the pod stays fast under overload instead of slow.

⭐⭐ THE ONE-LINE VERSION: ***HEALTH CHECKS MUST DISTINGUISH
⭐   "BROKEN" FROM "BUSY". MOST DO NOT.***
```

```python
# ⭐⭐ /readyz THAT DOES NOT LIE, AND DOES NOT AMPLIFY
@app.get("/readyz")
async def readyz():
    if not app.state.ready:                 # ⭐ draining (Day 324)
        raise HTTPException(503)
    # ⭐⭐ CACHED DEPENDENCY CHECK — one probe per 5 s for the
    # ⭐   whole pod, NOT one DB round trip per health check
    if not await dependency_cache.ok():     # ⭐ 5 s TTL
        raise HTTPException(503)
    return {"ok": True}

@app.get("/livez")
async def livez():
    return {"ok": True}                     # ⭐⭐ NO DEPENDENCIES.
                                            #   EVER.
```

# Part 5 — ⭐ Connection handling, and the slow client

```
⭐⭐ WHY NGINX SITS IN FRONT OF GUNICORN — THE REAL REASON:
⭐   ⭐ A gunicorn ***sync*** worker serves one connection at a
⭐     time (Day 323).
⭐   ⭐ A client on a train uploads at 5 KB/s.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THAT WORKER IS OCCUPIED FOR MINUTES DOING NOTHING.
⭐     ⭐ Twenty such clients and your service is down —
⭐     ⭐⭐ WITH 2% CPU. This is Slowloris, arriving by accident.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Nginx buffers the whole request, then hands a complete
⭐     one to gunicorn in microseconds. ⭐⭐ IT IS NOT THERE FOR
⭐     SPEED; IT IS THERE TO ***DECOUPLE CLIENT SPEED FROM
⭐     WORKER OCCUPANCY.***
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ (An async uvicorn worker is far less vulnerable — but
⭐     you still want the buffering, TLS and static files
⭐     elsewhere.)

⭐⭐ FIVE SETTINGS THAT MATTER, WITH THE FAILURE EACH PREVENTS:
⭐   ⭐ `keepalive` upstream ⇒ avoids a TCP+TLS handshake per
⭐     request (~1–2 ms and a port).
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ TIMEOUTS AT EVERY HOP, DECREASING INWARD ⇒ or the
⭐     outer layer gives up while the inner keeps working, and
⭐     you pay for work nobody will read (Day 287).
⭐   ⭐ `proxy_buffering off` ⇒ ⭐⭐ REQUIRED FOR SSE, or events
⭐     arrive in a clump minutes later (Day 269).
⭐   ⭐ Max body size ⇒ at the edge, before it reaches Python.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ `X-Forwarded-For` + `ProxyHeadersMiddleware` ⇒ or
⭐     every client IP in your rate limiter is the LB's, and
⭐     your per-IP limit becomes a global one (Day 314).
```

# Part 6 — ⭐⭐ What the load balancer cannot do

```
⭐⭐ SAY THIS PART UNPROMPTED. IT IS WHERE SENIORITY SHOWS.

⭐⭐ ① IT CANNOT FIX AN OVERLOADED DEPENDENCY. ⭐ Ten app pods
⭐      in front of one saturated Postgres is ten queues in
⭐      front of one queue. ⭐⭐ ADDING POD ELEVEN MAKES IT WORSE
⭐      — more connections, more contention, more context
⭐      switching.
⭐⭐ ② IT CANNOT CREATE CAPACITY. It only distributes it. If λW
⭐      exceeds your total slots, ⭐ the queue grows regardless
⭐      of how fairly you spread it.
⭐⭐ ③ IT CANNOT MAKE A STATEFUL APP STATELESS (Day 324). Sticky
⭐      sessions ***hide*** the problem until the next deploy.
⭐⭐ ④ IT IS ITSELF A SINGLE POINT OF FAILURE unless it is
⭐      replicated — ⭐ which is why cloud LBs are multi-AZ by
⭐      construction and why a self-hosted Nginx needs a
⭐      floating IP and a partner.
⭐⭐ ⑤ ⭐⭐ IT CANNOT TELL A SLOW BACKEND FROM A BROKEN ONE
⭐      UNLESS YOU TEACH IT — which is Part 4, and which is the
⭐      single highest-value paragraph in this day.
```

# Part 7 — ⭐ Outlier ejection: the good version of removal

```
⭐⭐ HEALTH CHECKS ASK THE BACKEND. ⭐⭐ OUTLIER DETECTION
⭐   WATCHES ***REAL TRAFFIC***.
⭐   ⭐ "Backend C returned 5xx on 12 of its last 20 requests
⭐     while A and B are at 0.1% ⇒ eject C for 30 s, then
⭐     probe it with a trickle."
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ IT IS COMPARATIVE, SO A ***FLEET-WIDE*** PROBLEM
⭐     EJECTS NOBODY — ⭐ which is exactly the property the
⭐     death spiral lacked.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Combine with a cap: never eject more than X% (this is
⭐     panic mode again, from the other direction).

⭐⭐ AND THE ESCALATING PROBE: after ejection, return traffic
⭐   ***gradually***. ⭐⭐ SENDING A COLD, JUST-RECOVERED BACKEND
⭐   ITS FULL SHARE INSTANTLY RE-KILLS IT — cold cache, cold
⭐   pool, cold JIT. ⭐ Nginx `slow_start`, Envoy's warm-up.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THIS IS THE SAME SHAPE AS DAY 326'S STAMPEDE AND
⭐     DAY 339'S RETRY STORM: ***RECOVERY MUST BE RAMPED, NOT
⭐     INSTANT.*** Three appearances, one law.
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ Liveness probe checking the database | A 30-second blip restarts the entire fleet. You built the outage |
| ⭐⭐ Health-check timeout tighter than p99 | Slow backends get marked dead. That's step 3 of the death spiral |
| ⭐⭐ No fail-open / panic threshold | When everything looks unhealthy, the LB removes everything and serves nothing |
| ⭐ `/healthz` behind the same worker queue | It reports dead exactly when the pod is merely busy |
| ⭐⭐ L4 balancer in front of gRPC/HTTP2 | One connection carries many requests, so all of them pin to one backend |
| ⭐ Round robin with variable request costs | The balancer isn't measuring, so it keeps feeding a queueing backend |
| ⭐ Gunicorn sync workers exposed directly | One slow client occupies a worker for minutes at 2% CPU |
| ⭐⭐ No `X-Forwarded-For` handling | Every client IP becomes the LB's; per-IP rate limits become global |
| ⭐ Timeouts increasing inward | The outer hop gives up while the inner keeps burning capacity |
| ⭐⭐ Full traffic to a recovered backend | Cold cache and cold pool. Ramp it, or you re-kill it |

## Interview questions

**1. "L4 or L7?"**
> ⭐ L7 at the edge, because you want path routing, retries of idempotent requests, TLS termination and
> canary-by-header. L4 for non-HTTP protocols and extreme throughput. ⭐⭐ And the concrete tie-breaker:
> **with gRPC or HTTP/2, an L4 balancer pins many requests to one connection and therefore one
> backend** — that's the "why is one pod hot and the rest idle" bug, and it needs L7 or a mesh.

**2. "Which algorithm would you pick?"**
> ⭐⭐ Least connections, because in-flight count *is* Little's Law measured per backend — it's a live
> measurement of business rather than a guess, and real APIs have wildly variable request costs.
> ⭐ Round robin is fine only when every backend and every request is identical, which is never.
> ⭐ In a distributed balancer with no global view, power-of-two-choices gets nearly the same result
> with no shared state.

**3. "Walk me through the health-check death spiral."**
> ⭐ Load rises, everything gets slow, health checks time out **on healthy backends**, the LB ejects
> one, its traffic lands on the others, they get slower, they fail too — ⭐⭐ and the load balancer
> ends up removing every backend and serving 100% errors while the fleet is fine. ⭐ Defences: a panic
> threshold that ignores health when most backends look bad, generous timeouts with N consecutive
> failures, a health endpoint that can't queue behind user traffic, and shedding inside the app so
> overload makes it fast-fail rather than slow.

**4. "Why must liveness not check dependencies?"**
> ⭐⭐ Because liveness means restart. A dependency check makes a shared, transient failure restart
> every pod at once — cold caches, a reconnection stampede, and a twenty-minute outage grown from a
> thirty-second blip. ⭐ Readiness is where dependencies belong: it removes you from rotation and lets
> you come back without losing your process state.

**5. "Why is Nginx in front of gunicorn?"**
> ⭐ Not for speed — **to decouple client speed from worker occupancy.** ⭐⭐ A sync worker serves one
> connection at a time, so twenty clients uploading at 5 KB/s take the service down at 2% CPU. Nginx
> buffers the complete request and hands it over in microseconds. ⭐ Also TLS, static files, body-size
> limits and `X-Forwarded-For`.

**6. "What can't a load balancer do?"**
> ⭐⭐ It can't create capacity, and it can't fix a saturated dependency — ten app pods in front of one
> overloaded Postgres is ten queues in front of one queue, and pod eleven makes it worse. ⭐ It can't
> make a stateful app stateless; sticky sessions just defer the bill to the next deploy. ⭐⭐ And it
> can't distinguish slow from broken unless you configure it to — which is where most LB-caused
> outages come from.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes.** Open by restating the job: **a load balancer exists to keep traffic away from
backends that can't serve it, not to spread load evenly** — and that framing is what makes the rest
follow. L7 at the edge for routing, retries and TLS; L4 for non-HTTP and raw throughput; **and with
gRPC, L4 pins many requests to one connection and therefore one backend, which is the one-pod-hot
bug.** Least connections as the default, because in-flight count is Little's Law measured per backend
— a measurement, not a guess — and power-of-two-choices when there's no global view. Then the part
that actually causes outages, told as a story: **load rises, everything slows, health checks time out
on healthy backends, the balancer ejects one, the rest get slower, and it removes the entire fleet
while the fleet is fine.** Give the defences in one breath — a panic threshold that ignores health
when most backends look bad, timeouts generous relative to p99, a health endpoint that can't queue
behind user traffic, and shedding inside the app. Close on limits: **a balancer can't create capacity
and can't fix a saturated database — ten pods in front of one overloaded Postgres is ten queues in
front of one queue** — and on the law that shows up three times in this stage: recovery must be
ramped, never instant.
