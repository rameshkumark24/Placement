## Interview questions

**1. "Tell me about a system you designed."**
> ⭐ Pick one you actually built, not one you read about. ⭐⭐ Structure it as the round itself: the
> constraint that shaped it, the number that decided the architecture, the one hard part, and the cost
> you accepted. ⭐ Then the part that lands: **what you'd do differently, with the reason** — that
> sentence is worth more than the rest of the answer combined, because it's the only part that proves
> you operated it afterwards.

**2. "How do you approach a design you've never seen?"**
> ⭐⭐ Pattern recognition before process: **eight shapes cover almost every distributed design**, and
> naming two in the first five minutes designs half the problem. ⭐ Reserve-then-confirm, precompute
> versus compute-on-read, cheap-filter-expensive-rank, ephemeral versus durable, admit-don't-scale, the
> log as source of truth, derived stores that must be rebuildable, and ramped recovery. ⭐ Then the
> seven moves on a clock, with a data model by minute eighteen and no new boxes after twenty-eight.

**3. "What's the most important thing in a design round?"**
> ⭐⭐ Attaching a number to every decision. **"We'd add a cache" and "reads are a hundred to one with a
> ten-gigabyte hot set, so one Redis node absorbs three hundred thousand reads a second" are the same
> box and completely different answers.** ⭐ Second is depth: one topic taken three levels down —
> mechanism, what breaks it, and the fix with its cost — beats five topics mentioned.

**4. "How would you know if you were ready?"**
> ⭐ Three unseen designs, recorded, forty-five minutes each, scored on nine rows — ⭐⭐ **and passing
> all three, because once is luck.** ⭐ Specifically at least a four on numbers, depth and
> communication. ⭐ And the honest check: **play the recording back audio-only. If it stops making
> sense, the design lived in the picture, and half a real panel is only half watching the screen.**

**5. "What don't you know?"**
> ⭐ Answer it directly and specifically — that's the point of the question. ⭐⭐ For example: **"I
> understand CRDTs conceptually but I've never implemented one, so I'd use a library and I can tell you
> exactly which property I need from it"**, or "I've read about Raft and run etcd, but I've never
> debugged a leader-election failure in production." ⭐ Specific boundaries make everything inside them
> credible; vague confidence makes everything suspect.

**6. "What's next for you technically?"**
> ⭐ Running what I design: containers, pipelines, Prometheus and Kubernetes, then cloud primitives with
> **cost treated as an engineering constraint**, and then the depth behind the answers — Raft in full,
> event sourcing, CDC, chaos engineering. ⭐⭐ And the practice that keeps this alive: **one unseen
> design, out loud, on a clock, every week** — because this skill decays faster than any other in the
> set, and it decays silently.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes: "How do you approach a system design problem you've never seen?"**

Lead with pattern recognition rather than process, because that's what actually happens in a good round:
**eight shapes cover almost every distributed design, and naming two in the first five minutes designs
half the problem.** Reserve-then-confirm whenever something is held across an operation you don't
control — **a hold is data with an expiry, not a lock.** Precompute versus compute-on-read, decided by
how skewed the distribution is, which is why feeds are hybrid. Cheap filter then expensive rank, which
shows up in retrieval, search, feed ranking, driver matching and autocomplete — five domains, one shape.
Ephemeral versus durable, and the tell is a write rate that would be dead tuples per second. Admit
rather than scale, because **a hundred-times spike with no ramp cannot be autoscaled.** The log as
source of truth, where catching up becomes a range query. Derived stores that must be rebuildable by a
command you've actually run. And recovery that is ramped, never instant. Then the process in one breath:
**seven moves on a clock, a data model by minute eighteen, no new boxes after twenty-eight, twelve
minutes of depth, and every number ending in "so this means".** Close honestly: **I know this works
because I recorded three unseen designs, scored them on nine rows, and played one back with the screen
off** — which is uncomfortable, and the only real evidence there is.
