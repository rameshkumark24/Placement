# Day 322 📏

**P-322** · 📏 **Scalability, latency vs throughput, and SLI/SLO/SLA** — ⭐⭐ Little's Law

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: "scalable" is not a property of code, it is a claim that you can meet a
> stated growth in a stated load parameter by adding resources at a cost that grows no faster than
> linearly — and until you have named the load parameter, the word means nothing.**

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ What "scalable" actually asserts

```
⭐⭐ THE USELESS ANSWER: "it can handle a lot of traffic."
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ A lot compared to what? ⭐ Of what shape? ⭐ At what cost?

⭐⭐ THE DEFINITION THAT SURVIVES A FOLLOW-UP:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ NAME THE LOAD PARAMETER. ⭐ Requests/second? Writes
⭐        per second? Followers per user? Rows scanned per query?
⭐        ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE PARAMETER IS THE DESIGN. Twitter's hard
⭐          number was never tweets/second — it was ***followers
⭐          per tweeting user***, which is Day 351.
⭐   ② ⭐ STATE THE GROWTH. 10x in eighteen months.
⭐   ③ ⭐ STATE WHAT MUST HOLD WHILE IT GROWS — usually a
⭐        latency percentile, not "it still works".
⭐   ④ ⭐⭐ STATE THE COST CURVE. If serving 10x costs 40x,
⭐        ⭐⭐ IT IS NOT SCALABLE, IT IS MERELY POSSIBLE.

⭐⭐ SAY THIS IN AN INTERVIEW AND YOU HAVE ALREADY SEPARATED
⭐   YOURSELF FROM THE CANDIDATE WHO SAID "WE'LL ADD MORE PODS".
```

# Part 3 — ⭐ Latency, response time, throughput — three different words

```
⭐⭐ THE DISTINCTION MOST CANDIDATES COLLAPSE:
⭐   ⭐ ***SERVICE TIME***  — how long the work takes once started.
⭐   ⭐ ***LATENCY***       — how long it waited before starting.
⭐   ⭐ ***RESPONSE TIME*** — what the user experiences.
⭐                            = latency + service time + transport.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ A SYSTEM UNDER LOAD DEGRADES IN THE ***WAIT***, NOT
⭐     IN THE WORK. Your handler still takes 40 ms. The user
⭐     waits 900 ms. Profiling the handler finds nothing.

⭐⭐ THROUGHPUT is requests completed per second — and it is
⭐   ***not*** 1/latency once there is any concurrency at all.

⭐⭐ NEVER REPORT A MEAN. ⭐ Report p50, p95, p99, p99.9, max.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ And recall Day 285: ⭐⭐ YOU CANNOT AVERAGE PERCENTILES
⭐     ACROSS PODS. You need a histogram, not a number.
```

```
⭐⭐ TAIL AMPLIFICATION — THE ARITHMETIC NOBODY DOES:
⭐   ⭐ One page load makes 10 independent backend calls.
⭐   ⭐ Each call is under its p99 with probability 0.99.
⭐   ⭐ P(all ten under p99) = 0.99^10 = ***0.904***.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ ~1 IN 10 PAGE LOADS CONTAINS A p99 CALL.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ YOUR SERVICE'S p99 IS YOUR PAGE'S p90.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Which is why fan-out designs (Day 351) care about the
⭐     tail far more than about the median, and why hedged
⭐     requests exist at all.
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ Little's Law — the one formula in this stage

```
⭐⭐   L = λ × W
⭐   ⭐ L = items in the system (concurrency, queue depth)
⭐   ⭐ λ = arrival rate (per second)
⭐   ⭐ W = time each item spends in the system (seconds)

⭐⭐ IT IS UNCONDITIONAL. ⭐ No distribution assumption, no
⭐ independence assumption. It holds for any stable system.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ WHICH IS WHY IT IS THE ONLY FORMULA WORTH MEMORISING.
```

```
⭐⭐ USE ①  — HOW MANY WORKERS?
⭐   ⭐ 200 req/s, mean response 120 ms.
⭐   ⭐ L = 200 × 0.12 = ***24 requests in flight***.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ YOU NEED 24 CONCURRENT SLOTS, MINIMUM, JUST TO KEEP
⭐     UP — before any headroom.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ 4 gunicorn sync workers = 4 slots = 4/0.12 = ⭐⭐ 33 req/s
⭐     CEILING. Everything above that queues, and Day 286's
⭐     "capacity" number is this arithmetic, not a guess.

⭐⭐ USE ②  — HOW BIG IS THE DB POOL? (Day 316 did this by hand)
⭐   ⭐ 24 in flight, each holding a connection for 30 ms of its
⭐     120 ms ⇒ L_db = 200 × 0.03 = ***6 connections***.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ HOLDING THE CONNECTION FOR ALL 120 ms NEEDS 24.
⭐     ⭐⭐ THAT IS THE WHOLE ARGUMENT FOR KEEPING NETWORK CALLS
⭐       OUT OF TRANSACTIONS, IN ONE LINE OF ARITHMETIC.

⭐⭐ USE ③  — HOW LONG IS THE QUEUE WAIT?
⭐   ⭐ Queue holds 500 messages, consumers drain 50/s.
⭐   ⭐ W = L/λ = 500/50 = ***10 seconds of backlog***.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ This is Day 262's "alert on oldest-message age" derived
⭐     rather than asserted.
```

```
⭐⭐ THE COROLLARY THAT EXPLAINS EVERY OUTAGE YOU WILL SEE —
⭐   ***THE UTILISATION CURVE***:
⭐     wait ≈ service_time × ρ / (1 − ρ)          ρ = utilisation

⭐   ρ = 0.50 ⇒ wait = 1.0 × service time
⭐   ρ = 0.80 ⇒ wait = 4.0 ×
⭐   ρ = 0.90 ⇒ wait = 9.0 ×
⭐   ρ = 0.95 ⇒ wait = 19  ×
⭐   ρ = 0.99 ⇒ wait = 99  ×
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THIS IS WHY "CPU IS ONLY AT 85%, WE'RE FINE" IS THE
⭐     MOST EXPENSIVE SENTENCE IN OPERATIONS.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ AND WHY AUTOSCALING ON CPU (Day 287) SCALES ***AFTER***
⭐     THE LATENCY HAS ALREADY GONE.
```

# Part 5 — ⭐⭐ SLI, SLO, SLA — three words, one of which costs money

```
⭐⭐ SLI — ***INDICATOR***. A measured ratio:
⭐        good events ÷ valid events.
⭐   ⭐ "Proportion of HTTP requests that returned < 500 in
⭐     under 300 ms, measured at the load balancer."
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ MEASURED WHERE THE USER IS. An SLI measured inside
⭐     your app cannot see the request that never arrived
⭐     because the pod was full — ⭐ which is precisely the
⭐     request that mattered.

⭐⭐ SLO — ***OBJECTIVE***. Your internal target for that SLI.
⭐        "99.9% over a rolling 28 days."

⭐⭐ SLA — ***AGREEMENT***. The same idea in a contract, with
⭐        a refund attached.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ RULE: THE SLO IS ALWAYS STRICTER THAN THE SLA. If they
⭐     are equal, you find out you breached at the same moment
⭐     your customer does.
```

```
⭐⭐ AVAILABILITY IS A RATIO OF REQUESTS, NOT OF MINUTES.
⭐   ⭐ "The server was up" is meaningless if it returned 503 to
⭐     40% of calls for an hour.

⭐⭐ THE BUDGET TABLE — MEMORISE THE 28-DAY COLUMN:
⭐   99%     ⇒ 6 h 43 m of failure allowed / 28 days
⭐   99.9%   ⇒ ***40 m 19 s***
⭐   99.95%  ⇒ 20 m 10 s
⭐   99.99%  ⇒ 4 m 2 s
⭐   99.999% ⇒ 24 s
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ 4 MINUTES A MONTH IS SHORTER THAN A DEPLOY. Claiming
⭐     four nines commits you to deploys that cannot fail, which
⭐     commits you to Day 283's expand/contract, canaries and
⭐     automated rollback. ⭐⭐ THE NINES BUY THE ENGINEERING, NOT
⭐     THE OTHER WAY AROUND.
```

# Part 6 — ⭐⭐ The error budget, and what it is *for*

```
⭐⭐ ERROR BUDGET = 1 − SLO, expressed in minutes or in requests.
⭐   ⭐ 99.9% of 100M monthly requests ⇒ 100,000 failures allowed.

⭐⭐ ITS PURPOSE IS NOT MEASUREMENT. IT IS ***ARBITRATION***.
⭐   ⭐ Budget remaining ⇒ ship features, take risks, deploy Friday.
⭐   ⭐ Budget exhausted ⇒ ⭐⭐ FEATURE FREEZE UNTIL IT REFILLS.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ IT CONVERTS "ARE WE RELIABLE ENOUGH?" FROM AN OPINION
⭐     HELD BY THE LOUDEST PERSON INTO A NUMBER BOTH SIDES
⭐     AGREED TO IN ADVANCE.

⭐⭐ BURN RATE = how fast you are spending it.
⭐   ⭐ 1x = you end the window exactly at budget.
⭐   ⭐ 14.4x for 1 hour = 2% of a 30-day budget gone.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Day 285's multi-window alert, restated: page on
⭐     ***fast burn*** (14.4x/1h), ticket on ***slow burn***
⭐     (3x/6h, 1x/3d). ⭐⭐ NEVER PAGE ON "ERROR RATE > 1%" —
⭐     that fires at 3 a.m. for a burn that would have taken
⭐     nine days to matter.
```

# Part 7 — ⭐ Choosing the right SLI, and the four-category menu

```
⭐⭐ FOUR CATEGORIES. PICK 2–3 PER SERVICE. NEVER MORE.
⭐   ① ⭐ AVAILABILITY — success ÷ valid requests.
⭐   ② ⭐ LATENCY — proportion served under a threshold.
⭐        ⇒ ⭐⭐ AS A RATIO, NOT AS "p99 < 300 ms". A ratio can be
⭐          summed across pods and windows; a percentile cannot.
⭐   ③ ⭐ FRESHNESS / QUALITY — for pipelines and derived data:
⭐        "95% of search results reflect writes < 60 s old."
⭐        ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE ONLY SLI THAT CATCHES A SILENTLY STOPPED
⭐          CONSUMER (Day 264's dead-man alert, formalised).
⭐   ④ ⭐ CORRECTNESS — "99.99% of ledger entries reconcile."

⭐⭐ AND THE RULE THAT KEEPS SLOs HONEST:
⭐   ***AN SLO YOU HAVE NEVER MISSED IS SET TOO LOOSE, AND AN
⭐      SLO YOU MISS EVERY MONTH IS BEING IGNORED.***
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Both are the same failure: the number is not steering
⭐     any decision.
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ "Scalable" with no load parameter | The word asserts nothing. Name the parameter and the growth, or don't use the word |
| ⭐⭐ Reporting a mean latency | The mean hides the tail entirely; a 40 ms mean is compatible with 10% of users at 2 s |
| ⭐⭐ Averaging p99s across pods | Not computable. Day 285. You need histograms merged, not numbers averaged |
| ⭐ Sizing a worker pool by feel | `L = λW` gives the floor in one line. Guessing gives an outage |
| ⭐⭐ Running at 90% utilisation on purpose | Queue wait is 9× service time there. The system is already broken and the dashboard is green |
| ⭐ SLI measured inside the application | It cannot see the request that never got a worker — the one that mattered |
| ⭐ SLO = SLA | You learn about the breach when your customer does |
| ⭐⭐ Latency SLO stated as a percentile | Percentiles don't aggregate; state it as *proportion under a threshold* |
| ⭐ Alerting on raw error rate | Pages for burns that would take a week to matter. Burn rate, multi-window |
| ⭐ Treating availability as uptime | The process was up and returning 503s. Ratio of requests, always |

## Interview questions

**1. "How would you make this system scalable?"**
> ⭐⭐ Refuse the question until it is well posed, politely: *"Scalable in which parameter?"* Then:
> name the load parameter, state today's number and the target multiple, name the constraint that
> must survive (a latency SLO), and only then talk about mechanism. ⭐ Finish with the cost curve —
> "10× traffic at roughly 3× cost, because the cache absorbs the read growth" is a senior answer;
> "we'd add replicas" is not.

**2. "What's Little's Law and where have you used it?"**
> ⭐⭐ `L = λW`. Concurrency equals arrival rate times time-in-system, unconditionally. ⭐ Then use it:
> 200 rps at 120 ms is 24 in flight, so four sync workers cap you at 33 rps; and a connection held for
> the whole request needs four times the pool of one held only for the query — which is the arithmetic
> argument for keeping HTTP calls out of transactions.

**3. "CPU is at 85%. Is that fine?"**
> ⭐⭐ No, and the reason is the utilisation curve, not a preference. At ρ = 0.85 queue wait is already
> ~5.7× service time; at 0.9 it is 9×. ⭐ Latency has left the building well before the CPU graph
> looks alarming — ⭐⭐ which is also why autoscaling on CPU is autoscaling on a lagging indicator.

**4. "Define an SLI for a checkout API."**
> ⭐ "Proportion of POST /checkout requests measured at the load balancer that return non-5xx within
> 800 ms, over a rolling 28 days, target 99.9%." ⭐⭐ Ratio not percentile, measured at the edge not in
> the app, rolling window not calendar month — ⭐ and I'd add a correctness SLI, because a checkout
> that 200s and loses the order is worse than one that 500s.

**5. "Your error budget is exhausted on day 9 of 28. What happens?"**
> ⭐⭐ The freeze that was agreed in advance: no feature deploys, reliability work only, until the
> rolling window refills. ⭐ The point of agreeing it beforehand is that the argument happens when
> nobody is angry. ⭐ And I'd expect a review asking whether the SLO is right — an unreachable target
> gets ignored, and an ignored target is worse than none.

**6. "Why is your service's p99 the page's p90?"**
> ⭐⭐ Tail amplification. Ten independent calls each under p99 with probability 0.99 gives 0.904 —
> so roughly one page load in ten contains a p99 call. ⭐ Fan-out multiplies your tail into the user's
> median, which is why fan-out designs hedge, and why cutting p99 helps more than cutting p50.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes.** Open by refusing the vague version: **"scalable" is a claim about a named load
parameter, a growth multiple, a constraint that must survive, and a cost curve** — and without those
four it is a mood. Then give the one formula: **L equals lambda W, concurrency is arrival rate times
time in system, and it holds without assumptions.** Use it twice, out loud — **two hundred requests a
second at a hundred and twenty milliseconds is twenty-four in flight, so four sync workers cap you at
thirty-three requests a second**, and a connection held for the whole request costs four times the pool
of one held only for the query. Then the utilisation curve, because it explains most outages: **at
ninety percent utilisation the queue wait is nine times the service time**, so the dashboard is green
while the users are gone, and CPU-based autoscaling reacts after the latency has already left. Close on
measurement: **an SLI is a ratio of good events to valid events, measured where the user is**, the SLO
is stricter than the SLA so you find out first, and the error budget exists to settle the
features-versus-reliability argument in advance, with a number, while nobody is angry.
