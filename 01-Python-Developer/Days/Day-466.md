# Day 466 🎙️

**P-466** · 🎙️ **Communication under evaluation** — ⭐⭐ "I don't know" is a scoring answer

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: the interviewer cannot see your thinking, so an unstated correct thought
> scores exactly the same as no thought at all — and the whole skill of this round is making your
> reasoning audible without narrating noise.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ Thinking aloud, and the line between signal and noise

```
⭐ ***THE PROBLEM:*** ⭐ silence is the worst possible state.
⭐   ⭐ Thirty seconds of quiet and the interviewer does not know
⭐     whether you are ⭐⭐ SOLVING IT, STUCK, OR PANICKING — ⭐ and
⭐     they will assume the worst, because that is the safe
⭐     assumption when writing a score.

⭐ ***BUT THE OVERCORRECTION IS ALSO A FAILURE:***
⭐   ⭐ ❌ ***NOISE:*** ⭐ "okay so I'm going to make a list, no
⭐     wait, a dict, hmm, actually maybe a set, let me think, so
⭐     if I iterate…" ⭐⭐ THIS READS AS THRASHING AND IT IS
⭐     EXHAUSTING TO SCORE.

⭐ ⭐⭐ ***THE DISTINCTION: NARRATE ***DECISIONS***, NOT
⭐   ***KEYSTROKES***.***
⭐   ⭐ ✅ "I'm going to use a dict keyed by the complement,
⭐     because that turns the inner scan into a lookup and takes
⭐     this from n² to n."
⭐   ⭐ ✅ "Two options here — sort first, or a heap. ⭐⭐ SORTING
⭐     IS SIMPLER AND n log n EITHER WAY, SO I'LL SORT AND COME
⭐     BACK IF k IS MUCH SMALLER THAN n."
⭐   ⭐ ✅ ⭐⭐ "I'M GOING TO BE QUIET FOR A MINUTE WHILE I WORK
⭐     THROUGH THE INDICES." ⭐ ***Announced silence is not
⭐     silence.*** ⭐⭐ THIS ONE LINE SOLVES MOST OF THE PROBLEM.

⭐ ***THE THREE THINGS TO SAY OUT LOUD, ALWAYS:***
⭐   ⭐ ① ***the option you rejected and why*** — ⭐⭐ THE ONLY WAY
⭐     JUDGEMENT BECOMES VISIBLE;
⭐   ⭐ ② ***your assumption***, before you rely on it — ⭐ "I'm
⭐     assuming the input fits in memory; tell me if not";
⭐   ⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ ***THE THING YOU KNOW IS WRONG BUT ARE DOING
⭐     ANYWAY*** — ⭐ "this is O(n²) and I know it; I want a
⭐     correct version on the board first."
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ ③ CONVERTS A WEAKNESS INTO A DEMONSTRATION OF
⭐     JUDGEMENT, ⭐ and it stops the interviewer wondering
⭐     whether you noticed.
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ How to not know something

```
⭐ ***THE FEAR:*** ⭐ "I don't know" ends the round.
⭐ ***THE TRUTH:*** ⭐⭐ BLUFFING ENDS THE ROUND. ⭐ "I don't know"
⭐   is neutral, ⭐⭐ AND THE ***RIGHT*** VERSION OF IT SCORES
⭐   POSITIVELY.

⭐ ***THE FOUR-PART SHAPE:***
⭐   ⭐ ① ⭐⭐ SAY IT PLAINLY. ⭐ "I don't know."
⭐   ⭐ ② ***mark the boundary*** — ⭐ what you ***do*** know that
⭐     is adjacent. ⭐⭐ THIS IS THE PART THAT SCORES, ⭐ because
⭐     knowing the edge of your knowledge is itself expertise.
⭐   ⭐ ③ ***reason from what you know*** — ⭐ "but I'd expect it
⭐     to behave like X because…"
⭐   ⭐ ④ ⭐⭐ SAY HOW YOU'D FIND OUT ⭐ — and be specific: not
⭐     "I'd Google it" but "I'd check the pgvector docs for
⭐     whether iterative scan is in this version, and confirm
⭐     with `EXPLAIN`."

⭐ ***WORKED:***
⭐   ⭐ Q: "How does Python's GIL interact with `asyncio`?"
⭐   ⭐ ❌ a confident wrong paragraph. ⭐⭐ YOUR INTERVIEWER KNOWS.
⭐   ⭐ ✅ ⭐⭐ "I KNOW `asyncio` IS SINGLE-THREADED COOPERATIVE
⭐     CONCURRENCY, SO THE GIL ISN'T THE CONSTRAINT — the
⭐     constraint is that any blocking call stalls the whole
⭐     loop. ⭐ What I'm less sure about is the exact interaction
⭐     when you mix a thread pool in via `to_thread`. ⭐⭐ MY
⭐     MENTAL MODEL IS THAT THE POOL THREADS STILL CONTEND FOR
⭐     THE GIL SO IT ONLY HELPS FOR I/O OR C EXTENSIONS THAT
⭐     RELEASE IT — but I'd want to verify that rather than
⭐     assert it."
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ THAT ANSWER SCORES HIGHER THAN A CORRECT ONE DELIVERED
⭐     WITHOUT THE BOUNDARY, ⭐ because it tells the interviewer
⭐     they can trust your confident statements.

⭐ ⭐⭐ THE UNDERLYING PRINCIPLE: ***CALIBRATION IS A SKILL THEY
⭐   ARE SCORING.*** ⭐ An engineer whose certainty is
⭐   uncorrelated with correctness is dangerous, ⭐⭐ AND YOU HAVE
⭐   SPENT A WHOLE STAGE LEARNING WHY (DAY 449).
```

# Part 3 — Recovering mid-sentence

```
⭐ ***YOU WILL SAY SOMETHING WRONG. THE RECOVERY IS SCORED, NOT
⭐   THE ERROR.***

⭐ ***THE THREE SITUATIONS:***

⭐ ① ⭐⭐ ***YOU CATCH IT YOURSELF.*** ⭐ Best case, and it is
⭐   actively good.
⭐   ⭐ "…so we'd sort by end time — ⭐⭐ ACTUALLY NO, THAT'S FOR
⭐     INTERVAL SCHEDULING. ⭐ For merging we want start time.
⭐     Let me redo that."
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ SELF-CORRECTION IS A ***POSITIVE*** SIGNAL. ⭐ Do not
⭐     apologise for it — ⭐ just correct and continue.

⭐ ② ***THEY CATCH IT.*** ⭐ "Are you sure about that?"
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ THAT SENTENCE ALWAYS MEANS NO. ⭐ Treat it as a gift.
⭐   ⭐ ✅ "Let me re-check… no, you're right, that breaks when
⭐     the intervals touch at a single point. ⭐⭐ THE CONDITION
⭐     SHOULD BE `<=` NOT `<`."
⭐   ⭐ ❌ ⭐⭐ DEFENDING IT. ⭐ The cost of being wrong is small;
⭐     the cost of being unable to hear a correction is the
⭐     round.

⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ ***YOU REALISE YOUR WHOLE APPROACH IS WRONG*** with ten
⭐   minutes left. ⭐ The hardest one.
⭐   ⭐ ✅ ⭐⭐ "I'VE TALKED MYSELF INTO A CORNER — THIS APPROACH
⭐     DOESN'T HANDLE CYCLES AND PATCHING IT WILL BE WORSE THAN
⭐     RESTARTING. ⭐ I want to switch to a topological sort. ⭐ Is
⭐     that alright with the time we have?"
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ THAT IS A ***SENIOR*** MOVE — recognising sunk cost,
⭐     saying it plainly, and asking about the constraint you
⭐     don't control. ⭐ Grinding on a doomed approach in silence
⭐     is the failure.
```

# Part 4 — The clock, and the room

```
⭐ ***CLOCK MANAGEMENT IS PART OF THE SCORE, AND IT IS INVISIBLE
⭐   UNTIL YOU RUN OUT.***
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ ASK AT THE START: "HOW LONG DO WE HAVE, AND HOW MUCH
⭐     OF IT DO YOU WANT ON CODE VERSUS DISCUSSION?" ⭐ It costs
⭐     ten seconds and it tells you how to spend the hour.
⭐   ⭐ ***CHECKPOINT OUT LOUD:*** ⭐ "We're about halfway — I've
⭐     got a working brute force. ⭐⭐ SHALL I OPTIMISE, OR WOULD
⭐     YOU RATHER I TESTED THIS FIRST?"
⭐     ⭐ ⭐⭐ THIS LETS THEM STEER, AND THEY ***WANT*** TO STEER —
⭐       ⭐ they have a rubric and they know which box is empty.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AT T-5: STOP ADDING. ⭐ Test what exists, state the
⭐     complexity, name what you'd do with more time. ⭐⭐ A
⭐     COMPLETE SIMPLE SOLUTION SCORES ABOVE AN INCOMPLETE CLEVER
⭐     ONE, ALMOST ALWAYS.

⭐ ***THE ROOM — SMALL THINGS THAT MOVE SCORES:***
⭐   ⭐ ***restate the question before starting.*** ⭐⭐ CATCHES
⭐     MISUNDERSTANDINGS WHILE THEY ARE STILL FREE.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ ASK CLARIFYING QUESTIONS ***THAT CHANGE YOUR
⭐     APPROACH***. ⭐ "Sorted input?" is useful. ⭐ "What's the
⭐     business context?" on a two-sum is theatre, and it reads
⭐     as such.
⭐   ⭐ ***treat a hint as information, not as failure.***
⭐     ⭐⭐ INTERVIEWERS HINT BECAUSE THEY WANT YOU TO PASS. ⭐ Not
⭐     taking one costs far more than needing one.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ REMOTE: SHARE THE SCREEN EARLY, TYPE IN A BIG FONT,
⭐     AND SAY WHAT YOU ARE DOING WHEN YOU LEAVE THE EDITOR ⭐ —
⭐     silence plus a frozen screen is the worst thirty seconds
⭐     in remote interviewing.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ ENERGY IS SCORED WHETHER OR NOT ANYONE ADMITS IT. ⭐ At
⭐     the fourth interview of the day you will sound flat, and
⭐     flat reads as uninterested rather than tired.
```

# Part 5 — The two questions that end every interview

```
⭐ ① ⭐⭐ ***"DO YOU HAVE ANY QUESTIONS FOR ME?"***
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ "NO, I THINK YOU'VE COVERED EVERYTHING" IS A SCORED
⭐     NEGATIVE. ⭐ It reads as indifference on a day when they
⭐     are deciding whether you want this.
⭐   ⭐ ***Have four, and make them diagnostic*** (Day 460):
⭐     ⭐ "What does your eval set look like?"
⭐     ⭐ "What's your cost per request?"
⭐     ⭐ "How do you roll back a prompt?"
⭐     ⭐ ⭐⭐ "WHAT HAPPENS WHEN THE PROVIDER IS DOWN?"
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ EACH ONE IS SIMULTANEOUSLY A GOOD QUESTION AND A TEST
⭐     OF WHETHER THIS TEAM IS SERIOUS. ⭐ And ask them warmly —
⭐     the same words asked coldly read as an audit.
⭐   ⭐ ***Also ask one human question.*** ⭐ "What's the thing
⭐     that most surprised you about working here?"

⭐ ② ***"IS THERE ANYTHING ELSE YOU'D LIKE TO ADD?"***
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ THIS IS A FREE THIRTY SECONDS AND ALMOST EVERYONE
⭐     WASTES IT. ⭐ Use it to close a gap you noticed:
⭐   ⭐ "One thing — I gave a shaky answer on the caching
⭐     question. ⭐⭐ WHAT I SHOULD HAVE SAID IS THAT THE CACHE
⭐     KEY HAS TO INCLUDE THE TENANT AND PRINCIPAL SET, BECAUSE
⭐     OTHERWISE A CACHE HIT ***BYPASSES*** THE PERMISSION FILTER
⭐     ENTIRELY."
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ RECOVERING A QUESTION YOU FLUFFED IS ONE OF THE FEW
⭐     WAYS TO MOVE A SCORE AFTER THE FACT, ⭐ and it demonstrates
⭐     exactly the self-correction from Part 3.
```

# 💬 Interview

**1. "You went quiet for a while there."**

⭐ That's a fair hit, and it's the thing I actively manage now. ⭐⭐ Silence is the worst state in an
interview, because the interviewer can't tell whether I'm solving it, stuck, or panicking — and when
they have to write a score, they'll assume the worst. ⭐ But narrating every keystroke is its own
failure; it reads as thrashing. ⭐⭐ So I narrate *decisions* rather than keystrokes, and when I genuinely
need to think, I say "I'm going to be quiet for a minute while I work through the indices." ⭐ Announced
silence isn't silence, and that one sentence solves most of the problem.

**2. "How do you handle a question you don't know?"**

⭐ Plainly, and then I mark the boundary — which is the part that actually scores. ⭐⭐ "I don't know" is
neutral; bluffing ends the round, because my interviewer has built the thing I'd be bluffing about.
⭐ The shape I use is: say I don't know, say what I *do* know that's adjacent, reason from it, and say
specifically how I'd find out — not "I'd Google it" but "I'd check whether iterative scan is in this
pgvector version and confirm with EXPLAIN." ⭐⭐ Knowing the edge of your knowledge is itself expertise,
and it's what makes my confident statements trustworthy. ⭐ Calibration is being scored whether or not
anyone says so.

**3. "You realise your approach is wrong with ten minutes left. What do you do?"**

⭐ Say it out loud immediately and ask about the constraint I don't control. ⭐⭐ "I've talked myself into
a corner — this doesn't handle cycles, and patching it will be worse than restarting. I'd like to switch
to a topological sort. Is that alright with the time we have?" ⭐ Recognising sunk cost and naming it is
a senior move; grinding on a doomed approach in silence is the actual failure. ⭐⭐ And it gives the
interviewer the chance to say "actually, just talk me through it" — which they often will, because
they're scoring the reasoning more than the finished code.

**4. "The interviewer gives you a hint. What does that mean?"**

⭐ That they want me to pass. ⭐⭐ Interviewers hint because a stuck candidate produces no signal, and no
signal is a no. ⭐ So I take it as information rather than as evidence of failure — not taking a hint
costs far more than needing one, because it reads as either not listening or not able to change
direction. ⭐⭐ Same with "are you sure about that?", which always means no. ⭐ I treat that as a gift and
re-check, rather than defending. ⭐⭐ The cost of being wrong is small; the cost of being unable to hear
a correction is the round.

**5. "Any questions for me?"**

⭐ Always, and I'd make them diagnostic rather than generic. ⭐⭐ What does your eval set look like? What's
your cost per request? How do you roll back a prompt? What happens when the provider is down? ⭐ Each of
those is a real question I want answered *and* it tells me whether the team has operated one of these
systems — if nobody knows the cost per request, that's my first month's work and a good story. ⭐⭐ I'd
ask them warmly, because the same words asked coldly read as an audit. ⭐ And I'd add one human one:
what surprised you most about working here?

**6. "Anything else you'd like to add?"**

⭐ I treat that as a free thirty seconds that almost everyone wastes. ⭐⭐ If I fluffed something earlier,
that's where I fix it: "One thing — my answer on caching was shaky. What I should have said is that the
cache key has to include the tenant and principal set, because otherwise a cache hit *bypasses* the
permission filter entirely, since the code path containing it never runs." ⭐ Recovering a question after
the fact is one of the few ways to move a score once the round is effectively over, ⭐⭐ and it
demonstrates the self-correction they were watching for anyway.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

⭐⭐ **Two minutes, out loud: narrate the first ninety seconds of a coding problem — badly, then well.**

First the bad version, so you can hear it. **"Okay so, hmm, I could use a list here — actually maybe a
dictionary, wait, let me think about this, so if I loop through and, hmm, no…"** That's thrashing. It
contains no decision, gives the interviewer nothing to score, and it's exhausting to listen to.

Now the good version. **"Let me restate it: given an array and a target, return the indices of two
numbers that sum to the target, and I can assume exactly one solution. The brute force is the nested
loop at O(n²) — I'll say that so we've both got it on the table, but I don't want to write it. The
observation is that for each element I'm asking 'have I already seen target minus this?', and 'have I
seen' is a hash lookup. So one pass, a dict from value to index, and it's O(n) time and O(n) space. I'm
going to trade space for time here deliberately — if memory were the constraint I'd sort and use two
pointers instead, at n log n. Let me write it."**

Notice what that did in ninety seconds: **restated the problem, named the rejected alternative and why,
stated the complexity before writing, and flagged the trade-off explicitly.** Four scoring events before
a single line of code.

Then rehearse the three lines that save rounds. **"I'm going to be quiet for a minute while I work
through the indices."** — **"I've talked myself into a corner; patching this will be worse than
restarting, and I'd like to switch approach. Is that alright with the time we have?"** — **"I don't
know. What I do know is the adjacent thing, and here's specifically how I'd check the rest."**
