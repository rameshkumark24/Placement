# Day 249 🏗️

**P-249** · 🏗 **Requirements** — ⭐⭐ writing down what you are actually building

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** BUILD

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: a requirement you cannot write a failing test for is not a requirement,
> it is a hope.**
>
> ⭐ Writing requirements feels like paperwork and is not. ⭐⭐ It is the cheapest place in the whole
> project to discover that two people meant different things — and the only place where changing your
> mind costs an afternoon instead of a fortnight.

---

# Part 1 — ⭐ The document, and why each section exists

```
⭐⭐ SIX SECTIONS. NOTHING ELSE. A twenty-page PRD nobody reads is worse
⭐   than a two-page one everybody does.

⭐   ① THE PROBLEM         ⭐ one paragraph, in the user's language
⭐   ② THE ACTORS          ⭐ who, and what each may see
⭐   ③ THE STORIES         ⭐⭐ with TESTABLE acceptance criteria
⭐   ④ ⭐⭐ OUT OF SCOPE     ⭐ the most valuable section
⭐   ⑤ NON-FUNCTIONAL      ⭐⭐ with NUMBERS, or it is decoration
⭐   ⑥ RISKS               ⭐ what could make this fail, and the response
```

```
⭐⭐ ① THE PROBLEM — and the trap is writing the SOLUTION here.
⭐   ❌ "A web app with a job scheduling calendar and a mobile view."
⭐        ⭐ That is a design. It forecloses every alternative before
⭐        anyone has asked whether a calendar is the right shape.
⭐   ✅ ⭐⭐ "Small field-service companies schedule work by phone and
⭐        whiteboard. Dispatchers double-book technicians roughly weekly;
⭐        each incident costs a wasted visit and an apologetic call.
⭐        Invoices are raised from paper notes, days late, and 15% have
⭐        an error."
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ NOTICE WHAT THE GOOD VERSION GIVES YOU FOR FREE: the domain,
⭐     the primary invariant (no double-booking), the money path, AND
⭐     two numbers you can later claim to have improved.
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ Stories, and acceptance criteria that are tests

```
⭐   ❌ "As a dispatcher I want to assign jobs so that work gets done."
⭐      ⭐⭐ UNTESTABLE. Every implementation satisfies it, including a
⭐      broken one.

⭐   ✅ ⭐⭐ AS A DISPATCHER, I ASSIGN A TECHNICIAN TO A JOB.
⭐      GIVEN a scheduled job and an available technician
⭐      WHEN I assign them for 09:00–11:00
⭐      THEN the job becomes `dispatched` and the technician is shown as
⭐           busy for that window
⭐      ⭐⭐ AND GIVEN that technician already has 10:00–12:00
⭐          WHEN I attempt the assignment
⭐          THEN it is rejected with 409 and a message naming the clash
⭐      ⭐⭐ AND GIVEN two dispatchers attempt overlapping assignments
⭐          ***SIMULTANEOUSLY***
⭐          THEN ***EXACTLY ONE*** succeeds
⭐
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THAT LAST CLAUSE IS THE ONE THAT MATTERS. It is a
⭐     REQUIREMENT, not an implementation note, and writing it here is
⭐     what stops it being discovered in production.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ EVERY CRITERION MUST NAME A ***STATUS CODE OR A STATE***.
⭐     "Shows an error" is not a criterion; "409 with `code:
⭐     assignment_conflict`" is.
```

```
⭐⭐ THE FOUR STORIES PEOPLE FORGET, AND EACH IS WHERE BUGS LIVE:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ THE UNHAPPY PATH — what happens when it fails? A story with
⭐     no failure criterion produces code with no error handling.
⭐   ② ⭐⭐ THE CONCURRENT PATH — two actors, same object, same instant.
⭐   ③ ⭐ THE AUTHORISATION PATH — "a technician requests another
⭐     technician's job ⇒ 404, not 403" (⭐⭐ Day 118: 403 confirms the
⭐     record exists).
⭐   ④ ⭐ THE LIFECYCLE PATH — what happens to this when the parent is
⭐     cancelled, or the user is deactivated, or the tenant is deleted?
⭐     ⭐⭐ Nobody writes these, and they are half of the real work.
```

```
⭐⭐ AND THE ONE ARTEFACT WORTH MORE THAN THE STORIES: ***THE STATE
⭐   TRANSITION TABLE.*** Write it now, not on Day 254.

⭐      FROM \ ACTION  schedule dispatch start complete invoice pay cancel
⭐      draft             ✅       ❌     ❌     ❌      ❌    ❌   ✅
⭐      scheduled         ❌       ✅     ❌     ❌      ❌    ❌   ✅
⭐      dispatched        ❌       ❌     ✅     ❌      ❌    ❌   ✅
⭐      in_progress       ❌       ❌     ❌     ✅      ❌    ❌   ⭐⭐?
⭐      completed         ❌       ❌     ❌     ❌      ✅    ❌   ❌
⭐      invoiced          ❌       ❌     ❌     ❌      ❌    ✅   ❌
⭐      paid / cancelled  ❌       ❌     ❌     ❌      ❌    ❌   ❌
⭐
⭐   ⭐⭐ THE `?` IS THE POINT. "Can you cancel a job a technician is
⭐     currently doing?" is a BUSINESS question with a real answer, and
⭐     the table is what forces someone to answer it. ⭐ Every `?` you
⭐     leave becomes an `if` somebody guesses at, differently, in three
⭐     places.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Out of scope: the section that decides whether you finish

```
⭐⭐ EACH EXCLUSION GETS ONE LINE OF JUSTIFICATION, BECAUSE THE
⭐   JUSTIFICATION IS WHAT MAKES IT HOLD UNDER PRESSURE.

⭐   ❌ Offline mode — ⭐ sync conflict resolution is a project, not a
⭐      feature. Revisit only after launch.
⭐   ❌ SSO/SAML — ⭐⭐ password + refresh rotation + reuse detection
⭐      demonstrates the same competence and is finishable.
⭐   ❌ Multi-currency — ⭐ one currency in integer minor units; stated
⭐      explicitly so it is a decision, not an oversight.
⭐   ❌ Route optimisation — ⭐ a solver, not a web application.
⭐   ❌ Recurring/repeat jobs — ⭐⭐ tempting and cheap-looking; it adds
⭐      a whole scheduling-expansion subsystem and a timezone/DST
⭐      problem (Day 159). ⭐ The best example of a "small" feature
⭐      that isn't.
⭐   ❌ Native mobile — ⭐ responsive web.

⭐⭐ AND THE THREE THINGS PEOPLE PUT OUT OF SCOPE THAT MUST NOT BE:
⭐   ⭐⭐ ❌❌ "AUTH — we'll add it later." ⭐⭐ Authorisation shapes every
⭐     query (Day 184 #9). Retrofitting it means rewriting every query
⭐     you wrote.
⭐   ⭐⭐ ❌❌ "MULTI-TENANCY — single tenant for now." ⭐⭐ Same reason,
⭐     worse. The tenant column belongs in the first migration or it
⭐     belongs in a rewrite.
⭐   ⭐⭐ ❌❌ "OBSERVABILITY — after launch." ⭐ Then your first
⭐     production problem is diagnosed by guessing.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE RULE: YOU MAY DEFER A ***FEATURE***. YOU MAY NOT DEFER A
⭐   ***CROSS-CUTTING CONCERN***, because the cost of adding one is
⭐   proportional to how much code already exists.
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ Non-functional requirements, with numbers

```
⭐⭐ "IT SHOULD BE FAST" IS NOT A REQUIREMENT. THESE ARE:

⭐ PERFORMANCE (Day 147's SLIs — pick your own numbers and justify them)
⭐   ⭐ job list p95 < 300 ms at 10,000 jobs per tenant
⭐   ⭐ assignment write p95 < 500 ms
⭐   ⭐⭐ AND THE ONE PEOPLE OMIT: **a hard query-count ceiling per
⭐     endpoint** — job list ≤ 4 queries regardless of page size.
⭐     ⭐ That is testable in CI; latency is not, reliably.
⭐ SCALE — ⭐⭐ state the shape you are designing for, so "it doesn't
⭐   scale" has a definition:
⭐   ⭐ 200 tenants · largest 60 technicians · 50k jobs/year
⭐   ⭐ 40 concurrent dispatchers at 09:00 (⭐⭐ your peak is a
⭐     WEEKDAY MORNING, not a uniform distribution — say so)
⭐ AVAILABILITY & DATA
⭐   ⭐ 99.5% monthly (⭐⭐ ≈ 3.6 hours down — say the minutes, not the
⭐     nines, or nobody feels it)
⭐   ⭐⭐ RPO ≤ 5 minutes · RTO ≤ 1 hour — ⭐ and Day 201 said the RTO
⭐     is whatever your restore actually took, so this is a claim you
⭐     will have to measure.
⭐ SECURITY
⭐   ⭐ passwords argon2id · ⭐ access token 15 min, refresh 30 days
⭐     with rotation and reuse detection · ⭐⭐ NO CROSS-TENANT READ IS
⭐     POSSIBLE EVEN WITH A VALID TOKEN — and there is a test file for
⭐     that sentence (Day 165).
⭐ COMPLIANCE
⭐   ⭐ delete-my-account means erasure, not a flag (⭐⭐ Day 188 — and
⭐     it changes your schema, so it belongs here and not in a ticket).
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Writing the solution in the problem statement | It forecloses alternatives before anyone questions them. |
| Stories with no testable criteria | Every implementation satisfies "so work gets done", including a broken one. |
| Criteria that say "shows an error" | Name a status code and an error code. |
| No concurrent-path story | The race is a requirement, not an implementation detail. |
| No lifecycle stories | What happens on cancel, deactivate and tenant delete is half the real work. |
| Leaving `?` in the state table | Each one becomes an `if` three people guess at differently. |
| An out-of-scope list with no justifications | It won't survive the first person who wants the feature. |
| Deferring auth, tenancy or observability | Those are cross-cutting; the cost of adding them scales with existing code. |
| "It should be fast" | Give a p95, a load, and a query-count ceiling. |
| Availability in nines | Say the minutes. Nobody feels 99.5%. |
| An RTO you haven't measured | Day 201: your real RTO is whatever the restore took. |
| A risk register with no triggers | Nothing is ever acted on, because no moment ever arrives. |
| Treating "recurring jobs" as a small feature | It's a scheduling-expansion subsystem plus a DST problem. |

## Interview questions

**Q: How do you write a good requirement?**

> So that a failing test can be written from it. That's the bar, and it rules out most of what gets
> written — "as a dispatcher I want to assign jobs so work gets done" is satisfied by every
> implementation including a broken one. A usable version names a state and a status code: given a
> scheduled job and an available technician, when I assign them, the job becomes dispatched; given a
> clash, it's rejected with a 409 and an error code naming the conflict. And the criterion I'd insist
> on that most people treat as an implementation detail: given two dispatchers attempting overlapping
> assignments simultaneously, exactly one succeeds. That's a requirement — writing it down is what
> stops it being discovered in production, and it's the difference between a system that has a
> concurrency story and one that has a concurrency incident.

**Q: What's the most valuable part of a requirements document?**

> The out-of-scope list, with a justification per line. Most projects fail by being sixty percent
> built rather than by being badly built, and the failure is scope. The justifications matter because
> they're what makes an exclusion survive the first person who wants the feature: "offline mode is
> excluded because sync conflict resolution is a project rather than a feature" is arguable and
> therefore durable, whereas an unjustified list is just a wish. The other half of that discipline is
> knowing what you're *not allowed* to defer: you can defer a feature, but you cannot defer a
> cross-cutting concern. Authorisation shapes every query, multi-tenancy shapes every table, and
> observability decides whether your first production problem is diagnosed or guessed at — the cost of
> retrofitting each is proportional to how much code already exists.

**Q: What non-functional requirements would you write?**

> Ones with numbers, and ideally ones testable in CI. Latency targets — a p95 for the list endpoint at
> a stated data volume — but I'd emphasise the one people omit: a hard query-count ceiling per
> endpoint, because that's assertable in a test and latency isn't reliably. Then the scale shape,
> stated so that "it doesn't scale" has a definition — number of tenants, size of the largest, and the
> concurrency at peak, noting that peak is a weekday morning rather than a uniform distribution.
> Availability as minutes rather than nines, because nobody feels 99.5%. RPO and RTO, with the honesty
> that the RTO is a claim until I've timed an actual restore. And one security requirement written as
> a sentence with a test file behind it: no cross-tenant read is possible even with a valid token.

**Q: What's a risk register for?**

> Making the fallback a decision rather than a panic. The value isn't the list of risks, which everyone
> can produce — it's the *trigger*, an observable condition that says "now we do the fallback".
> Without one, nobody ever declares the moment, and the project quietly absorbs the overrun. The
> example I'd give is the payment provider: deciding in advance that a fake gateway is an acceptable
> outcome if the sandbox is unavailable for two days is what stops that day becoming a week — because
> the thing being demonstrated is the transactional outbox, and the provider is incidental to it.
> Naming that in advance means the decision gets made on engineering grounds rather than sunk-cost
> ones.

## Today's build

1. ⭐ Write the problem statement in the user's language, with **two numbers** in it.
2. ⭐⭐ Write eight to twelve stories with Given/When/Then criteria. Every criterion names a state or a status code.
3. ⭐⭐ For each story, add the unhappy path. Delete any story where you can't write one — it wasn't a story.
4. ⭐⭐ Write at least two **concurrent-path** criteria, including your race.
5. ⭐ Write the four lifecycle stories: cancel, deactivate a user, delete a tenant, delete a customer.
6. ⭐⭐ Draw the full state transition table. Resolve every `?` and record the reasoning next to it.
7. ⭐ Write the authorisation stories, including one that must return **404 rather than 403**.
8. ⭐⭐ Write the out-of-scope list — six items, one justification each.
9. ⭐ Write the non-functional section: latency, **query-count ceilings**, scale shape, availability in minutes, RPO/RTO, security, compliance.
10. ⭐⭐ Write the five-row risk register, with an observable trigger for every row.
11. ⭐ Commit it as `docs/requirements.md` and tag the commit. You will diff against it on Day 287.
12. ⭐⭐ Read the whole thing aloud to someone non-technical. Every place they ask "what does that mean?" is a place you wrote a design instead of a requirement.

# 🚪 Exit questions

*Answer aloud, no notes.*

1. State the bar a requirement must clear.
2. Give the six sections and what each is for.
3. Why is the solution in the problem statement a mistake?
4. Turn a vague story into one with testable criteria, out loud.
5. Name the four story types people forget, and what each prevents.
6. Why is the state transition table worth more than the stories?
7. What does an unresolved `?` in that table become?
8. Why does each exclusion need a justification?
9. Which three things may never be deferred, and what do they have in common?
10. Give five non-functional requirements with numbers, including the one testable in CI.
11. Why say minutes rather than nines?
12. What makes a risk register useful, and what makes one decorative?
13. Why is "recurring jobs" a bad example of a small feature?

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes: "What are you building, and what will be hard about it?"**

Lead with the problem in the user's words and include the two numbers, because numbers are what turn a
description into a claim you can later prove you improved. Then the actors in one line each. Then go
straight to the three hard parts rather than the feature list — **the race, the money moment, and the
authorisation rule that isn't just a URL check** — and for the race, say the acceptance criterion out
loud: two dispatchers, simultaneously, exactly one succeeds. Then the out-of-scope list with
justifications, because the exclusions say more about your judgement than the inclusions. Close on the
non-functional number you'd hold yourself to in CI: **the query-count ceiling**, because it's the one
that's actually enforceable rather than aspirational.
