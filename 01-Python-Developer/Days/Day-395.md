# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ Envelope encryption, derived rather than recited

```
⭐ START FROM WHY YOU CANNOT JUST SEND KMS YOUR DATA:
⭐   ⭐ `Encrypt` accepts at most ***4 KB***.
⭐   ⭐ It is a network call — ⭐ tens of milliseconds.
⭐   ⭐ It has per-key request quotas.
⭐   ⭐⭐ ⇒ ENCRYPTING A 200 MB UPLOAD THROUGH KMS DIRECTLY IS
⭐     IMPOSSIBLE, NOT MERELY SLOW.

⭐⭐ SO: ***ENCRYPT THE DATA LOCALLY WITH A KEY, AND ASK KMS TO
⭐   PROTECT ONLY THAT KEY.***

⭐ ENCRYPT:
⭐   ① ⭐ `GenerateDataKey(KeyId=cmk)` → returns ***two*** things:
⭐      ⭐ `Plaintext` (a real AES key) and
⭐      ⭐ `CiphertextBlob` (the same key, encrypted by the CMK)
⭐   ② ⭐ AES-encrypt your 200 MB ***locally*** with `Plaintext`.
⭐      ⭐⭐ FAST. NO NETWORK. NO SIZE LIMIT.
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ ***THROW THE PLAINTEXT KEY AWAY*** (`del`, and mean it).
⭐   ④ ⭐ Store `CiphertextBlob` next to the ciphertext.
⭐ DECRYPT:
⭐   ① ⭐ `Decrypt(CiphertextBlob)` → the plaintext key back.
⭐   ② ⭐ Decrypt locally.

⭐⭐ ⇒ AND THE THREE CONSEQUENCES ARE THE WHOLE PRODUCT:
⭐ ① ⭐⭐ ***ENCRYPTION IS LOCAL AND FAST.*** ⭐ KMS is touched
⭐    once per ***object***, not once per byte.
⭐ ② ⭐⭐ ***YOU CAN CACHE THE DATA KEY*** AND AMORTISE THE KMS
⭐    CALL ACROSS MANY OBJECTS — ⭐ which is exactly what S3
⭐    Bucket Keys does, and what the encryption SDK's data key
⭐    cache does for your own code (Part 4).
⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ ***DISABLE OR DELETE THE CMK AND EVERY CIPHERTEXT
⭐    EVERYWHERE BECOMES UNDECRYPTABLE — INSTANTLY, INCLUDING
⭐    BACKUPS, SNAPSHOTS AND COPIES YOU FORGOT ABOUT.***
⭐    ⭐⭐ THAT IS ***CRYPTO-SHREDDING***, AND IT IS THE ONLY
⭐    MECHANISM YOU HAVE THAT GENUINELY DELETES DATA FROM PLACES
⭐    YOU CANNOT ENUMERATE. ⭐ It is the honest answer to "delete
⭐    all of this customer's data", ⭐⭐ AND IT IS ALSO THE MOST
⭐    DANGEROUS BUTTON IN YOUR ACCOUNT.
```

# Part 2 — Which key, and why it matters

```
⭐ AWS-OWNED ...... ⭐ invisible, ⭐ free. ⭐ You have no
⭐                  relationship with it.
⭐ AWS-MANAGED .... ⭐ `aws/s3`, `aws/rds`. ⭐ Free. ⭐ Rotated
⭐                  annually by AWS.
⭐                  ⭐⭐ AND THE THREE LIMITS THAT DECIDE IT:
⭐                  ⭐ ① you cannot edit the key policy,
⭐                  ⭐ ② you cannot share it cross-account,
⭐                  ⭐⭐ ③ YOU CANNOT DISABLE IT — SO THERE IS NO
⭐                     CRYPTO-SHREDDING STORY AT ALL.
⭐ CUSTOMER-MANAGED ⭐ $1/month + $0.03 per 10,000 requests.
⭐                  ⭐⭐ FULL POLICY CONTROL, CROSS-ACCOUNT
⭐                  SHARING, ROTATION ON YOUR TERMS, ⭐⭐ AND THE
⭐                  ONLY KIND WITH A REVOCATION STORY.

⭐⭐ ⇒ THE RULE: ***AWS-MANAGED FOR ANYTHING WHERE ENCRYPTION IS
⭐   A CHECKBOX. CUSTOMER-MANAGED THE MOMENT YOU NEED TO ANSWER
⭐   "WHO CAN DECRYPT THIS?" OR "MAKE THIS UNREADABLE".***
⭐   ⭐ A dollar a month is not the consideration; ⭐⭐ THE REQUEST
⭐   CHARGES CAN BE (PART 4).

⭐ ⭐ Symmetric (AES-256) for almost everything.
⭐ ⭐ Asymmetric for signing, or for giving someone a public key.
⭐ ⭐⭐ MULTI-REGION KEYS: ⭐ the same key material in several
⭐   regions with the same key id. ⭐⭐ THE SPECIFIC PROBLEM THEY
⭐   SOLVE: ⭐ an encrypted RDS snapshot or S3 object replicated
⭐   to another region ⭐⭐ CANNOT BE DECRYPTED THERE WITHOUT ONE
⭐   — ⭐ which people discover during a disaster-recovery test,
⭐   ⭐⭐ WHICH IS THE GOOD OUTCOME, OR DURING AN ACTUAL DISASTER,
⭐   WHICH IS NOT.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Key policies, and the lockout that support cannot fix

```
⭐⭐ ***A KEY POLICY IS THE ***PRIMARY*** ACCESS CONTROL FOR A
⭐   KMS KEY — AND THIS IS THE ONE PLACE AWS INVERTS THE RULE
⭐   YOU LEARNED ON DAY 381.***
⭐   ⭐ For S3, an IAM policy alone can grant access.
⭐   ⭐⭐ FOR KMS, ***AN IAM POLICY GRANTS NOTHING UNLESS THE KEY
⭐     POLICY DELEGATES TO IAM.*** ⭐ Every key policy therefore
⭐     starts with the statement that enables IAM at all:

⭐   { "Sid": "EnableIAM",
⭐     "Effect": "Allow",
⭐     "Principal": {"AWS": "arn:aws:iam::111:root"},
⭐     "Action": "kms:*", "Resource": "*" }

⭐   ⭐ `root` here means "this account's IAM", ⭐ not the root
⭐     user.

⭐⭐ AND NOW THE WARNING THAT IS THE SHARPEST IN THE STAGE:
⭐   ⭐⭐ ***IF YOU WRITE A KEY POLICY THAT OMITS THAT STATEMENT
⭐   AND NAMES ONLY A ROLE THAT IS LATER DELETED, THE KEY IS
⭐   PERMANENTLY UNMANAGEABLE.***
⭐   ⭐ Nobody can edit the policy, because editing it requires
⭐     permission the policy no longer grants anyone.
⭐   ⭐⭐ AWS SUPPORT CANNOT HELP. ⭐ There is no override.
⭐   ⭐⭐ EVERY CIPHERTEXT UNDER THAT KEY IS GONE. ⭐ Permanently.
⭐   ⭐⭐ ⇒ ***ALWAYS INCLUDE THE ROOT STATEMENT. ALWAYS.*** ⭐ It
⭐     is not a weakening; ⭐ it delegates to IAM, which you
⭐     control and audit.

⭐ GRANTS: ⭐ temporary, programmatic permissions on a key.
⭐   ⭐⭐ THE RIGHT TOOL WHEN AN AWS SERVICE NEEDS TO USE YOUR KEY
⭐   ON YOUR BEHALF FOR A WHILE — ⭐ they can be retired, and they
⭐   do not require editing the policy.
⭐ ⭐⭐ AND THE CONDITION WORTH KNOWING: `kms:ViaService`.
⭐   ⭐ "This key may only be used ***through S3***" — ⭐⭐ SO A
⭐   STOLEN ROLE CANNOT CALL `Decrypt` DIRECTLY ON ARBITRARY
⭐   CIPHERTEXT, ⭐ only through the service you intended.
```

# Part 4 — The cost and throttling you will actually hit

```
⭐ $0.03 per 10,000 requests. ⭐ Reads as free.

⭐⭐ NOW THE JOB: A PYTHON PIPELINE READING ***50 MILLION***
⭐   SSE-KMS OBJECTS FROM S3.
⭐   ⭐ 50,000,000 `Decrypt` calls ⭐⭐ = $150
⭐   ⭐⭐ AND — WORSE — YOU HIT THE PER-KEY REQUEST QUOTA
⭐     (thousands to tens of thousands per second, ⭐ shared
⭐     ***across the whole account***), ⭐⭐ AND GET
⭐     `ThrottlingException` — ⭐ which your 32-thread pool from
⭐     Day 386 turns into a retry storm, ⭐⭐ AND THE JOB SLOWS TO
⭐     A CRAWL FOR REASONS THAT LOOK LIKE S3 BEING SLOW.

⭐⭐ ⇒ TWO FIXES, AND THEY ARE THE SAME IDEA:
⭐   ⭐⭐ ① ***S3 BUCKET KEYS.*** ⭐ One setting. ⭐ S3 derives a
⭐      bucket-level key and reuses it, ⭐⭐ CUTTING KMS CALLS BY
⭐      ~99% AND THE COST WITH THEM. ⭐ There is no reason not to
⭐      have it on.
⭐   ⭐⭐ ② FOR APPLICATION-LEVEL ENCRYPTION, THE
⭐      `aws-encryption-sdk` ***DATA KEY CACHE*** — ⭐ reuse one
⭐      data key for N messages or T seconds, bounded explicitly.
⭐      ⭐⭐ YOU ARE TRADING BLAST RADIUS FOR CALLS, ***AND THE
⭐      LIBRARY MAKES YOU STATE THE TRADE IN NUMBERS*** — ⭐ which
⭐      is the right way round.
```

# Part 5 — Secrets Manager vs Parameter Store

| | Parameter Store | Secrets Manager |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | ⭐⭐ **Standard tier free** | $0.40/secret/mo + $0.05/10k calls |
| Size | 4 KB (8 KB advanced) | 64 KB |
| **Rotation** | ⭐ none, you build it | ⭐⭐ **built-in, Lambda-driven** |
| Cross-region | manual | ⭐ replication built in |
| Versioning | history | ⭐⭐ **staging labels** |
| Right for | ⭐ config, and secrets you rotate by hand | ⭐⭐ **anything needing automatic rotation** |

```
⭐⭐ THE HONEST RULE: ***PARAMETER STORE UNTIL AUTOMATIC ROTATION
⭐   IS AN ACTUAL REQUIREMENT.*** ⭐ At 200 secrets, Secrets
⭐   Manager is $80/month — ⭐ small, ⭐⭐ BUT PAYING IT FOR
⭐   SECRETS NOBODY ROTATES IS BUYING A FEATURE YOU HAVE
⭐   SWITCHED OFF.

⭐⭐ AND ROTATION IS THE THING WORTH LEARNING, BECAUSE IT IS DAY
⭐   372's HARD PART IMPLEMENTED FOR YOU — ⭐ a four-step state
⭐   machine your Lambda must implement:
⭐   ⭐ ① `createSecret` — ⭐ generate the new value, store it as
⭐      ⭐⭐ `AWSPENDING`. ⭐ The old one is still `AWSCURRENT`.
⭐   ⭐ ② `setSecret` — ⭐ actually change it at the source (the
⭐      database).
⭐   ⭐ ③ `testSecret` — ⭐⭐ CONNECT USING THE PENDING VALUE.
⭐      ⭐ This step is why rotation is safe.
⭐   ⭐ ④ `finishSecret` — ⭐ move the `AWSCURRENT` label.

⭐⭐ ⇒ AND ***THAT IS EXACTLY DAY 372's REQUIREMENT — TWO
⭐   CREDENTIALS VALID AT ONCE*** — ⭐ with the labels making the
⭐   overlap explicit. ⭐⭐ AND POSTGRES'S ONE-PASSWORD-PER-ROLE
⭐   CONSTRAINT IS EXACTLY WHY AWS SHIPS AN ***ALTERNATING-USERS***
⭐   ROTATION STRATEGY: ⭐ two roles, `app_a` and `app_b`,
⭐   alternating. ⭐⭐ THE PROBLEM YOU DERIVED ON DAY 372 HAS A
⭐   NAMED PRODUCT FEATURE, WHICH IS A GOOD SIGN YOU DERIVED IT
⭐   CORRECTLY.
```

# Part 6 — The Python patterns

```python
# ⭐⭐ THE TENSION TO RESOLVE: Day 372 said "read the credential
# ⭐   at USE, not at import" — but fetching per request is an
# ⭐   API call and $0.05/10k on your hot path.
# ⭐⭐ THE ANSWER IS A CACHE WITH A TTL PLUS A FORCED REFRESH ON
# ⭐   AUTH FAILURE. You get both properties.

import time, json, threading, boto3

class Secret:
    def __init__(self, secret_id, ttl=300):
        self._id, self._ttl = secret_id, ttl
        self._v, self._at, self._lock = None, 0.0, threading.Lock()
        self._sm = boto3.client("secretsmanager")

    def get(self, force=False):
        with self._lock:
            if force or self._v is None or time.time() - self._at > self._ttl:
                raw = self._sm.get_secret_value(SecretId=self._id)
                self._v = json.loads(raw["SecretString"])   # ⭐ a JSON blob
                self._at = time.time()
            return self._v

DB = Secret("prod/db")

def connect():
    try:
        return psycopg.connect(password=DB.get()["password"], ...)
    except psycopg.OperationalError as e:
        if "authentication failed" not in str(e):
            raise
        # ⭐⭐ ROTATION HAPPENED MID-TTL. REFETCH ***ONCE*** AND
        # ⭐   RETRY. ⭐ Without this, rotation causes a 5-minute
        # ⭐   partial outage every time it runs.
        return psycopg.connect(password=DB.get(force=True)["password"], ...)
```

```
⭐⭐ AND FOUR SMALLER THINGS THAT EACH COST SOMEONE A NIGHT:
⭐   ⭐⭐ ① ***THE SECRET VALUE IS A JSON STRING, NOT A
⭐      PASSWORD.*** ⭐ `json.loads` then a `KeyError` — ⭐⭐ SO
⭐      VALIDATE ITS SHAPE AT STARTUP (DAY 372), NOT ON THE
⭐      FIRST CONNECTION AT 3 A.M.
⭐   ⭐ ② On Lambda, use the ⭐⭐ PARAMETERS AND SECRETS EXTENSION
⭐      — ⭐ a local HTTP cache, so you are not paying an API call
⭐      per cold start.
⭐   ⭐ ③ ECS injects secrets by ARN at task start (Day 390) —
⭐      ⭐⭐ SIMPLEST OPTION, AND IT MEANS A ROTATION NEEDS A TASK
⭐      RESTART. ⭐ That is a legitimate trade; ⭐⭐ JUST KNOW YOU
⭐      MADE IT.
⭐   ⭐ ④ ⭐⭐ NEVER LOG THE SECRET OBJECT. ⭐ Give the class a
⭐      `__repr__` that returns `<Secret prod/db>` — ⭐⭐ BECAUSE
⭐      AN ERROR SDK WILL HAPPILY SERIALISE YOUR LOCALS
⭐      (DAY 375).
```

# Part 7 — What encryption at rest does not do

```
⭐⭐ ***YOUR APPLICATION CAN DECRYPT. THEREFORE AN ATTACKER WITH
⭐   YOUR APPLICATION'S ROLE CAN DECRYPT.*** ⭐ Encryption at rest
⭐   does nothing whatsoever against a stolen credential.

⭐ WHAT IT ***DOES*** DEFEND:
⭐   ⭐ A disk or a backup tape leaving the building.
⭐   ⭐ A snapshot shared or made public by accident.
⭐   ⭐ A decommissioned drive.
⭐   ⭐ An auditor's question, ⭐⭐ AND THAT IS A LEGITIMATE
⭐     REASON — ⭐ compliance is a real requirement even when the
⭐     threat model is thin.
⭐   ⭐⭐ AND CRYPTO-SHREDDING, WHICH IS THE ONE GENUINELY
⭐     IRREPLACEABLE CAPABILITY (PART 1).

⭐⭐ ⇒ SO SAY IT PLAINLY: ***ENCRYPTION AT REST IS A CONTROL
⭐   AGAINST PHYSICAL AND ACCIDENTAL EXPOSURE. IAM IS THE
⭐   CONTROL AGAINST STOLEN CREDENTIALS.*** ⭐ Turning on
⭐   encryption is table stakes and costs nothing; ⭐⭐ BELIEVING
⭐   IT PROTECTS YOU FROM AN APPLICATION COMPROMISE IS THE
⭐   MISTAKE, ⭐ and being able to say which is which is what
⭐   separates a real answer from a recited one.
```

# 💬 Interview

**1. ⭐⭐ "Explain envelope encryption and why it exists."**
> ⭐ It exists because you can't send KMS your data: `Encrypt` caps at 4 KB, it's a network call, and
> it has request quotas. ⭐⭐ **So `GenerateDataKey` returns a plaintext key and an encrypted copy of
> it; you encrypt locally with the plaintext key, discard it, and store the encrypted key next to the
> ciphertext. To read, you ask KMS to decrypt the key.** ⭐ Three consequences fall out: encryption is
> local and fast because KMS is touched once per object rather than per byte; ⭐⭐ **you can cache the
> data key and amortise the call, which is what S3 Bucket Keys does; and disabling the CMK makes every
> ciphertext everywhere undecryptable instantly, including backups and copies you can't enumerate.**
> ⭐ That last one is crypto-shredding, ⭐⭐ **and it's the only mechanism that genuinely deletes data
> from places you don't know about — which also makes it the most dangerous button in the account.**

**2. "AWS-managed or customer-managed keys?"**
> ⭐ AWS-managed where encryption is a compliance checkbox — they're free and rotate annually. ⭐⭐
> **Customer-managed the moment I need to answer "who can decrypt this?" or "make this unreadable",
> because AWS-managed keys can't have their policy edited, can't be shared cross-account, and can't be
> disabled — so there's no revocation story at all.** ⭐ The dollar a month is irrelevant; ⭐⭐ **the
> request charges can be relevant, which is a different conversation.** ⭐ And I'd think about
> multi-region keys early, ⭐⭐ **because an encrypted snapshot replicated to another region can't be
> decrypted there without one — which people discover during a DR test if they're lucky and during a
> disaster if they're not.**

**3. ⭐⭐ "What's the most dangerous thing you can do in KMS?"**
> ⭐ Write a key policy that omits the statement enabling IAM and names only a role that later gets
> deleted. ⭐⭐ **The key becomes permanently unmanageable — nobody can edit the policy, because editing
> it needs permission the policy no longer grants anyone. AWS Support cannot help, there's no
> override, and every ciphertext under that key is gone.** ⭐ KMS is the one place AWS inverts the
> normal rule: ⭐⭐ **for S3 an IAM policy alone can grant access, but for KMS an IAM policy grants
> nothing unless the key policy delegates to IAM** — which is why every key policy starts with the
> root statement, and that isn't a weakening, it delegates to IAM which I control and audit.

**4. ⭐⭐ "A data job reading 50 million encrypted S3 objects is slow and expensive. Why?"**
> ⭐ Every object is a KMS `Decrypt` call. ⭐⭐ **Fifty million calls is $150, and worse, you hit the
> per-key request quota — which is shared across the account — and start getting `ThrottlingException`,
> which a 32-thread pool turns into a retry storm that looks like S3 being slow.** ⭐ The fix is S3
> Bucket Keys: one setting, ⭐⭐ **S3 derives a bucket-level key and reuses it, cutting KMS calls by
> about 99%.** ⭐ For application-level encryption the equivalent is the encryption SDK's data key
> cache, ⭐⭐ **where you're trading blast radius for calls and the library makes you state the trade in
> numbers — which is the right way round.**

**5. "Secrets Manager or Parameter Store?"**
> ⭐⭐ **Parameter Store until automatic rotation is an actual requirement** — the standard tier is free
> and SecureString gives you KMS encryption. ⭐ At 200 secrets Secrets Manager is $80 a month, which is
> small, ⭐⭐ **but paying it for secrets nobody rotates is buying a feature you've switched off.**
> ⭐ Where it earns its money is rotation, ⭐⭐ **which is a four-step state machine — create the pending
> value, set it at the source, test a connection with it, then move the label — and that's exactly the
> "two credentials valid at once" requirement made explicit.** ⭐ And Postgres allowing one password per
> role is precisely why AWS ships an alternating-users strategy with two roles, ⭐⭐ **so the constraint
> I'd derive from first principles has a named product feature, which is a good sign the derivation is
> right.**

**6. "Do you fetch a secret per request?"**
> ⭐ No — that's an API call and a charge on the hot path. ⭐⭐ **But I don't read it once at import
> either, because then rotation breaks me until a deploy.** ⭐ The resolution is a TTL cache plus a
> forced refresh on authentication failure: ⭐⭐ **normal operation costs nothing, and when rotation
> happens mid-TTL the auth error triggers exactly one refetch and a retry, instead of a five-minute
> partial outage every time rotation runs.** ⭐ I'd also validate the secret's JSON shape at startup —
> ⭐⭐ **it's a JSON blob, not a password string, and a `KeyError` belongs at boot rather than on the
> first connection at 3 a.m.**

**7. ⭐⭐ "What does encryption at rest actually protect you from?"**
> ⭐ A disk or backup leaving the building, an accidentally-shared snapshot, a decommissioned drive,
> and an auditor's question — which is a legitimate reason even when the threat model is thin. ⭐ Plus
> crypto-shredding. ⭐⭐ **What it does not protect against is a stolen credential: my application can
> decrypt, so an attacker holding my application's role can decrypt.** ⭐ Encryption at rest is a
> control against physical and accidental exposure; ⭐⭐ **IAM is the control against stolen
> credentials** — and knowing which is which is the difference between a real answer and a recited one.
