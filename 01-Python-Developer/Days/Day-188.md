# Day 188

**L-188** · 🐘 **Schema design in practice** — ⭐⭐ normalisation, and the four places you stop on purpose

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: normalisation removes duplication of the *same fact*. It says nothing
> about storing a *different fact that happens to look similar* — and confusing those two is why
> normalised schemas produce wrong invoices.**
>
> ⭐ Everybody can recite third normal form. ⭐⭐ Almost nobody can say where to stop, why an order
> line must copy the price, or what a soft delete does to a unique constraint. Those three are the
> day.

# Part 1 — ⭐ Normal forms, derived from the anomalies they prevent

```
⭐⭐ START WITH THE DISEASE, NOT THE CURE. Here is one bad table:

⭐   orders(id, customer_email, customer_city, product_sku,
⭐          product_name, qty, unit_price)

⭐   ⭐ UPDATE ANOMALY: a customer moves city ⇒ you must update N rows,
⭐        and if you miss one the database now holds two truths.
⭐   ⭐ INSERT ANOMALY: you cannot record a new product until somebody
⭐        orders it — the fact has nowhere to live.
⭐   ⭐ DELETE ANOMALY: deleting the last order for a product ERASES the
⭐        product. ⭐⭐ You lost a fact you never meant to store there.
⭐
⭐⭐ THE THREE FORMS, AS ONE SENTENCE EACH:
⭐   1NF — ⭐ one value per cell. No "tags: 'a,b,c'" strings, no repeating
⭐         columns `phone1, phone2, phone3`.
⭐   2NF — ⭐ no non-key column depends on only PART of a composite key.
⭐         (`order_items(order_id, sku, product_name)` violates it —
⭐         `product_name` depends on `sku` alone.)
⭐   3NF — ⭐ no non-key column depends on ANOTHER non-key column.
⭐         (`customer_city` depends on `customer_email`, not on order id.)
⭐   BCNF — ⭐ the strict version of 3NF; ⭐⭐ in practice, if you reached
⭐         3NF with a surrogate key and honest thinking, you are there.
⭐
⭐⭐ THE MNEMONIC THAT COMPRESSES ALL OF IT:
⭐   "EVERY NON-KEY ATTRIBUTE DEPENDS ON **THE KEY, THE WHOLE KEY, AND
⭐    NOTHING BUT THE KEY.**"
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ the key (1NF) · the whole key (2NF) · nothing but the key (3NF).
```

```
⭐⭐ THE OPERATIONAL TEST, WHICH BEATS RECITING FORMS:
⭐   ⭐ "IF THIS FACT CHANGED, HOW MANY ROWS WOULD I HAVE TO UPDATE?"
⭐        ⭐⭐ More than one ⇒ it is duplicated ⇒ normalise it out.
⭐   ⭐ "COULD TWO ROWS DISAGREE ABOUT SOMETHING THEY SHOULD NOT?"
⭐        ⭐⭐ Yes ⇒ you have an anomaly waiting.
⭐   ⭐⭐ NORMALISE BY DEFAULT. Denormalise with a REASON AND A GUARD.
⭐     ⭐ The reverse — "design for reads first" — produces schemas that
⭐       are wrong within a quarter and unfixable within a year.
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ The four places you deliberately stop

## ⭐⭐ ① The historical snapshot — and this one is not denormalisation at all

```sql
-- ⭐⭐ THE MOST IMPORTANT SCHEMA MISTAKE IN COMMERCIAL SOFTWARE:
CREATE TABLE order_items (
    order_id   bigint REFERENCES orders,
    product_id bigint REFERENCES products
    -- ⭐⭐ "the price is in `products`, joining is the normalised way"
);
-- ⭐⭐ THE PRODUCT'S PRICE CHANGES. ⭐⭐ EVERY HISTORICAL INVOICE IN YOUR
--    DATABASE SILENTLY CHANGES. Refunds are wrong. Reports are wrong.
--    Your accounts do not reconcile and nobody can explain why.

-- ✅ THE CORRECT SCHEMA:
CREATE TABLE order_items (
    order_id        bigint REFERENCES orders,
    product_id      bigint REFERENCES products,   -- ⭐ which product
    product_name    text     NOT NULL,            -- ⭐⭐ AS IT WAS THEN
    unit_price_cents bigint  NOT NULL,            -- ⭐⭐ AS IT WAS THEN
    tax_rate        numeric  NOT NULL             -- ⭐⭐ AS IT WAS THEN
);
```

> ⭐⭐ **This is not "denormalising for performance". `products.price` is *the current price*;
> `order_items.unit_price_cents` is *the price at which this sale happened*. They are two different
> facts that share a number today and will not tomorrow.** ⭐ The same reasoning covers the shipping
> address on an order, the tax rate, the customer's name on an invoice, and the terms of a contract.
> ⭐⭐ The test: **"is this a fact about the entity, or a fact about the event?"**

## ⭐ ② The maintained counter

```sql
ALTER TABLE posts ADD COLUMN comment_count int NOT NULL DEFAULT 0;
-- ⭐ Replaces a COUNT(*) subquery on every row of every listing page.
-- ⭐⭐ AND IT IS A LIE WAITING TO HAPPEN. THE THREE WAYS TO KEEP IT TRUE:
--   ⭐ (a) a TRIGGER  — always correct, invisible, and a debugging tax
--   ⭐ (b) the app, in the SAME TRANSACTION, with `F()`/`col = col + 1`
--   ⭐⭐ (c) BOTH OF THE ABOVE ARE POINTLESS WITHOUT (c):
--          A RECONCILIATION JOB that recomputes the truth periodically
--          and ALERTS ON DRIFT. ⭐⭐ Every denormalised counter drifts.
--          Every one. The only question is whether you find out.
-- ⭐⭐ AND NEVER `count = count + 1` IN PYTHON AFTER A READ (Day 172).
```

## ⭐ ③ The read model / materialised view

```sql
CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW daily_revenue AS
SELECT date_trunc('day', created_at) AS day, sum(total_cents) ...
CREATE UNIQUE INDEX ON daily_revenue (day);   -- ⭐⭐ REQUIRED for the
REFRESH MATERIALIZED VIEW CONCURRENTLY daily_revenue;  -- ⭐⭐ ...this
-- ⭐⭐ WITHOUT `CONCURRENTLY`, REFRESH TAKES AN ACCESS EXCLUSIVE LOCK AND
--    THE DASHBOARD IS DOWN FOR THE DURATION. With it, readers continue —
--    but it needs that unique index and it is slower. ⭐ Day 138's lock
--    lesson, in a place people do not expect it.
-- ⭐ A materialised view is a CACHE with SQL syntax: it has staleness,
--   an invalidation strategy and a refresh cost. Treat it as Day 143.
```

## ⭐ ④ The deliberate array / JSONB column

```
⭐ `tags text[]` or `attributes jsonb` instead of a child table.
⭐   ⭐⭐ LEGITIMATE WHEN: the values are owned entirely by the parent, are
⭐      never queried across parents, and have no attributes of their own.
⭐   ⭐⭐ ILLEGITIMATE THE MOMENT you need "all posts tagged X" without a
⭐      GIN index, a foreign key into the values, or a rename of one tag
⭐      everywhere. ⭐ Day 198 gives JSONB its own day and its own rules.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐ The four relationship shapes, and the two that go wrong

```
⭐ ONE-TO-MANY — the FK lives on the MANY side. Nobody gets this wrong.
⭐ ONE-TO-ONE  — ⭐⭐ usually a sign you should have one table. Legitimate
⭐   for: optional heavy columns you want out of the main heap (TOAST is
⭐   often the better answer), or genuinely different access permissions.

⭐⭐ MANY-TO-MANY — and the mistake is not the join table, it is treating
⭐   the join table as plumbing:
⭐     enrollments(student_id, course_id)
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE MOMENT SOMEONE ASKS "WHEN DID THEY ENROL?" OR "WHAT GRADE?"
⭐     IT WAS ALWAYS AN ENTITY. ⭐ Give it a surrogate key and a name from
⭐     the domain (`Enrollment`), not `student_courses`. ⭐⭐ Most join
⭐     tables become entities; design them as ones that have not yet.
```

```
⭐⭐ HIERARCHIES — four options, and the choice is about READ SHAPE:

⭐ ① ADJACENCY LIST: `parent_id` on the row.
⭐    ⭐ Trivial writes. ⭐⭐ "Give me the whole subtree" = a RECURSIVE CTE:
⭐        WITH RECURSIVE t AS (
⭐          SELECT * FROM cat WHERE id = 7
⭐          UNION ALL SELECT c.* FROM cat c JOIN t ON c.parent_id = t.id
⭐        ) SELECT * FROM t;
⭐    ⭐⭐ FOR 95% OF CASES THIS IS THE RIGHT ANSWER AND PEOPLE SKIP IT
⭐       BECAUSE THEY DO NOT KNOW RECURSIVE CTEs.
⭐ ② CLOSURE TABLE: a row per ancestor-descendant pair.
⭐    ⭐ O(1) subtree reads. ⭐⭐ Writes touch O(depth × subtree) rows.
⭐ ③ MATERIALISED PATH / `ltree`: `'1.4.9'`, indexed with GiST.
⭐    ⭐ Fast reads, ⭐⭐ moving a subtree rewrites every descendant.
⭐ ④ NESTED SETS: ⭐⭐ do not. Any insert renumbers half the table.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ DEFAULT TO ① UNTIL A MEASUREMENT SAYS OTHERWISE.
```

```
⭐⭐ POLYMORPHIC ASSOCIATIONS — "a comment on a post OR a photo OR a
⭐   video". Three designs, and one of them is popular and wrong:

⭐ ⓐ ❌ (commentable_type text, commentable_id bigint)
⭐    ⭐⭐ NO FOREIGN KEY IS POSSIBLE. The database cannot enforce that
⭐       `commentable_id = 55` exists — so orphans accumulate silently and
⭐       nothing tells you. ⭐ Rails made this normal; it is still wrong.
⭐ ⓑ ✅ NULLABLE FK PER TYPE + a CHECK that exactly one is set:
⭐       post_id, photo_id, video_id
⭐       CHECK (num_nonnulls(post_id, photo_id, video_id) = 1)
⭐    ⭐⭐ FULL REFERENTIAL INTEGRITY. ⭐ Cost: a migration per new type,
⭐       which is honest — a new type IS a schema change.
⭐ ⓒ ✅ A SHARED PARENT TABLE (`commentable(id)`), each concrete table
⭐       referencing it. ⭐ Clean, and one extra join everywhere.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ CHOOSE ⓑ UNDER ~5 TYPES, ⓒ ABOVE. ⓐ ONLY IF YOU ARE WILLING TO
⭐     WRITE THE ORPHAN-DETECTION JOB YOURSELF — and you will not.
```

```
⭐⭐ EAV (entity-attribute-value): `(entity_id, attr_name, value text)`.
⭐   ⭐ Every query becomes a self-join per attribute. No types. No
⭐     constraints. No usable indexes. ⭐⭐ IT IS A DATABASE INSIDE YOUR
⭐     DATABASE, WITH NONE OF THE FEATURES.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ IF THE ATTRIBUTES ARE GENUINELY USER-DEFINED, USE `jsonb` — it
⭐     is EAV with types, indexes and operators. ⭐ EAV is what people
⭐     built before JSONB existed; there is no reason to choose it now.
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ Soft deletes, honestly

```
⭐ `deleted_at timestamptz NULL` — added in an afternoon, paid for
⭐   forever. ⭐⭐ HERE IS THE FULL BILL:

⭐ 1. ⭐⭐ YOUR UNIQUE CONSTRAINT IS NOW WRONG.
⭐    UNIQUE(email) means a user who deletes their account can never
⭐    sign up again. ⭐ FIX: a PARTIAL unique index —
⭐       CREATE UNIQUE INDEX ON users (email) WHERE deleted_at IS NULL;
⭐    ⭐⭐ Almost nobody does this until the first support ticket.
⭐ 2. ⭐⭐ EVERY QUERY IN THE APPLICATION IS NOW WRONG BY DEFAULT.
⭐    One forgotten `WHERE deleted_at IS NULL` shows a deleted record.
⭐    ⭐ Mitigations: a default manager (Django), a `with_loader_criteria`
⭐      (SQLAlchemy), or a VIEW that everything reads instead of the
⭐      table. ⭐⭐ THE VIEW IS THE MOST ROBUST AND THE LEAST USED.
⭐ 3. ⭐⭐ FOREIGN KEYS DO NOT UNDERSTAND IT. A child can reference a
⭐    "deleted" parent and the database is perfectly happy.
⭐ 4. ⭐⭐ CASCADE IS NOW YOUR JOB. Soft-deleting a user leaves their
⭐    orders visible unless you wrote that code. Nobody wrote that code.
⭐ 5. ⭐⭐ THE TABLE ONLY GROWS. Indexes carry dead rows forever, and your
⭐    "small" table is 60% tombstones.
⭐ 6. ⭐⭐ AND THE ONE THAT IS A LEGAL PROBLEM: "DELETE MY ACCOUNT" UNDER
⭐    GDPR/CCPA MEANS ERASURE. A soft delete is NOT a deletion, and
⭐    "we set a flag" is not a defence.

⭐⭐ THE ALTERNATIVES, RANKED:
⭐   ⭐ (a) HARD DELETE + AN ARCHIVE TABLE — the deleted row is MOVED.
⭐        ⭐⭐ Constraints keep working, the hot table stays small, and
⭐        "restore" is an INSERT. ⭐ This is the right default.
⭐   ⭐ (b) A `status` COLUMN when the states are DOMAIN states
⭐        (`cancelled`, `archived`) — ⭐⭐ because then it is not a delete
⭐        at all, it is a lifecycle, and modelling it as one is honest.
⭐   ⭐ (c) SOFT DELETE — when you genuinely need instant undo on a table
⭐        with few relationships. ⭐⭐ WITH THE PARTIAL UNIQUE INDEX AND
⭐        THE VIEW, ON DAY ONE.
```

# Part 5 — ⭐ Keys, and the schema smells checklist

```
⭐⭐ NATURAL vs SURROGATE KEY — the argument settled in one line:
⭐   ⭐ A natural key (email, ISBN, VAT number) is a BUSINESS VALUE, and
⭐     ⭐⭐ BUSINESS VALUES CHANGE. A surrogate key is meaningless and
⭐     therefore never needs to change.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ SURROGATE PRIMARY KEY + A UNIQUE CONSTRAINT ON THE NATURAL
⭐     KEY. You get stability AND the integrity rule. ⭐ The only common
⭐     exception is a pure join table, where the composite key is right.

⭐⭐ COMPOSITE PRIMARY KEYS: fine in the database, ⭐ awkward in ORMs and
⭐   in URLs, and ⭐⭐ they propagate — every child FK carries all columns.
```

```
⭐⭐ THE SCHEMA REVIEW CHECKLIST — run it on your capstone tonight:

⭐   ☐ Any column that would need updating in >1 row if a fact changed?
⭐   ☐ Any historical/priced/addressed row that JOINS for a value it
⭐       should have SNAPSHOT? ⭐⭐ ← the expensive one
⭐   ☐ Any nullable column that has no meaningful "unknown" case?
⭐   ☐ Any `_type`/`_id` polymorphic pair with no FK?
⭐   ☐ Any comma-separated string in a text column?
⭐   ☐ Any `data jsonb` holding fields you filter on every request?
⭐   ☐ Any unique constraint that ignores `deleted_at`?
⭐   ☐ Any foreign key with no index on the referencing side?
⭐   ☐ Any counter with no reconciliation job?
⭐   ☐ Any `timestamp` that should be `timestamptz`?
⭐   ☐ Any money column that is not `numeric` or integer cents?
⭐   ☐ Any table with no primary key at all? ⭐⭐ (It happens. Logical
⭐       replication refuses to replicate updates on such a table.)
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Reciting normal forms without the anomalies | Learn the update/insert/delete anomaly each one prevents. |
| Joining to `products` for an order's price | The price at sale time is a different fact. Snapshot it on the line. |
| Denormalising "for performance" before measuring | Normalise by default; denormalise with a reason and a guard. |
| A counter column with no reconciliation job | Every denormalised counter drifts. The only question is whether you find out. |
| `REFRESH MATERIALIZED VIEW` without `CONCURRENTLY` | ACCESS EXCLUSIVE — the dashboard is down for the duration. |
| Treating a join table as plumbing | Most become entities. Give it a domain name and a surrogate key. |
| Nested sets for a hierarchy | Any insert renumbers half the table. Adjacency list plus a recursive CTE. |
| `(commentable_type, commentable_id)` | No foreign key is possible; orphans accumulate silently. |
| EAV for user-defined attributes | It's a database with no types, constraints or indexes. Use `jsonb`. |
| Soft delete with an unchanged unique constraint | A deleted user can never sign up again. Partial unique index. |
| Relying on every query to remember `deleted_at IS NULL` | Use a default manager, loader criteria, or read from a view. |
| Believing a soft delete satisfies "delete my data" | It doesn't. Erasure means erasure. |
| A natural key as the primary key | Business values change. Surrogate PK plus a unique constraint. |
| A table with no primary key | Logical replication can't replicate updates to it. |

## Interview questions

**Q: Why does an order line store the product's price?**

> Because they're different facts. `products.price` means "what this costs today"; the line's
> `unit_price_cents` means "what this sale happened at". They agree on the day of the order and
> diverge the first time someone runs a promotion — and if you joined, every historical invoice in
> the database silently changes with them. Refunds compute wrong, revenue reports move retroactively,
> and finance can't reconcile. People frame this as denormalising for performance, which misses the
> point entirely: normalisation eliminates duplication of the *same* fact, and this isn't duplication
> at all. The general test I use is "is this a fact about the entity, or a fact about the event?" —
> and it covers the shipping address on an order, the tax rate, the customer name on an invoice, and
> the terms on a contract, all of which have the same failure mode.

**Q: When would you denormalise?**

> Four cases, and each carries an obligation. Historical snapshots, which as I said aren't really
> denormalisation. Maintained counters, where the read pattern makes a `COUNT(*)` per row untenable —
> and the obligation is a reconciliation job that recomputes the truth and alerts on drift, because
> every counter drifts eventually. Read models or materialised views for reporting, which are caches
> with SQL syntax and need the same staleness thinking as any cache, plus `REFRESH CONCURRENTLY` or
> the dashboard goes down during refresh. And arrays or JSONB when values are owned entirely by the
> parent and never queried across parents. What I wouldn't do is design for reads first — that
> produces schemas that are wrong within a quarter, because you optimised for the queries you had
> before you knew the domain.

**Q: What's wrong with a polymorphic `commentable_type`/`commentable_id` pair?**

> You can't put a foreign key on it, so the database cannot enforce that the referenced row exists.
> Orphans accumulate quietly — you delete a photo, its comments stay, nothing errors, and six months
> later a page crashes on a null. The two designs that keep integrity are a nullable foreign key per
> type with a `CHECK (num_nonnulls(...) = 1)`, which is my choice under about five types, and a shared
> parent table that each concrete type references, which is better above that. The objection to the
> first is that adding a type means a migration, but I think that's honest — a new commentable type
> genuinely *is* a schema change, and pretending otherwise is what got us the version with no
> integrity.

**Q: Soft delete or hard delete?**

> Hard delete plus an archive table, as the default. Soft delete looks like an afternoon's work and
> then bills you forever: your unique constraint on email now stops a deleted user from ever signing
> up again unless you switched to a partial unique index; every query in the codebase is wrong unless
> it remembers the predicate; foreign keys don't understand the flag, so children can reference
> deleted parents; cascading becomes your job and nobody writes that code; the table only grows; and
> under GDPR "delete my account" means erasure, so a flag isn't a defence. Moving the row to an
> archive table keeps constraints working, keeps the hot table small, and makes restore an insert.
> The one place I'd still use a flag is where the states are genuinely domain states — cancelled,
> archived, suspended — and then it isn't a delete at all, it's a lifecycle, and modelling it as one
> is the honest thing.

**Q: How do you model a category tree?**

> Adjacency list — a `parent_id` column — plus a recursive CTE for subtree queries, until a
> measurement says otherwise. Writes are trivial, there's no invariant to maintain, and the recursive
> CTE is one query rather than the N+1 loop most people write. The reason people reach for closure
> tables or materialised paths first is usually that they don't know recursive CTEs exist. If reads
> genuinely dominate and the tree is deep, a closure table gives O(1) subtree reads at the cost of
> writes touching depth times subtree size, and `ltree` gives fast reads but rewrites every descendant
> when you move a subtree. Nested sets I'd avoid outright — every insert renumbers half the table.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes: "How do you design a schema?"**

Open by normalising by default and saying why — anomalies, not aesthetics — with one concrete
example of an update anomaly. Then get quickly to where you stop, because that's where the judgement
is. Lead with the snapshot, and lead with the consequence rather than the rule: if the order line
joins for the price, changing a price rewrites history and finance can't reconcile. Then say the
thing that reframes it — that isn't denormalisation, it's two different facts, one about the entity
and one about the event. Then counters with their reconciliation obligation, and read models as
caches with SQL syntax. Spend your last thirty seconds on soft deletes, because it's where most
schemas are quietly broken: **the unique constraint stops the deleted user signing up again, every
query is wrong unless it remembers the predicate, foreign keys don't understand the flag, and under
GDPR a flag isn't a deletion.** Close with the alternative — hard delete plus an archive table.
