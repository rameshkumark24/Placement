# Day 343 🗳️

**P-343** · 🗳️ **Consensus** — Raft intuition, quorums, and ⭐⭐ why you should not build one

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: consensus is how a group of machines agrees on one value when any of them
> may crash and none can tell "dead" from "slow" — and the single most useful thing it gives an
> application developer is not agreement, it is the fencing token, which is the only thing that makes
> a distributed lock actually safe.**

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ What problem consensus solves

```
⭐⭐ THE UNDERLYING FACT, AND IT HAS APPEARED THREE TIMES NOW:
⭐   ***YOU CANNOT DISTINGUISH A CRASHED NODE FROM A SLOW ONE.***
⭐   ⭐ Day 328's failover detection. ⭐ Day 330's proof.
⭐   ⭐⭐ AND FLP (1985): IN AN ASYNCHRONOUS SYSTEM WITH EVEN ONE
⭐     FAULTY PROCESS, ***NO*** DETERMINISTIC ALGORITHM
⭐     GUARANTEES CONSENSUS.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ SO REAL ALGORITHMS ADD ***TIMEOUTS*** — they give up
⭐     guaranteed termination and get "terminates in practice,
⭐     almost always". ⭐ Raft's randomised election timeout is
⭐     exactly this concession, and knowing that is worth more
⭐     than reciting the phases.

⭐⭐ WHAT CONSENSUS BUYS YOU — FOUR THINGS:
⭐   ① ⭐ ***LEADER ELECTION*** — exactly one primary (Day 328).
⭐   ② ⭐ ***ATOMIC BROADCAST*** — a totally-ordered replicated
⭐        log, which is really the same problem.
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ ***DISTRIBUTED LOCKS THAT ARE ACTUALLY SAFE*** — Part
⭐        5, and the reason this day matters to you.
⭐   ④ ⭐ ***CONFIGURATION AND MEMBERSHIP*** — everyone agrees
⭐        who is in the cluster.

⭐⭐ AND THE THREE GUARANTEES ANY CONSENSUS PROTOCOL MUST GIVE:
⭐   ⭐ AGREEMENT (no two nodes decide differently) ·
⭐   ⭐ VALIDITY (the value decided was proposed by someone) ·
⭐   ⭐ TERMINATION (it eventually decides — the one FLP takes
⭐     away, and the one timeouts buy back).
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ Quorums, and the arithmetic

```
⭐⭐ ***A MAJORITY QUORUM IS ⌊N/2⌋ + 1.***
⭐   ⭐ N=3 ⇒ 2. N=5 ⇒ 3. N=7 ⇒ 4.
⭐⭐ THE PROPERTY THAT MAKES IT WORK: ***ANY TWO MAJORITIES
⭐   INTERSECT IN AT LEAST ONE NODE.*** ⭐ So a new leader's
⭐   quorum must contain someone who knew about the last
⭐   committed write. ⭐⭐ THAT ONE SENTENCE IS THE WHOLE
⭐   INTUITION.

⭐⭐ FAULT TOLERANCE: N nodes tolerate ⌊(N−1)/2⌋ failures.
⭐   ⭐ 3 ⇒ 1. ⭐ 5 ⇒ 2. ⭐ 7 ⇒ 3.

⭐⭐ THE THREE CONSEQUENCES PEOPLE MISS:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ EVEN NUMBERS ARE WASTEFUL. ⭐ 4 nodes tolerate 1
⭐        failure — ***the same as 3*** — while costing more and
⭐        making a partition more likely to split evenly.
⭐        ⭐⭐ ALWAYS USE AN ODD NUMBER.
⭐   ② ⭐⭐ MORE NODES = MORE FAULT TOLERANCE AND ***WORSE
⭐        LATENCY***. Every write waits for a majority, so a
⭐        7-node cluster waits for the 4th-fastest. ⭐ 5 is the
⭐        usual sweet spot; 3 for most things.
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ TWO DATACENTRES CANNOT FORM A MAJORITY (Day 330).
⭐        ⭐ 2+2 splits evenly; 3+2 means losing the site with 3
⭐        kills you. ⭐⭐ THE ANSWER IS THREE FAILURE DOMAINS,
⭐        AND THE THIRD CAN BE A TINY WITNESS THAT HOLDS NO DATA
⭐        AND ONLY VOTES.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Raft, in the form you can actually explain

```
⭐⭐ RAFT DECOMPOSES CONSENSUS INTO THREE PROBLEMS, AND THAT
⭐   DECOMPOSITION ***IS*** ITS CONTRIBUTION — Paxos does the
⭐   same job and is famously hard to reason about.

⭐⭐ ① ***LEADER ELECTION***
⭐   ⭐ Every node is FOLLOWER, CANDIDATE or LEADER.
⭐   ⭐ Time is divided into ***TERMS*** — ⭐⭐ A MONOTONICALLY
⭐     INCREASING NUMBER, WHICH IS THE LOGICAL CLOCK THAT MAKES
⭐     EVERYTHING ELSE WORK.
⭐   ⭐ No heartbeat within a ***randomised*** timeout
⭐     (150–300 ms) ⇒ become CANDIDATE, increment the term,
⭐     request votes.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ EACH NODE VOTES ONCE PER TERM ⇒ AT MOST ONE LEADER
⭐     PER TERM. ⭐ That is the safety property, and it is
⭐     enforced by a single rule.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ RANDOMISED TIMEOUTS ARE WHAT PREVENT SPLIT VOTES
⭐     REPEATING FOREVER — ⭐ it is jitter (Day 339), doing the
⭐     same job it always does.

⭐⭐ ② ***LOG REPLICATION***
⭐   ⭐ Clients send to the leader; it appends and replicates.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AN ENTRY IS ***COMMITTED*** ONCE A MAJORITY HAS
⭐     STORED IT. ⭐ Only then is it applied and acknowledged.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ THE LEADER NEVER OVERWRITES OR DELETES ITS OWN
⭐     ENTRIES; FOLLOWERS' CONFLICTING ENTRIES ARE TRUNCATED TO
⭐     MATCH. ⭐ Logs converge by forcing them to.

⭐⭐ ③ ***SAFETY — THE ELECTION RESTRICTION***
⭐   ⭐⭐ A NODE WILL ONLY VOTE FOR A CANDIDATE WHOSE LOG IS AT
⭐     LEAST AS UP TO DATE AS ITS OWN.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ COMBINED WITH QUORUM INTERSECTION, THIS GUARANTEES A
⭐     NEW LEADER ALREADY CONTAINS EVERY COMMITTED ENTRY. ⭐ No
⭐     committed entry is ever lost. ⭐⭐ THIS IS THE PART TO SAY
⭐     IF YOU ONLY GET ONE SENTENCE.

⭐⭐ THE PIECES WORTH NAMING FOR CREDIBILITY: ⭐ log compaction
⭐   via snapshots (the log cannot grow forever); ⭐ joint
⭐   consensus for membership changes (⭐⭐ NAIVELY CHANGING
⭐   MEMBERSHIP CAN PRODUCE TWO DISJOINT MAJORITIES AND
⭐   THEREFORE TWO LEADERS); ⭐ and ⭐⭐ READ LINEARIZABILITY
⭐   NEEDING EITHER A QUORUM READ OR A LEADER LEASE — because
⭐   a leader that has been partitioned away does not know it
⭐   yet.
```

# Part 5 — ⭐⭐ Distributed locks and the fencing token

```
⭐⭐ THE NAIVE LOCK, WHICH IS WHAT MOST PEOPLE SHIP:
⭐     `SET lock:resource <uuid> NX EX 30`
⭐   ⭐ ✔ Better than nothing. ⭐ ✔ Correct value check on
⭐     release (via Lua, not GET-then-DEL).
⭐   ⭐ ✘ ⭐⭐ AND IT IS ***NOT SAFE***, FOR A REASON THAT HAS
⭐     NOTHING TO DO WITH REDIS:

⭐⭐ THE FAILURE — ⭐ learn this sequence, it is the whole point:
⭐   ① Client A acquires the lock, TTL 30 s.
⭐   ② ⭐⭐ CLIENT A STALLS — A 45-SECOND GC PAUSE, A SUSPENDED
⭐      CONTAINER, A NETWORK BLIP. ⭐ It does not know.
⭐   ③ The lock expires. ⭐ Client B acquires it, legitimately.
⭐   ④ ⭐⭐ CLIENT A WAKES UP ***STILL BELIEVING IT HOLDS THE
⭐      LOCK*** AND WRITES.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ TWO WRITERS. THE LOCK PROVIDED NO PROTECTION AT ALL,
⭐     AND NO TTL VALUE FIXES THIS — you cannot bound a pause.

⭐⭐ ***THE FENCING TOKEN*** — the fix, and it is beautiful:
⭐   ⭐ The lock service returns a ***monotonically increasing
⭐     number*** with each grant. A gets 33, B gets 34.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ EVERY WRITE CARRIES ITS TOKEN, AND THE ***STORAGE***
⭐     REJECTS ANY TOKEN LOWER THAN THE HIGHEST IT HAS SEEN.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ A'S LATE WRITE ARRIVES WITH 33, IS REJECTED, AND
⭐     SAFETY IS PRESERVED ***WITHOUT*** ANYONE HAVING TO
⭐     DETECT THE PAUSE.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ AND THE CATCH THAT MAKES THIS A REAL DESIGN
⭐     CONSTRAINT: ***THE RESOURCE MUST PARTICIPATE.*** ⭐ If
⭐     you are writing to S3 or calling a third party that
⭐     cannot check a token, ⭐⭐ FENCING IS UNAVAILABLE AND
⭐     YOU MUST MAKE THE OPERATION IDEMPOTENT INSTEAD.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ In Postgres it is one line: `UPDATE … WHERE
⭐     fence_token < :token` — ⭐⭐ WHICH IS DAY 310'S
⭐     CONDITIONAL UPDATE, WEARING ITS DISTRIBUTED-SYSTEMS HAT.

⭐⭐ AND THE HONEST HIERARCHY TO STATE:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ BEST: DON'T NEED A LOCK. ⭐ A conditional `UPDATE`
⭐     in the database is atomic and needs no external service
⭐     at all (Days 311, 316).
⭐   ② ⭐ Redis lock ⇒ ⭐⭐ EFFICIENCY ONLY. ⭐ "Usually only one
⭐     worker sends the email" — a duplicate is wasteful, not
⭐     wrong.
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ CORRECTNESS ⇒ etcd/ZooKeeper with a fencing token
⭐     AND a resource that checks it.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ AND SAY THE REDLOCK CAVEAT IF ASKED: MULTI-NODE
⭐     REDLOCK IS DISPUTED FOR CORRECTNESS PURPOSES BECAUSE IT
⭐     RELIES ON BOUNDED CLOCK DRIFT AND BOUNDED PAUSES —
⭐     ⭐ neither of which you have.
```

# Part 6 — ⭐ Split brain, leases, and time

```
⭐⭐ ***SPLIT BRAIN***: two nodes both believe they are leader.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Divergent writes, and a manual merge nobody wants.

⭐⭐ THE THREE DEFENCES:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ QUORUM. ⭐ Only a majority can elect, and there is
⭐        only one majority — ⭐ so the minority side simply
⭐        cannot make progress. ⭐⭐ THIS IS WHY CP SYSTEMS STOP
⭐        SERVING RATHER THAN DIVERGE (Day 330).
⭐   ② ⭐ ***LEASES***: leadership is time-bounded and must be
⭐        renewed. ⭐⭐ A LEADER THAT CANNOT RENEW MUST ***STEP
⭐        DOWN BEFORE ITS LEASE EXPIRES***, not after —
⭐        ⭐ which means it must stop serving slightly early to
⭐        be safe. ⭐⭐ THAT ASYMMETRY IS THE ENTIRE SAFETY
⭐        ARGUMENT FOR LEASES.
⭐   ③ ⭐ ***FENCING / STONITH***: make the old leader unable to
⭐        write, rather than asking it to stop (Day 328).

⭐⭐ AND THE CLOCK PROBLEM UNDERNEATH ALL OF IT:
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ NEVER USE WALL-CLOCK TIME (`time.time()`) FOR
⭐     TIMEOUTS OR LEASES. ⭐ NTP steps, leap seconds and VM
⭐     migrations move it ***backwards***.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ USE `time.monotonic()`. ⭐ In Python this is a
⭐     one-word fix that prevents a class of bug that is
⭐     essentially impossible to reproduce.
⭐   ⭐ Clock skew between machines is routinely 10–100 ms.
⭐     ⭐⭐ WHICH IS WHY "LAST WRITE WINS" BY TIMESTAMP LOSES
⭐     DATA (Day 330), AND WHY SPANNER NEEDED ATOMIC CLOCKS TO
⭐     MAKE TIME-BASED ORDERING SAFE.
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ Building your own consensus | Subtle in ways tests don't reveal. Even mature implementations have long bug histories |
| ⭐⭐ A Redis lock used for correctness | A GC pause outlives the TTL and two clients write. No TTL value fixes it |
| ⭐⭐ Fencing token with a resource that ignores it | The token only works if the storage rejects lower ones |
| ⭐ Even numbers of nodes | 4 tolerates the same failures as 3, costs more, and splits evenly |
| ⭐⭐ Two datacentres for a quorum cluster | No majority exists. Three failure domains, even if the third is a witness |
| ⭐ More nodes for "more reliability" | Every write waits for a majority; 7 nodes waits for the fourth-fastest |
| ⭐⭐ `time.time()` for leases or timeouts | NTP steps and VM migrations move it backwards. Use `time.monotonic()` |
| ⭐ Last-write-wins by timestamp | Clock skew is 10–100 ms routinely. It silently keeps the older write |
| ⭐ Assuming a leader knows it's still leader | A partitioned leader finds out late. Quorum reads or a lease |
| ⭐⭐ Naive membership changes | Can produce two disjoint majorities and two leaders. Joint consensus |
| ⭐ Reaching for consensus when a conditional UPDATE would do | The database is already a linearizable register for one row |

## Interview questions

**1. "Explain Raft."**
> ⭐ Three parts. **Leader election**: randomised timeouts, terms as a logical clock, one vote per node
> per term so there's at most one leader per term. **Log replication**: the leader appends and
> replicates, and an entry is committed once a majority has stored it. ⭐⭐ **Safety**: you only vote for
> a candidate whose log is at least as up to date as yours — **combined with quorum intersection, that
> guarantees a new leader already holds every committed entry, so no committed entry is ever lost.**
> ⭐ Randomised timeouts are jitter, doing the same job as everywhere else — stopping split votes from
> repeating forever.

**2. "How many nodes and why?"**
> ⭐ Odd, and usually three or five. Majority is floor(N/2)+1 and tolerance is floor((N−1)/2), ⭐⭐ so
> **four tolerates exactly the same single failure as three while costing more and splitting evenly
> under partition.** ⭐ More nodes means more tolerance and worse latency, because every write waits for
> a majority — seven waits for the fourth-fastest. ⭐⭐ And they must span **three** failure domains,
> since two datacentres can't form a majority — the third can be a witness holding no data.

**3. "Is a Redis lock safe?"**
> ⭐ For efficiency, yes; for correctness, no. ⭐⭐ **A forty-five-second GC pause outlives a
> thirty-second TTL: the lock expires, another client legitimately takes it, and the first wakes up
> still believing it holds the lock and writes.** ⭐ No TTL value fixes that, because you can't bound a
> pause. ⭐⭐ The fix is a **fencing token** — a monotonically increasing number per grant, carried on
> every write, with **the storage rejecting anything lower than the highest it has seen.** ⭐ And the
> constraint worth naming: if the resource can't check tokens — S3, a third-party API — fencing isn't
> available and you need idempotency instead.

**4. "What is split brain and how do you prevent it?"**
> ⭐ Two nodes both believing they're leader, producing divergent writes and a manual merge nobody
> wants. ⭐ Three defences: quorum, since only one majority can exist and the minority simply can't make
> progress — that's why CP systems stop serving rather than diverge; ⭐⭐ leases, where **a leader that
> can't renew must step down *before* expiry, not after**, which means stopping slightly early, and
> that asymmetry is the whole safety argument; ⭐ and fencing, which makes the old leader *unable* to
> write rather than asking it to stop.

**5. "Would you build a consensus system?"**
> ⭐⭐ No. I'd use etcd, ZooKeeper, or a database that already embeds Raft — implementations are subtle
> in ways testing doesn't reveal, and even the mature ones have long public bug histories written by
> specialists and verified by Jepsen. ⭐ The stronger answer names the property I want rather than the
> algorithm: ⭐⭐ usually a **fencing token**, or "exactly one primary, with Patroni doing the election".

**6. "Do you actually need consensus for this?"**
> ⭐ Usually no, and I'd say so. ⭐⭐ One writer per key is a conditional `UPDATE` — **the database is
> already a linearizable register for that row**, with no external service at all. ⭐ One runner of a
> nightly job across replicas is a Postgres advisory lock. ⭐ Consensus is for "which node is the
> primary" and for cluster membership — and even there I'd use Patroni rather than writing it.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes.** Start from the fact that has now appeared three times: **you cannot distinguish a
crashed node from a slow one** — FLP says no deterministic algorithm guarantees consensus in an
asynchronous system with one faulty process, so real algorithms add timeouts and trade guaranteed
termination for terminating in practice. Give Raft in three parts, and make the third the memorable
one: randomised election timeouts with one vote per term give at most one leader; a majority commits an
entry; **and you only vote for a candidate whose log is at least as complete as yours, which combined
with quorum intersection guarantees the new leader already holds every committed entry.** Give the
sizing arithmetic and its consequences — **odd numbers always, because four tolerates the same failures
as three; more nodes means more tolerance and worse latency; and two datacentres cannot form a majority,
so you need a third failure domain even if it's a witness that holds no data.** Then pivot to the part
that matters to an application developer: **a Redis lock is not safe for correctness, because a
forty-five-second pause outlives a thirty-second TTL and two clients write** — and no TTL fixes it,
because you can't bound a pause. **The fix is a fencing token: a monotonically increasing number per
grant, carried on every write, with the storage rejecting anything lower** — which in Postgres is one
`WHERE` clause. Close on judgement: **use etcd, never build one**, name the property you want rather
than the algorithm, and be willing to say that most of the time a conditional `UPDATE` is already all
the consensus you need.
