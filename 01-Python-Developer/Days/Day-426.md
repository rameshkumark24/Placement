# Day 426 ⚖️

**P-426** · ⚖️ **LLM-as-judge** — ⭐⭐ its four failure modes, and the humans that keep it honest

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: a judge model is a measuring instrument, and an uncalibrated instrument is
> worse than no instrument — because it produces a number that looks like evidence, and a team will
> ship a regression against a confident 4.2 that they would never have shipped against "we don't
> know."**

# Part 1 — When you actually need one

```
⭐ ⭐⭐ ONLY WHEN DAY 425's GRADERS ① AND ② CANNOT DO IT.
⭐   ⭐ ✅ Legitimate: ⭐ "is this summary faithful to the source?"
⭐     ⭐ "is this answer helpful?" ⭐ "is the tone appropriate?"
⭐     ⭐ "did it follow all six instructions?"
⭐   ⭐ ❌ Not legitimate — ⭐⭐ AND THIS IS WHERE MOST JUDGE USAGE
⭐     ACTUALLY IS: ⭐ "is the JSON valid" (parse it), ⭐ "is the
⭐     category one of four" (check the set), ⭐ "is it under 100
⭐     words" (count), ⭐⭐ "IS THE QUOTE IN THE SOURCE" (`in`).

⭐⭐ ⇒ SO THE FIRST MOVE IS ALWAYS TO ***SHRINK THE JUDGE'S
⭐   JOB***: ⭐ decompose the quality question into the parts code
⭐   can check and the residue that genuinely needs judgement.
⭐   ⭐⭐ A JUDGE ASSESSING ONE NARROW DIMENSION IS FAR MORE
⭐   RELIABLE THAN ONE ASSESSING "QUALITY", ⭐ and much cheaper.
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ The four failure modes

```
⭐⭐ ① ***POSITION BIAS.*** ⭐ In a pairwise comparison, models
⭐    systematically prefer the response shown ***first*** (some
⭐    prefer the second — ⭐⭐ THE POINT IS IT IS NOT NEUTRAL).
⭐    ⭐ Measured effects can be large — enough to reverse a
⭐      verdict.
⭐    ⭐⭐ ⇒ FIX: RUN EVERY COMPARISON ***BOTH WAYS*** AND COUNT A
⭐      WIN ONLY IF IT SURVIVES THE SWAP. ⭐ Disagreement between
⭐      the two orders is itself a signal: ⭐⭐ IT MEANS "TOO CLOSE
⭐      TO CALL", WHICH IS INFORMATION YOU WANT.

⭐⭐ ② ***VERBOSITY BIAS.*** ⭐ Longer answers score higher, ⭐⭐ EVEN
⭐    WHEN THEY ADD NOTHING — ⭐ a preference-tuning artefact
⭐    (Day 417) inherited by the judge.
⭐    ⭐⭐ ⇒ THE DANGER: OPTIMISING A PROMPT AGAINST A JUDGE SLOWLY
⭐      MAKES YOUR OUTPUTS LONGER AND MORE EXPENSIVE WHILE THE
⭐      SCORE CLIMBS. ⭐ You have optimised the metric, not the
⭐      product (Goodhart).
⭐    ⭐ ⇒ Fix: put length in the rubric explicitly, ⭐⭐ AND TRACK
⭐      OUTPUT TOKENS ALONGSIDE THE SCORE (DAY 425).

⭐⭐ ③ ***SELF-PREFERENCE.*** ⭐ A model tends to rate its own
⭐    family's output higher. ⭐⭐ ⇒ IF YOU ARE CHOOSING BETWEEN
⭐    PROVIDERS, DO NOT LET ONE OF THEM JUDGE. ⭐ Use a third
⭐    model, or humans.

⭐⭐ ④ ***SYCOPHANCY AND ANCHORING.*** ⭐ Day 417's artefact,
⭐    arriving as a measurement error: ⭐ tell the judge "this was
⭐    written by an expert" and scores rise; ⭐⭐ ASK "IS THERE A
⭐    PROBLEM WITH THIS?" AND IT WILL FIND ONE, BECAUSE FINDING
⭐    ONE IS THE PLAUSIBLE CONTINUATION OF THAT QUESTION.
⭐    ⭐ ⇒ Fix: neutral framing, ⭐⭐ NO METADATA ABOUT THE SOURCE,
⭐      AND NEVER A LEADING QUESTION.

⭐ ⭐ Plus two smaller ones worth knowing: ⭐⭐ SCORE COMPRESSION
⭐   (everything lands 6–8 on a 1–10 scale, so the scale has
⭐   three usable values) ⭐ and ***format bias*** (bullet points
⭐   score above prose regardless of content).
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Building one that works

```
⭐ ① ⭐⭐ ***BINARY OR THREE-POINT, NEVER 1–10.*** ⭐ A model cannot
⭐    reliably distinguish a 6 from a 7, ⭐⭐ AND NEITHER CAN YOUR
⭐    HUMAN LABELLERS, SO THE EXTRA RESOLUTION IS NOISE THAT
⭐    LOOKS LIKE SIGNAL. ⭐ `pass` / `fail`, or
⭐    `good`/`acceptable`/`bad`.
⭐ ② ⭐⭐ ***ONE DIMENSION PER CALL.*** ⭐ Faithfulness, then
⭐    helpfulness, then tone — ⭐⭐ SEPARATELY. ⭐ A single "quality"
⭐    score blends dimensions that move independently and hides
⭐    which one regressed.
⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ ***A RUBRIC WITH EXPLICIT CRITERIA AND EXAMPLES***, ⭐ not
⭐    "rate the quality". ⭐ The rubric is a prompt and every rule
⭐    from Day 422 applies.
⭐ ④ ⭐⭐ ***REASONING BEFORE THE VERDICT*** (DAY 423's FIELD
⭐    ORDER) — ⭐ and it also gives you something to read when you
⭐    disagree with the judge.
⭐ ⑤ ⭐ ***GIVE IT THE REFERENCE*** where one exists. ⭐⭐ JUDGING
⭐    AGAINST A GROUND TRUTH IS FAR MORE RELIABLE THAN JUDGING IN
⭐    THE ABSTRACT.
⭐ ⑥ ⭐ ***temperature=0*** (Day 418).
```

```python
class Verdict(BaseModel):
    # ⭐⭐ REASONING FIRST — conditions the verdict (Day 423)
    unsupported_claims: list[str] = Field(
        description="Claims in the ANSWER not supported by the "
                    "SOURCE. Quote each one verbatim. Empty if none.")
    verdict: Literal["faithful", "unfaithful"]

JUDGE = """You check whether an ANSWER is faithful to a SOURCE.

Faithful means: every factual claim in the ANSWER is stated in or
directly entailed by the SOURCE. Extra detail not in the SOURCE is
unfaithful, even if it is true in the real world.
Style, length and tone are NOT part of this judgement.

<source>{{ source }}</source>
<answer>{{ answer }}</answer>

List unsupported claims first, then give the verdict.
"""
# ⭐⭐ NOTE WHAT THIS RUBRIC DOES: it names ONE dimension, defines
# ⭐   it operationally, EXPLICITLY EXCLUDES the dimensions that
# ⭐   cause verbosity and format bias, and demands evidence
# ⭐   before a verdict. ⭐⭐ THAT IS FOUR OF THE FIXES FROM PART 2
# ⭐   IN ONE PROMPT.
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ Calibrating the judge, which is the whole point

```
⭐⭐ ***A JUDGE YOU HAVE NOT MEASURED AGAINST HUMANS IS A RANDOM
⭐   NUMBER GENERATOR WITH GOOD PROSE.***

⭐ THE PROCEDURE — ⭐ one afternoon, once, then quarterly:
⭐ ① ⭐ Take ~100 outputs spanning good, bad and borderline.
⭐ ② ⭐⭐ TWO HUMANS LABEL THEM ***INDEPENDENTLY***.
⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ MEASURE ***HUMAN–HUMAN*** AGREEMENT FIRST. ⭐ This step is
⭐    skipped by almost everyone and it is the most informative:
⭐    ⭐⭐ IF YOUR TWO HUMANS AGREE ONLY 70% OF THE TIME, YOUR TASK
⭐    DEFINITION IS AMBIGUOUS AND ***NO JUDGE CAN DO BETTER THAN
⭐    70%***. ⭐ Fix the rubric before you touch the model.
⭐ ④ ⭐ Run the judge on the same 100.
⭐ ⑤ ⭐⭐ MEASURE JUDGE–HUMAN AGREEMENT. ⭐ Use Cohen's kappa, not
⭐    raw agreement — ⭐⭐ BECAUSE IF 90% OF CASES ARE "GOOD", A
⭐    JUDGE THAT ALWAYS SAYS "GOOD" SCORES 90% AND IS WORTHLESS.
⭐    ⭐ Kappa corrects for chance agreement and exposes exactly
⭐    that.
⭐ ⑥ ⭐ Target: ⭐⭐ JUDGE–HUMAN AGREEMENT CLOSE TO HUMAN–HUMAN.
⭐    ⭐ That is the ceiling, ⭐⭐ AND EXCEEDING IT IS NOT A GOAL,
⭐    IT IS A SIGN YOU MEASURED SOMETHING WRONG.
⭐ ⑦ ⭐⭐ READ THE DISAGREEMENTS. ⭐ They are either a bad rubric
⭐    (fixable) or a genuinely hard case (label it and move on).

⭐⭐ ⇒ AND THE OPERATIONAL VERSION: ***KEEP A SMALL HUMAN-LABELLED
⭐   HOLDOUT AND RE-CHECK THE JUDGE QUARTERLY***, ⭐ because the
⭐   judge model gets silently updated (Day 420) ⭐⭐ AND YOUR
⭐   MEASURING INSTRUMENT DRIFTS WITHOUT TELLING YOU. ⭐ A quality
⭐   "improvement" that is really a judge change is a genuinely
⭐   nasty way to be wrong.
```

# Part 5 — Pairwise beats absolute

```
⭐ ⭐⭐ MODELS ARE MUCH BETTER AT "IS A BETTER THAN B?" THAN AT
⭐   "SCORE A OUT OF 10." ⭐ So for comparing two prompts or two
⭐   models:
⭐   ⭐ ① Generate both outputs for each eval case.
⭐   ⭐ ② ⭐⭐ JUDGE PAIRWISE, ***BOTH ORDERS*** (PART 2 ①).
⭐   ⭐ ③ Report the win rate with a ***tie*** option — ⭐⭐ FORCING
⭐      A CHOICE ON EQUIVALENT OUTPUTS MANUFACTURES NOISE.
⭐   ⭐ ④ ⭐⭐ COMPUTE A CONFIDENCE INTERVAL. ⭐ 52% wins on 40 cases
⭐      is not a result — ⭐⭐ IT IS A COIN FLIP WITH A DECIMAL
⭐      POINT, ⭐ and teams ship on exactly that number every day.

⭐ ⭐ Absolute scores are still useful for one thing:
⭐   ⭐⭐ TRACKING A SINGLE SYSTEM OVER TIME, ⭐ where the bias is
⭐   at least ***consistent***. ⭐ Just never compare an absolute
⭐   score across different rubrics, judges or judge versions.

⭐ ⭐⭐ AND THE CHEAPER OPTION PEOPLE FORGET: ***A SMALL
⭐   FINE-TUNED CLASSIFIER OR A CROSS-ENCODER CAN OUTPERFORM A
⭐   LARGE JUDGE ON A NARROW DIMENSION*** — ⭐ trained on your
⭐   human labels, ⭐⭐ 100× CHEAPER, DETERMINISTIC, AND IT DOES
⭐   NOT DRIFT WHEN A VENDOR SHIPS. ⭐ Once you have 500 labels
⭐   from Part 4, this is often the right answer.
```

# Part 6 — The human loop that makes all of it work

```
⭐ ⭐⭐ EVERY AI SYSTEM THAT STAYS GOOD HAS HUMANS LOOKING AT
⭐   OUTPUT ON A SCHEDULE. ⭐ There is no exception, and the
⭐   teams that skip it discover regressions from customers.

⭐ ① ⭐⭐ ***SAMPLE PRODUCTION OUTPUT WEEKLY.*** ⭐ 20 random plus
⭐    20 low-confidence (Day 424) plus 20 that users
⭐    thumbs-downed. ⭐⭐ ONE PERSON, ONE HOUR.
⭐ ② ⭐ ***MAKE FEEDBACK CHEAP TO GIVE AND CHEAP TO USE.***
⭐    ⭐ Thumbs up/down with an optional reason. ⭐⭐ THE VALUE IS
⭐    NOT THE RATIO — IT IS THAT A THUMBS-DOWN GIVES YOU AN INPUT
⭐    THAT ***BELONGS IN THE EVAL SET*** (DAY 425).
⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ ***REVIEW QUEUES FOR HIGH-STAKES OUTPUT.*** ⭐ If a wrong
⭐    answer costs real money, a human approves before it lands
⭐    (Day 414's placement rule). ⭐⭐ AND THE REVIEW TOOL MUST
⭐    SHOW THE SOURCE ALONGSIDE, ⭐ or reviewing is guessing.
⭐ ④ ⭐ ***LABEL AS YOU GO.*** ⭐ Every review is a labelled
⭐    example. ⭐⭐ AFTER SIX MONTHS THAT IS A FINE-TUNING DATASET
⭐    AND A JUDGE CALIBRATION SET YOU DID NOT PAY EXTRA FOR.

⭐⭐ ⇒ AND THE HONEST ECONOMICS: ***ONE PERSON-HOUR A WEEK IS
⭐   CHEAPER THAN ONE UNDETECTED QUALITY REGRESSION***, ⭐ and it
⭐   is the only mechanism that catches the failure mode nobody
⭐   thought to put in the eval set (Day 425 Part 6).
```

# 💬 Interview

**1. ⭐⭐ "When do you use an LLM as a judge?"**
> ⭐ Only when deterministic graders can't do it — faithfulness, helpfulness, tone, instruction
> compliance. ⭐⭐ **And most judge usage in practice isn't that: it's checking JSON validity, whether a
> category is in a set, whether output is under 100 words, whether a quote is in the source. All of
> those are `parse`, `in`, `len` and a substring check.** ⭐ So the first move is to shrink the judge's
> job — ⭐⭐ **decompose the quality question into the parts code can check and the residue that
> genuinely needs judgement, because a judge assessing one narrow dimension is far more reliable and
> much cheaper than one assessing "quality".**

**2. ⭐⭐ "What are the failure modes?"**
> ⭐ Four. ⭐⭐ **Position bias — models systematically prefer whichever response is shown first, enough
> to reverse a verdict, so you run every comparison both ways and only count a win that survives the
> swap; disagreement between the two orders is itself the signal that it's too close to call.**
> ⭐⭐ **Verbosity bias — longer answers score higher even when they add nothing, which means optimising
> a prompt against a judge slowly makes your outputs longer and more expensive while the score climbs.
> You've optimised the metric, not the product.** ⭐⭐ **Self-preference — a model rates its own family
> higher, so never let a candidate provider judge a provider comparison.** ⭐⭐ **And sycophancy: ask "is
> there a problem with this?" and it finds one, because finding one is the plausible continuation of
> that question.** ⭐ Plus score compression, where everything lands 6 to 8 on a ten-point scale.

**3. "How do you design a judge prompt?"**
> ⭐⭐ **Binary or three-point, never one-to-ten — a model can't reliably distinguish a six from a
> seven, and neither can your human labellers, so the extra resolution is noise that looks like
> signal.** ⭐ One dimension per call, ⭐⭐ **because a single "quality" score blends dimensions that move
> independently and hides which one regressed.** ⭐ An explicit rubric with criteria and examples, not
> "rate the quality". ⭐ Reasoning before the verdict, which is the same field-order point as
> chain-of-thought and also gives me something to read when I disagree. ⭐ The reference where one
> exists. ⭐ And temperature zero.

**4. ⭐⭐ "How do you know the judge is any good?"**
> ⭐ You calibrate it against humans, ⭐⭐ **because a judge you haven't measured is a random number
> generator with good prose.** ⭐ A hundred outputs, two humans labelling independently — ⭐⭐ **and then
> the step almost everyone skips, which is the most informative: measure human–human agreement first.
> If your two humans agree only 70% of the time, your task definition is ambiguous and no judge can
> beat 70%, so fix the rubric before touching the model.** ⭐ Then judge–human agreement, ⭐⭐ **measured
> with Cohen's kappa rather than raw agreement — because if 90% of cases are "good", a judge that
> always says "good" scores 90% and is worthless, and kappa exposes exactly that.** ⭐ Target is
> judge–human close to human–human; that's the ceiling. ⭐ And re-check quarterly against a holdout,
> ⭐⭐ **because the judge model gets silently updated and your measuring instrument drifts — a quality
> "improvement" that's really a judge change is a nasty way to be wrong.**

**5. ⭐⭐ "Pairwise or absolute scoring?"**
> ⭐ Pairwise for comparisons — ⭐⭐ **models are much better at "is A better than B" than at "score A out
> of ten".** ⭐ Both orders, with a tie option, ⭐⭐ **because forcing a choice on equivalent outputs
> manufactures noise — and with a confidence interval, since 52% wins on 40 cases is a coin flip with
> a decimal point, and teams ship on exactly that number every day.** ⭐ Absolute scores are fine for
> tracking one system over time where the bias is at least consistent, ⭐ never for comparing across
> rubrics or judge versions. ⭐ And the cheaper option people forget: ⭐⭐ **once you have 500 human
> labels, a small fine-tuned classifier or cross-encoder often beats a large judge on a narrow
> dimension — 100× cheaper, deterministic, and it doesn't drift when a vendor ships.**

**6. "How much human review is needed?"**
> ⭐⭐ **Every AI system that stays good has humans looking at output on a schedule — there's no
> exception, and teams that skip it learn about regressions from customers.** ⭐ One person, one hour a
> week: twenty random outputs, twenty low-confidence ones, twenty that users thumbs-downed. ⭐⭐ **And
> the value of a thumbs-down isn't the ratio — it's that it hands you an input that belongs in the
> eval set.** ⭐ Every review is also a labelled example, ⭐⭐ **so after six months you have a
> fine-tuning dataset and a judge calibration set you didn't pay extra for.** ⭐ The economics are
> straightforward: ⭐⭐ **one person-hour a week is cheaper than one undetected quality regression, and
> it's the only mechanism that catches the failure nobody thought to put in the eval set.**

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes.** Frame it as instrumentation: **a judge model is a measuring instrument, and an
