# Day 344 ⏱️

**P-344** · ⏱️ **The system design interview framework** — ⭐⭐ forty-five minutes, seven moves

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** BUILD

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: a system design round is not a test of whether you know what a CDN is — it
> is a test of whether you can take an under-specified problem, narrow it to something buildable,
> justify each decision with a number, and go deep on the one part that is actually hard, all while
> saying what you are doing out loud.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ What is actually being scored

```
⭐⭐ THE FIVE THINGS AN INTERVIEWER WRITES DOWN — ⭐ and only
⭐   one of them is knowledge:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ SCOPING — did you narrow a vague prompt into
⭐        something a team could build this quarter?
⭐   ② ⭐⭐ JUSTIFICATION — is every box attached to a number or
⭐        a requirement? ⭐ "We'd add a cache" vs "reads are
⭐        100:1 and the hot set is 10 GB, so one Redis node
⭐        absorbs 300k reads/s".
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ DEPTH — can you go three levels down on ***one***
⭐        thing? ⭐ Breadth without depth reads as having watched
⭐        videos.
⭐   ④ ⭐⭐ TRADE-OFFS — did you say what each choice ***costs***,
⭐        unprompted?
⭐   ⑤ ⭐ COMMUNICATION — could a colleague follow you? Did you
⭐        take a hint?

⭐⭐ AND THE THREE THINGS THAT ARE ***NOT*** SCORED, WHICH
⭐   PEOPLE SPEND TIME ON ANYWAY:
⭐   ⭐ ✘ Drawing a complete architecture. ⭐⭐ NOBODY FINISHES.
⭐     ⭐ An unfinished design with two deep sections beats a
⭐     complete shallow one, every time.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ Naming specific AWS products.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ Getting the estimate arithmetically exact.

⭐⭐ THE MOST COMMON FAILURE, AND IT IS NOT TECHNICAL:
⭐   ***DRAWING BOXES FOR THIRTY MINUTES WITHOUT SAYING WHY ANY
⭐      OF THEM ARE THERE.***
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ The clock: seven moves, forty-five minutes

```
⭐⭐ WRITE THIS ON THE BOARD AT MINUTE ZERO. ⭐ It signals
⭐   structure before you have said anything technical, and it
⭐   keeps you honest at minute 30.

⭐⭐ ① 0–5   ***CLARIFY & SCOPE***     ⇒ functional + non-functional
⭐⭐ ② 5–10  ***ESTIMATE***            ⇒ QPS, storage, the ratio
⭐⭐ ③ 10–13 ***API***                 ⇒ 3–5 endpoints, signatures
⭐⭐ ④ 13–18 ***DATA MODEL***          ⇒ tables/keys, the access patterns
⭐⭐ ⑤ 18–28 ***HIGH-LEVEL DESIGN***   ⇒ the boxes, and the data flow
⭐⭐ ⑥ 28–40 ***DEEP DIVE***           ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE ROUND IS DECIDED HERE
⭐⭐ ⑦ 40–45 ***BOTTLENECKS & WRAP***  ⇒ what breaks at 10×

⭐⭐ THE RULES THAT MAKE THE CLOCK WORK:
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AT MINUTE 18 YOU MUST HAVE A DATA MODEL. ⭐ If you
⭐     do not, you are over-scoping — cut a feature out loud.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AT MINUTE 28 YOU MUST STOP DRAWING. ⭐ This is Day
⭐     308's minute-30 rule, moved earlier because the deep
⭐     dive is longer here.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ NEVER LET THE INTERVIEWER CHOOSE THE DEEP DIVE
⭐     BY DEFAULT. ⭐ Offer two: *"the interesting parts are the
⭐     fan-out and the hot-key problem — which would you
⭐     rather?"* ⭐⭐ THIS IS THE SINGLE HIGHEST-LEVERAGE
⭐     SENTENCE IN THE WHOLE ROUND, because it puts you in a
⭐     part you have prepared while still letting them steer.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Move ①: scoping, which decides your grade

```
⭐⭐ FUNCTIONAL — ASK, THEN ***WRITE DOWN THREE***:
⭐   ⭐ "Who uses this, and what are the two or three things
⭐     they do?"
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ THEN NARROW ALOUD: *"I'll design posting, reading a
⭐     feed and following. I'm leaving out DMs, ads, search and
⭐     moderation — tell me if you'd rather I include one."*
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE OUT-OF-SCOPE SENTENCE IS WORTH MORE THAN THE
⭐     IN-SCOPE ONE. It proves you know the full problem and
⭐     chose.

⭐⭐ NON-FUNCTIONAL — FIVE QUESTIONS, AND EACH CHANGES A BOX:
⭐   ① ⭐ SCALE: "How many users? DAU? What's the read:write
⭐        ratio?" ⭐⭐ THE RATIO IS THE ONE THAT MOVES THE
⭐        ARCHITECTURE.
⭐   ② ⭐⭐ CONSISTENCY: "If someone posts, must ***they***
⭐        see it immediately? Must ***others***?" ⭐ Day 331's
⭐        asymmetry, asked as a question.
⭐   ③ ⭐ LATENCY: "What's acceptable at p99, and for which
⭐        operation?"
⭐   ④ ⭐ AVAILABILITY: "What happens if this is down for five
⭐        minutes — annoying, or money?"
⭐   ⑤ ⭐ DURABILITY: "Can we ever lose one of these?"

⭐⭐ AND THE MOVE THAT SEPARATES SENIOR CANDIDATES:
⭐   ***STATE THE ASSUMPTION WHEN YOU DON'T GET AN ANSWER.***
⭐   ⭐ "You've said it's up to me — I'll assume 10M DAU and a
⭐     100:1 read ratio, ⭐⭐ AND I'LL FLAG WHERE A 10:1 RATIO
⭐     WOULD CHANGE THE DESIGN."
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Never stall waiting for a number. ⭐⭐ AN ASSUMPTION
⭐     SAID ALOUD IS A DECISION; AN ASSUMPTION IN YOUR HEAD IS
⭐     A TRAP.
```

---

# Part 4 — ⭐ Moves ②–④: numbers, API, data

```
⭐⭐ ② ESTIMATE (Day 323) — ⭐⭐ FIVE LINES, AND EVERY LINE
⭐   ENDS IN "SO…":
⭐   ⭐ Writes/s · reads/s · peak · storage at 3–5 years · hot
⭐     set in RAM.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ STOP AT THE FIRST NUMBER THAT DECIDES SOMETHING AND
⭐     SAY WHAT IT DECIDED.

⭐⭐ ③ API — ⭐ three to five endpoints, with real signatures.
⭐   ⭐ `POST /v1/urls {long_url, custom_alias?} -> {short_url}`
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Show pagination as a ***cursor*** (Day 194) — ⭐⭐ IT
⭐     TAKES FIVE SECONDS AND SIGNALS YOU HAVE PAGINATED
⭐     SOMETHING REAL.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Mention idempotency on writes (Day 335) in one clause.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE API IS ALSO A SCOPING DEVICE: if you cannot list
⭐     five endpoints, the scope is still too vague.

⭐⭐ ④ DATA MODEL — ⭐⭐ THE MOST UNDER-USED MOVE IN THE WHOLE
⭐   INTERVIEW.
⭐   ⭐ Tables/entities, the ***primary key***, the ***access
⭐     patterns***, and ⭐⭐ THE ONE INDEX THAT MATTERS.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ SAY THE SHARD KEY HERE (Day 329), NOT LATER — it
⭐     constrains every later decision, so deciding it late
⭐     means redoing the design.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ And say which store: *"Postgres for the record,
⭐     because there are real invariants; Redis for the hot
⭐     path; S3 for blobs."* ⭐ Day 332's framework in one
⭐     sentence.
```

# Part 5 — ⭐⭐ Moves ⑤–⑥: the design, and the dive that decides it

```
⭐⭐ ⑤ HIGH-LEVEL — ⭐ six to eight boxes, no more.
⭐   ⭐ client → CDN/gateway → service → cache → database
⭐     ⭐ + queue + workers + object storage.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THEN ***WALK ONE WRITE AND ONE READ THROUGH IT OUT
⭐     LOUD***, END TO END. ⭐ This is what makes it a design
⭐     rather than a drawing, and it is where you catch your own
⭐     missing pieces.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Do not draw a box you cannot justify in one sentence.
⭐     ⭐⭐ AN UNJUSTIFIED BOX IS AN INVITATION TO A QUESTION YOU
⭐     CANNOT ANSWER.

⭐⭐ ⑥ DEEP DIVE — ⭐⭐ TWELVE MINUTES, ONE OR TWO TOPICS, THREE
⭐   LEVELS DOWN. ⭐ This is the round.
⭐   ⭐ LEVEL 1: "We shard by user_id."
⭐   ⭐ LEVEL 2: "Consistent hashing with 150 vnodes, so adding
⭐     a node moves 1/N of keys rather than 80%."
⭐   ⭐ LEVEL 3: ⭐⭐ "AND THE WHALE TENANT BREAKS THAT, BECAUSE
⭐     ONE KEY IS ONE SHARD — SO A DIRECTORY LAYER LETS ME PIN
⭐     THEM, AT THE COST OF A LOOKUP I THEN HAVE TO CACHE."
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ LEVEL 3 IS THE WHOLE SIGNAL. ⭐ Level 1 is a
⭐     vocabulary check.

⭐⭐ THE SIX DEEP DIVES THAT COVER MOST PROMPTS — ⭐ prepare
⭐   these and you are prepared for almost anything:
⭐   ① ⭐ The hot key / celebrity problem (326, 329).
⭐   ② ⭐ Fan-out on write vs read (351).
⭐   ③ ⭐ Exactly-once and idempotency (335).
⭐   ④ ⭐ The consistency choice for one specific operation
⭐        (330, 331).
⭐   ⑤ ⭐ Cascading failure and the defences (339).
⭐   ⑥ ⭐ Storage engine choice and the access pattern (332).
```

# Part 6 — ⭐⭐ Move ⑦, and the five sentences that end a round well

```
⭐⭐ ⑦ BOTTLENECKS — ⭐ five minutes, and it is not a summary.
⭐   ⭐ "At 10× the bottleneck moves from the cache to the
⭐     write path, because…"
⭐   ⭐ "The single point of failure is X; here's how I'd
⭐     remove it, and what that costs."
⭐   ⭐ "Here's what I'd monitor, and the one alert I'd page on."
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ "HERE'S WHAT I DELIBERATELY DID NOT BUILD, AND WHEN
⭐     I WOULD."

⭐⭐ THE FIVE SENTENCES WORTH REHEARSING VERBATIM:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ "THE COST OF THAT IS ___, AND I'D ACCEPT IT BECAUSE
⭐      ___."
⭐   ② ⭐⭐ "I DON'T KNOW — HERE'S HOW I'D FIND OUT."
⭐      ⇒ ⭐ Far stronger than a confident wrong answer, and
⭐        interviewers deliberately probe past your edge to see
⭐        which one you do.
⭐   ③ ⭐ "That's a good point — let me change the design."
⭐      ⇒ ⭐⭐ TAKING A HINT IS SCORED. DEFENDING A BROKEN
⭐        DESIGN IS SCORED TOO, NEGATIVELY.
⭐   ④ ⭐ "This is over-engineered for the numbers we said —
⭐      one Postgres would do it."
⭐   ⑤ ⭐ "The interesting parts are A and B — which would you
⭐      like me to go deep on?"
```

# Part 7 — ⭐ The failure modes, named so you can catch yourself

```
⭐⭐ ① ***THE BUZZWORD CASCADE*** — Kafka, Redis, Cassandra,
⭐      Kubernetes, all in ninety seconds, none justified.
⭐      ⇒ ⭐ FIX: one box per sentence of justification.
⭐⭐ ② ***THE SILENT DRAWER*** — five minutes of diagramming
⭐      with no narration.
⭐      ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE INTERVIEWER CANNOT SCORE WHAT THEY CANNOT
⭐        HEAR. ⭐ Narrate continuously, even while drawing.
⭐⭐ ③ ***THE INFINITE SCOPER*** — twelve minutes of questions.
⭐      ⇒ ⭐ Five minutes, then assume and move.
⭐⭐ ④ ***THE BREADTH-ONLY TOUR*** — everything mentioned,
⭐      nothing examined.
⭐      ⇒ ⭐⭐ ONE DEEP DIVE BEATS FIVE MENTIONS.
⭐⭐ ⑤ ***THE DEFENDER*** — argues with a hint.
⭐      ⇒ ⭐ Hints are gifts. Take them visibly.
⭐⭐ ⑥ ***THE OVER-ENGINEER*** — shards a system with 40 rps.
⭐      ⇒ ⭐⭐ SAY "THIS FITS ON ONE BOX" WHEN IT DOES. It is a
⭐        judgement test as often as a knowledge test.
⭐⭐ ⑦ ***THE NUMBERLESS DESIGNER*** — no estimate, so nothing
⭐      is justified and every follow-up is unanswerable.

⭐⭐ AND THE HONEST NOTE ON REMOTE ROUNDS: ⭐ practise on the
⭐   actual tool (Excalidraw, a shared doc). ⭐⭐ FUMBLING THE
⭐   WHITEBOARD COSTS REAL MINUTES, AND MINUTES ARE THE SCARCE
⭐   RESOURCE.
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ Drawing boxes without justification | Every unjustified box invites a question you can't answer |
| ⭐⭐ No estimate | Nothing is justified, and every follow-up becomes an opinion |
| ⭐⭐ Breadth with no depth | Reads as having watched videos. One level-3 dive beats five mentions |
| ⭐ Twelve minutes of clarifying questions | Five, then assume aloud and move |
| ⭐⭐ Letting the interviewer pick the deep dive | Offer two you've prepared. Highest-leverage sentence in the round |
| ⭐ Silent drawing | They can't score what they can't hear |
| ⭐⭐ Defending against a hint | Hints are gifts and taking them is explicitly scored |
| ⭐ Sharding a 40-rps system | It's a judgement test. Say "this fits on one box" when it does |
| ⭐⭐ Deciding the shard key late | It constrains everything. Say it at the data model, not in the deep dive |
| ⭐ Trying to finish the whole architecture | Nobody finishes. Unfinished with depth beats complete and shallow |
| ⭐ Naming products instead of properties | "A durable log with replay" travels; "MSK" doesn't |

## Interview questions

**1. "How do you approach a system design question?"**
> ⭐ Seven moves on a forty-five-minute clock, written on the board at minute zero: scope, estimate,
> API, data model, high-level, deep dive, bottlenecks. ⭐⭐ Two hard rules — **a data model by minute
> eighteen, and stop drawing at minute twenty-eight** — because the deep dive is where the round is
> decided and it needs twelve minutes. ⭐ And I'd offer two candidate deep dives rather than let one be
> chosen for me.

**2. "What do you ask in the first five minutes?"**
> ⭐ Two or three functional flows, then five non-functional questions: scale and **the read:write
> ratio**, which operation needs to be consistent and for whom, p99 targets, what five minutes of
> downtime costs, and whether data can be lost. ⭐⭐ Then I narrow out loud and name what I'm excluding
> — **the out-of-scope sentence is worth more than the in-scope one**, because it proves I know the
> full problem and chose. ⭐ And if they won't give a number I state my assumption and flag where a
> different number changes the design.

**3. "What does a good deep dive look like?"**
> ⭐ Three levels. "We shard by user id" is level one and just vocabulary. ⭐ Level two is consistent
> hashing with virtual nodes so adding a node moves 1/N of keys. ⭐⭐ Level three is **naming what
> breaks it — the whale tenant, because one key is one shard — and the directory layer that fixes it,
> plus the lookup cost that fix introduces.** ⭐ Level three is the entire signal.

**4. "What if you don't know something?"**
> ⭐⭐ Say so and say how I'd find out: *"I don't know Cassandra's compaction strategies well enough to
> pick one here — I'd benchmark with our write pattern, and the property I need is X."* ⭐ Interviewers
> deliberately probe past the edge of what you know to see which you do — a confident wrong answer
> costs far more than an honest boundary, because it makes everything else you said unverifiable.

**5. "How do you avoid over-engineering?"**
> ⭐ Estimate first, and let the number decide. ⭐⭐ If it's forty requests a second and two hundred and
> fifty megabytes, **I say "one Postgres and one app server, and here's why I'm not sharding"** — that's
> a judgement test as often as a knowledge test, and interviewers plant it. ⭐ Then I name the trigger:
> "I'd revisit at roughly ten thousand writes a second, which is where one primary stops."

**6. "How do you handle a hint you disagree with?"**
> ⭐ Take it visibly and reason about it out loud: *"That's a good point — if writes are that heavy,
> fan-out on write stops working; let me switch to a hybrid."* ⭐⭐ If I genuinely think it's wrong I say
> the trade-off rather than the verdict — **"that's cheaper on reads but it costs me ordering; do you
> want me to take that trade?"** ⭐ Defending a broken design is scored, negatively.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes: "How do you approach a system design interview?"**

Lead with what's being scored, because it reframes everything: **scoping, justification, depth,
trade-offs and communication — only one of which is knowledge.** Then the clock, written on the board
at minute zero so structure is visible before you've said anything technical: five minutes scoping, five
estimating, three on the API, five on the data model, ten high-level, **twelve on the deep dive, which is
where the round is actually decided**, and five on bottlenecks. Give the two hard rules — **a data model
by minute eighteen, or you're over-scoped and should cut a feature out loud, and stop drawing at minute
twenty-eight.** Then the single highest-leverage sentence: **offer two deep dives and let them choose**,
so you land somewhere you've prepared while they still steer. Say what depth means concretely: **level
one is "we shard by user id", level three is "and the whale tenant breaks that, because one key is one
shard, so a directory pins them at the cost of a lookup I then have to cache."** Close on the two
things that lose rounds that aren't technical: **drawing boxes nobody asked you to justify, and
defending a design against a hint** — and on the sentence that costs nothing and buys credibility:
**"this fits on one box, and here's the number where I'd revisit that."**
