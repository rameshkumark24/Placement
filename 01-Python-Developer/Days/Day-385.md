# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ The burst-credit trap

> The single most confusing performance failure in AWS, because the graph looks healthy while the
> service is dying.

```
⭐⭐ HOW t-FAMILY ACTUALLY WORKS:
⭐   ⭐ A t3.medium has a ***baseline*** of 20% of 2 vCPUs.
⭐   ⭐ Below baseline, it ***accrues*** CPU credits (24/hour).
⭐   ⭐ Above baseline, it ***spends*** them to run at full speed.
⭐   ⭐ The bucket is capped (576 credits ≈ 24 hours' worth).

⭐⭐ NOW THE FAILURE, STEP BY STEP:
⭐ ① ⭐ You deploy a change that raises steady CPU from 15% to
⭐    35%. ⭐ Everything is fast. ⭐⭐ THE DEPLOY LOOKS SUCCESSFUL.
⭐ ② ⭐ You are now spending credits faster than you earn them.
⭐    ⭐ The full bucket lasts ***hours***.
⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ SOMETIME THAT NIGHT — OR THREE DAYS LATER, IF THE
⭐    BUCKET WAS FULL AND TRAFFIC IS DIURNAL — THE BUCKET HITS
⭐    ZERO.
⭐ ④ ⭐⭐ THE INSTANCE IS THROTTLED TO ***20%*** AND STAYS THERE.
⭐    ⭐ Latency triples. ⭐ Queues build. ⭐ Timeouts cascade
⭐    (Day 336).
⭐ ⑤ ⭐⭐ AND THE CPU GRAPH SHOWS A ***FLAT LINE AT 20%***, WHICH
⭐    LOOKS LIKE A HEALTHY, UNDER-UTILISED SERVER. ⭐ Every
⭐    instinct says "it is not CPU-bound".

⭐⭐ ⇒ THREE RULES, AND THEY ARE NOT NEGOTIABLE:
⭐   ⭐⭐ ① ***ALARM ON `CPUCreditBalance`, NOT ONLY ON
⭐      `CPUUtilization`.*** ⭐ A flat-at-baseline CPU line with a
⭐      zero credit balance is the signature. ⭐⭐ IF YOU TAKE ONE
⭐      THING FROM TODAY, TAKE THIS.
⭐   ⭐⭐ ② ***t-FAMILY FOR SPIKY OR IDLE. NEVER FOR STEADY
⭐      LOAD.*** ⭐ Dev boxes, bastions, cron runners: perfect.
⭐      ⭐ A production API at 40% average: wrong instance.
⭐   ⭐⭐ ③ ***`unlimited` MODE DOES NOT FIX IT — IT CONVERTS A
⭐      PERFORMANCE SURPRISE INTO A BILLING SURPRISE*** ($0.05
⭐      per vCPU-hour of surplus). ⭐ Which is better! ⭐⭐ BUT ONLY
⭐      IF SOMEONE IS WATCHING THE BILL (DAY 396).

⭐ ⭐⭐ AND THE PYTHON-FLAVOURED VERSION: THIS IS ALSO WHY A
⭐   t3.micro "WORKS FINE" FOR YOUR FLASK DEMO AND FALLS OVER
⭐   THE FIRST TIME SOMEONE RUNS A REAL LOAD TEST — ⭐ the test
⭐   is long enough to drain the bucket, ⭐ and your p99 chart has
⭐   a cliff in it at exactly the drain point.
```

# Part 3 — EBS, and the third limit nobody checks

```
⭐ THE VOLUME TYPES, HONESTLY:
⭐   ⭐⭐ gp3 — ***THE DEFAULT. ALWAYS.*** ⭐ 3,000 IOPS and 125
⭐     MB/s baseline ***regardless of size***, ⭐ tunable up to
⭐     16,000 / 1,000 MB/s, ⭐⭐ AND ~20% CHEAPER PER GB THAN gp2.
⭐   ⭐⭐ gp2 — ⭐ IOPS scale with size (3 per GB), ⭐⭐ SO PEOPLE
⭐     OVER-PROVISIONED DISK TO BUY IOPS. ⭐⭐ THERE IS NO REASON
⭐     TO STILL BE ON gp2. ⭐ Migrating is an online modify, no
⭐     downtime. ⭐ Go and check your volumes today.
⭐   ⭐ io2 / io2 Block Express — real guaranteed IOPS, 99.999%
⭐     durability. ⭐ For a database that has earned it.
⭐   ⭐ st1 / sc1 — throughput HDD. ⭐⭐ TERRIBLE FOR RANDOM I/O,
⭐     EXCELLENT AND CHEAP FOR BIG SEQUENTIAL SCANS.

⭐⭐ THE THREE CEILINGS — AND THE THIRD IS THE ONE PEOPLE MISS:
⭐   ① ⭐ the volume's IOPS
⭐   ② ⭐ the volume's throughput (MB/s)
⭐   ③ ⭐⭐ ***THE INSTANCE'S EBS BANDWIDTH.***
⭐ ⭐⭐ ⇒ A 16,000-IOPS gp3 ON A SMALL INSTANCE DELIVERS NOWHERE
⭐   NEAR 16,000 IOPS, ⭐ because the instance itself is capped —
⭐   ⭐ an m6g.large gets ~4,750 Mbps to EBS. ⭐⭐ THE SYMPTOM IS
⭐   "WE PAID FOR IOPS AND NOTHING GOT FASTER", ⭐ and the metric
⭐   that proves it is `EBSIOBalance%` / `EBSByteBalance%`
⭐   dropping — ⭐⭐ WHICH IS BURST CREDITS AGAIN, ON A DIFFERENT
⭐   RESOURCE. ⭐ (Smaller instances burst their EBS bandwidth
⭐   too. ⭐⭐ THE PATTERN REPEATS EVERYWHERE IN AWS: SMALL THINGS
⭐   BURST, AND BURSTING ENDS.)

⭐ INSTANCE STORE (the `d` suffix): ⭐ physically attached NVMe.
⭐   ⭐ Survives reboot. ⭐⭐ GONE ON STOP, TERMINATE, OR HOST
⭐     FAILURE — ⭐ and "gone" means gone, not degraded.
⭐   ⭐ Legitimate uses: ⭐ scratch space, ⭐ a local cache you can
⭐     rebuild, ⭐⭐ AND — THE PYTHON ONE — ***SORT SPILL AND
⭐     TEMPORARY FILES***: point `TMPDIR` and pandas/polars spill
⭐     at it and a shuffle-heavy job gets dramatically faster for
⭐     free.
⭐   ⭐⭐ NEVER YOUR DATABASE'S DATA DIRECTORY. ⭐ People do.
```

```python
# ⭐⭐ SPOT DONE PROPERLY: poll IMDS for the interruption notice
# ⭐ and drain. ⭐ This is Day 378's graceful shutdown, sourced
# ⭐ from the cloud instead of from the orchestrator.
import threading, requests, time

_URL = "http://169.254.169.254/latest/meta-data/spot/instance-action"
draining = threading.Event()

def _watch(token_hdr):                       # ⭐ IMDSv2 headers
    while not draining.is_set():
        r = requests.get(_URL, headers=token_hdr, timeout=1)
        if r.status_code == 200:             # ⭐⭐ ~120 s LEFT
            draining.set()                   # ⭐ stop taking work
        time.sleep(5)

# ⭐ In the worker loop:
#   if draining.is_set(): break              # ⭐ no new tasks
#   finish in-flight, ack, exit cleanly
# ⭐⭐ AND THE RULE THAT MAKES SPOT SAFE AT ALL: ***ONLY FOR WORK
# ⭐   THAT IS IDEMPOTENT AND RE-RUNNABLE*** (DAY 335). ⭐ Stateless
# ⭐   web tier behind a load balancer, batch workers, CI runners:
# ⭐   yes. ⭐⭐ ANYTHING HOLDING STATE THAT ISN'T ELSEWHERE: NO.
```

# 💬 Interview

**1. ⭐⭐ "Why did our API get slow three days after a deploy that looked fine?"**
> ⭐ If it's on a t-family instance, that's the burst-credit trap and the timing is the tell.
> ⭐⭐ **The deploy raised steady CPU above the instance's baseline, so it started spending credits
> faster than it earned them; the bucket holds about a day's worth, so it drained silently and then
> the instance was throttled to baseline — 20% on a t3.medium — permanently.** ⭐ Latency triples,
> queues build. ⭐⭐ **And the reason nobody spots it is that the CPU graph is a flat line at 20%, which
> looks like a healthy under-utilised server**, so every instinct says it isn't CPU. ⭐ The fix is to
> alarm on `CPUCreditBalance`, and to move steady workloads off t-family entirely — ⭐⭐ **`unlimited`
> mode doesn't solve it, it converts a performance surprise into a billing surprise.**

**2. "Would you move Python workloads to Graviton?"**
> ⭐ Yes, and it's usually the easiest 20–40% available. ⭐ Pure Python doesn't care about the
> architecture, and the ecosystem caught up — ⭐⭐ **there are manylinux aarch64 wheels for numpy,
> pandas, pydantic-core, cryptography, psycopg, pillow, the whole normal stack.** ⭐ The one real risk
> is a dependency with no arm64 wheel silently falling back to building from source, ⭐⭐ **which turns
> a 90-second image build into eleven minutes or a missing-header failure** — so I'd add
> `--only-binary :all:` in CI to turn a slow surprise into a loud one, and test the build on arm64
> before migrating.

**3. "gp2 or gp3?"**
> ⭐⭐ **gp3, always** — it decouples IOPS from size, gives 3,000 IOPS and 125 MB/s at any size, and is
> about 20% cheaper per gigabyte. ⭐ On gp2 you got 3 IOPS per gigabyte, so people over-provisioned
> disk purely to buy IOPS and paid for both. ⭐ Migration is an online modify with no downtime,
> ⭐⭐ **so a gp2 volume in production today is just money being left on the table.**

**4. ⭐⭐ "We paid for 16,000 IOPS and nothing got faster. Why?"**
> ⭐ Because there are three ceilings and you raised the wrong one. ⭐ The volume's IOPS, the volume's
> throughput, ⭐⭐ **and the instance's EBS bandwidth — and the third is what people forget.** ⭐ A small
> instance is capped at a few thousand Mbps to EBS regardless of what the volume can do. ⭐ The metric
> that proves it is `EBSIOBalance%` or `EBSByteBalance%` falling, ⭐⭐ **which is burst credits again on
> a different resource — that's the general pattern in AWS: small things burst, and bursting ends.**

**5. "How would you cut the compute bill this quarter?"**
> ⭐⭐ **Buy a Compute Savings Plan covering 60–70% of the steady baseline.** ⭐ It's an afternoon, no
> code change, and typically 30%+ off compute. ⭐ Compute rather than EC2-specific ⭐⭐ **because it
> applies to Fargate and Lambda too and survives an architecture change, which a reserved instance
> doesn't.** ⭐ Not 100% coverage — you want headroom to shrink. ⭐ Then Graviton, then right-sizing,
> then spot for anything stateless and re-runnable. ⭐⭐ **Nothing I write in code this quarter will
> beat the savings plan.**

**6. "When is spot safe?"**
> ⭐ When the work is idempotent and re-runnable, and something else holds the state: ⭐ a stateless
> web tier behind a load balancer, batch workers pulling from a queue, CI runners. ⭐ You poll IMDS for
> the interruption notice, ⭐⭐ **and on the two-minute warning you stop accepting new work and finish
> what's in flight — which is exactly the graceful shutdown from Kubernetes, sourced from the cloud
> instead of the orchestrator.** ⭐ Anything holding state that isn't durably elsewhere: no.
