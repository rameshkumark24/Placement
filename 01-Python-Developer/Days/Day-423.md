# Part 1 — Few-shot: what it actually teaches

```
⭐ ZERO-SHOT → ONE-SHOT → FEW-SHOT. ⭐ The examples go in the
⭐   prompt; ⭐⭐ NOTHING IS TRAINED. ⭐ It is called "in-context
⭐   learning" and no weights change.

⭐⭐ AND THE FINDING THAT REORGANISES HOW YOU USE IT: ***EXAMPLES
⭐   TEACH ***FORMAT AND DECISION BOUNDARY*** FAR MORE THAN THEY
⭐   TEACH ***THE ANSWER***.***
⭐   ⭐ Research repeatedly finds that even examples with
⭐     ***scrambled labels*** retain much of the benefit — ⭐⭐ THE
⭐     MODEL IS LEARNING "WHAT SHAPE OF THING GOES HERE" AND
⭐     "WHAT KIND OF INPUT MAPS TO WHAT KIND OF OUTPUT".
⭐   ⭐⭐ ⇒ SO THE HIGHEST-VALUE EXAMPLES ARE NOT THE TYPICAL
⭐     ONES — ⭐ the model already handles typical.
⭐     ⭐⭐ THEY ARE ***EDGE CASES AND BOUNDARIES***: ⭐ the empty
⭐     input, the ambiguous one, the one that should be refused,
⭐     the one that looks like category A and is category B.

⭐ ⭐⭐ THE PLATEAU: ⭐ 1 example is a large jump. ⭐ 3 is better.
⭐   ⭐ 5 is usually the knee. ⭐⭐ 20 IS RARELY BETTER THAN 5 AND
⭐   COSTS FOUR TIMES THE INPUT TOKENS ***ON EVERY REQUEST
⭐   FOREVER*** (DAY 421). ⭐ If you genuinely need 50 examples,
⭐   ⭐⭐ YOU HAVE FOUND THE FINE-TUNING CASE (DAY 451) — ⭐ that is
⭐   what the signal means.

⭐ ⭐ Three practical rules:
⭐   ⭐ ① ⭐⭐ MAKE THE EXAMPLES ***EXACTLY*** THE OUTPUT FORMAT YOU
⭐      WANT. ⭐ The model copies the shape precisely, including
⭐      your typos and your inconsistent capitalisation.
⭐   ⭐ ② ⭐⭐ BALANCE THE LABELS. ⭐ Five examples where four are
⭐      "bug" biases the classifier toward "bug", ⭐ and this is a
⭐      real, measurable skew.
⭐   ⭐ ③ ⭐ Put them in the stable prefix so they cache
⭐      (Day 415).
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ Chain-of-thought: what it buys, and where it does not

```
⭐ ⭐⭐ "REASON BEFORE ANSWERING" WORKS FOR A MECHANICAL REASON,
⭐   NOT A PSYCHOLOGICAL ONE: ***EACH TOKEN IS ONE FORWARD PASS,
⭐   SO GENERATING REASONING TOKENS SPENDS MORE COMPUTE ON THE
⭐   PROBLEM BEFORE COMMITTING*** (DAY 414).
⭐   ⭐ A model that must answer immediately gets one pass' worth
⭐     of computation for the whole problem.
⭐   ⭐⭐ AND CRUCIALLY: THE ANSWER TOKEN IS NOW CONDITIONED ON THE
⭐     REASONING, ⭐ so the intermediate results are ***in the
⭐     context*** rather than having to be held implicitly.

⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ WHERE IT HELPS: ⭐ multi-step arithmetic, ⭐ logical
⭐   deduction, ⭐ planning, ⭐ anything where step 3 depends on
⭐   step 2.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ WHERE IT DOES NOT: ⭐ extraction, ⭐ classification into a
⭐   known set, ⭐ formatting, ⭐ translation, ⭐ summarisation.
⭐   ⭐⭐ THE ANSWER IS ALREADY IN THE INPUT; THERE IS NOTHING TO
⭐   REASON ***ABOUT***, AND YOU ARE PAYING OUTPUT-RATE TOKENS AND
⭐   LATENCY FOR NOTHING. ⭐ This is the commonest waste: ⭐⭐ CoT
⭐   BOLTED ONTO A CLASSIFIER OUT OF HABIT.

⭐⭐ ***AND THE THREE THINGS ABOUT CoT THAT MATTER MORE THAN
⭐   WHETHER TO USE IT:***
⭐ ① ⭐⭐ ***THE REASONING IS NOT AN EXPLANATION.*** ⭐ It is
⭐    generated text that ***precedes*** the answer, ⭐⭐ NOT A
⭐    TRACE OF WHY THE ANSWER WAS PRODUCED. ⭐ A model can reason
⭐    correctly and then answer wrongly, ⭐⭐ OR PRODUCE PLAUSIBLE
⭐    REASONING THAT RATIONALISES A WRONG ANSWER. ⭐ So ***never
⭐    show raw reasoning to a user as a justification*** — ⭐⭐ IT
⭐    LOOKS LIKE AN AUDIT TRAIL AND IS NOT ONE.
⭐ ② ⭐⭐ ***ORDER MATTERS ABSOLUTELY: REASONING ***BEFORE***
⭐    ANSWER.*** ⭐ A schema with `{"answer": ..., "reasoning":
⭐    ...}` produces the answer ***first*** and then rationalises
⭐    it — ⭐⭐ WHICH GIVES YOU THE COST OF CoT AND NONE OF THE
⭐    BENEFIT. ⭐ Field order in a JSON schema is a
⭐    ***functional*** decision here, not a stylistic one, ⭐⭐ AND
⭐    IT IS THE MOST COMMONLY-MISSED DETAIL IN THIS WHOLE DAY.
⭐ ③ ⭐ ***YOU CAN HIDE IT.*** ⭐ Ask for reasoning, then extract
⭐    only the answer field. ⭐⭐ YOU PAY THE TOKENS AND THE USER
⭐    SEES A CLEAN RESULT.

⭐ ⭐ Variants worth knowing by name: ⭐ ***self-consistency***
⭐   (sample N reasonings, take the majority answer — ⭐⭐ REAL
⭐   ACCURACY GAINS AT N× THE COST, ⭐ and `n>1` makes it cheap
⭐   (Day 418)); ⭐ ***least-to-most*** (solve simpler
⭐   sub-problems first).
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Decomposition: the technique that keeps paying

```
⭐⭐ ***ONE PROMPT DOING FIVE THINGS IS WORSE THAN FIVE PROMPTS
⭐   DOING ONE THING EACH — AND THE REASON IS NOT MODEL
⭐   CAPABILITY, IT IS ENGINEERING.***

⭐ ❌ THE MEGA-PROMPT: ⭐ "Read this contract, extract the
⭐   parties, identify unusual clauses, assess risk, and write a
⭐   summary email."
⭐   ⭐ Failure modes: ⭐⭐ IT DOES THREE OF FIVE WELL; ⭐ you cannot
⭐     tell ***which*** step failed; ⭐ you cannot test steps
⭐     independently; ⭐ you cannot cache anything; ⭐⭐ AND
⭐     IMPROVING ONE STEP RISKS REGRESSING THE OTHER FOUR.

⭐ ✅ THE PIPELINE:
⭐   ① extract parties        → ⭐⭐ CHEAP MODEL, VALIDATE SCHEMA
⭐   ② extract clauses        → cheap model
⭐   ③ classify each clause   → ⭐⭐ CHEAP MODEL, `n` PARALLEL
⭐   ④ assess risk            → ⭐⭐ STRONG MODEL, SMALL INPUT
⭐   ⑤ write the email        → cheap model

⭐⭐ ⇒ AND THE FIVE THINGS YOU GAIN ARE ALL ENGINEERING WINS
⭐   RATHER THAN QUALITY ONES:
⭐   ⭐⭐ ① ***EACH STEP IS INDEPENDENTLY TESTABLE*** — so you have
⭐      five small eval sets instead of one impossible one
⭐      (Day 425).
⭐   ⭐⭐ ② ***EACH STEP CAN USE A DIFFERENT MODEL*** — ⭐ only
⭐      step ④ needs the expensive one, which is Day 421's
⭐      cascade applied ***within*** a task.
⭐   ⭐ ③ Steps 1–3 parallelise (`asyncio.gather`).
⭐   ⭐ ④ Failures are attributable and retryable in isolation.
⭐   ⭐⭐ ⑤ ***INTERMEDIATE RESULTS ARE CACHEABLE AND
⭐      INSPECTABLE*** — ⭐ which is what makes the thing
⭐      debuggable at 3 a.m.
⭐   ⭐ Cost: more calls, more latency, more orchestration.
⭐     ⭐⭐ USUALLY WORTH IT, AND ALWAYS WORTH IT ONCE THE FEATURE
⭐     MATTERS.

⭐⭐ ⇒ ***THIS IS DAY 399's DECOMPOSITION ARGUMENT — SEPARATE THE
⭐   THINGS THAT CHANGE AND FAIL SEPARATELY — ARRIVING INSIDE A
⭐   PROMPT.*** ⭐ And it is why an experienced engineer's first
⭐   move on a failing prompt is to ***split it***, not to reword
⭐   it.
```

# Part 4 — Prompt chaining patterns

```
⭐ ① ⭐ ***SEQUENTIAL*** — output of A is input to B.
⭐    ⭐⭐ THE RISK: ERRORS COMPOUND. ⭐ 90% accurate × 90% × 90%
⭐    ⭐⭐ = 73%. ⭐ Validate between steps, and prefer three steps
⭐    at 98% over five at 90%.
⭐ ② ⭐ ***PARALLEL + AGGREGATE*** — ask N things independently,
⭐    combine. ⭐⭐ ERRORS DO NOT COMPOUND, AND LATENCY IS THE
⭐    ***MAX*** RATHER THAN THE SUM. ⭐ Prefer this shape wherever
⭐    the sub-tasks are independent.
⭐ ③ ⭐ ***MAP-REDUCE*** — chunk a long document, process each,
⭐    combine (Day 415). ⭐⭐ THE REDUCE STEP LOSES CROSS-CHUNK
⭐    DETAIL AND THAT IS UNAVOIDABLE; ⭐ say so when you promise
⭐    the feature.
⭐ ④ ⭐ ***ROUTE-THEN-SPECIALISE*** — a cheap classifier picks
⭐    which specialised prompt to run. ⭐⭐ THE HIGHEST
⭐    VALUE-PER-COMPLEXITY PATTERN IN THIS LIST, ⭐ because each
⭐    specialised prompt is short, cacheable and separately
⭐    testable.
⭐ ⑤ ⭐ ***GENERATE-THEN-VALIDATE*** — produce, then check with
⭐    code (not another model, where code can do it).
⭐    ⭐⭐ ALWAYS PREFER A DETERMINISTIC VALIDATOR: ⭐ a regex, a
⭐    schema, a database lookup, a unit test. ⭐⭐ A SECOND LLM
⭐    CALL TO CHECK THE FIRST IS THE MOST OVERUSED PATTERN IN
⭐    THIS FIELD (DAY 426).
```

# Part 5 — The Python that keeps this maintainable

```python
# ⭐⭐ PROMPTS ARE NOT f-STRINGS SCATTERED THROUGH HANDLERS.
# ⭐   They are versioned artefacts with typed inputs (Day 427).
from jinja2 import Environment, StrictUndefined

_env = Environment(undefined=StrictUndefined)   # ⭐⭐ A MISSING
# ⭐   VARIABLE RAISES INSTEAD OF SILENTLY RENDERING "" — which
# ⭐   is how a prompt loses half its instructions in production
# ⭐   with nothing in the logs.

CLASSIFY = _env.from_string("""\
You classify customer feedback.

Return exactly one category from: bug, feature_request, praise, other.
If the text does not clearly fit, return `other`. Do not invent categories.

{% for ex in examples %}
<example>
<input>{{ ex.text }}</input>
<output>{"reasoning": "{{ ex.why }}", "category": "{{ ex.label }}"}</output>
</example>
{% endfor %}

<feedback>{{ text }}</feedback>

Return JSON with keys `reasoning` then `category`.
""")
# ⭐⭐ NOTE THE KEY ORDER IN THE INSTRUCTION ***AND*** IN THE
# ⭐   EXAMPLES: reasoning FIRST. ⭐ Reverse it and CoT stops
# ⭐   working while still costing you the tokens (Part 2).
```

```python
# ⭐ Parallel decomposition, bounded and timed (Days 398, 420).
async def analyse(contract: str) -> Analysis:
    async with asyncio.timeout(60):
        parties, clauses = await asyncio.gather(
            extract_parties(contract),      # ⭐ cheap model
            extract_clauses(contract),      # ⭐ cheap model
        )
    risks = await asyncio.gather(*[
        classify_clause(c) for c in clauses   # ⭐⭐ N IN PARALLEL
    ])
    # ⭐⭐ ONLY THE SMALL, HARD STEP GETS THE EXPENSIVE MODEL —
    # ⭐   and its input is now tiny, so it is cheap twice over.
    return await assess(parties, list(zip(clauses, risks)),
                        model="strong")
```

# Part 6 — When to stop prompting

```
⭐⭐ ***PROMPTING HAS A CEILING, AND RECOGNISING IT SAVES WEEKS.***
⭐   ⭐ Signs you have hit it:
⭐   ⭐ ① You are on prompt revision 15 and ⭐⭐ EVAL SCORES ARE
⭐      OSCILLATING RATHER THAN CLIMBING.
⭐   ⭐ ② ⭐⭐ FIXING CASE A BREAKS CASE B, REPEATEDLY. ⭐ That is
⭐      the model being at its capability limit for this task,
⭐      not you wording it badly.
⭐   ⭐ ③ The prompt is now 3,000 tokens of accumulated special
⭐      cases ⭐⭐ AND NOBODY DARES DELETE A LINE.
⭐   ⭐ ④ You need more than ~8 few-shot examples to hold
⭐      behaviour.

⭐ ⇒ ⭐⭐ WHAT TO DO INSTEAD, IN ORDER:
⭐   ⭐ ① ***DECOMPOSE*** (Part 3). ⭐⭐ THE FIRST ANSWER, AND
⭐      USUALLY THE RIGHT ONE.
⭐   ⭐ ② ***A STRONGER MODEL*** — ⭐ cheap to test, and it tells
⭐      you whether the task is even possible (Day 417).
⭐   ⭐ ③ ***RETRIEVAL***, if the failures are knowledge failures
⭐      (Day 429).
⭐   ⭐ ④ ***A TOOL***, if the failures are arithmetic or lookup
⭐      failures (Day 438).
⭐   ⭐ ⑤ ***FINE-TUNING***, if the failures are ***format or
⭐      style*** failures and you have hundreds of examples
⭐      (Day 451).
⭐   ⭐⭐ ⑥ AND THE ONE PEOPLE WILL NOT SAY: ***THE FEATURE MAY BE
⭐      A BAD FIT AND THE HONEST MOVE IS TO CHANGE THE PRODUCT***
⭐      — ⭐ narrow the scope, add a human step, or drop it.
⭐      ⭐⭐ SAYING THAT EARLY IS WORTH MORE THAN THREE WEEKS OF
⭐      PROMPT REVISIONS.
```

# 💬 Interview

**1. ⭐⭐ "How many few-shot examples, and which ones?"**
> ⭐ Three to five, and the knee is usually five — ⭐⭐ **twenty is rarely better and costs four times the
> input tokens on every request forever.** ⭐ And which matters more than how many: ⭐⭐ **examples teach
> format and decision boundary far more than they teach the answer — research repeatedly finds even
> scrambled labels retain much of the benefit — so the highest-value examples are edge cases, not
> typical ones. The model already handles typical.** ⭐ The empty input, the ambiguous one, the one that
> should be refused, the one that looks like category A and is B. ⭐ I'd also balance the labels, ⭐⭐ **because
> four "bug" examples out of five measurably biases the classifier**, and put them in the stable
> prefix so they cache. ⭐ And if I genuinely need fifty examples, ⭐⭐ **that's the signal I've found the
> fine-tuning case.**

**2. ⭐⭐ "When does chain-of-thought help?"**
> ⭐ When there are genuine steps — multi-step arithmetic, deduction, planning — ⭐⭐ **because each token
> is a forward pass, so reasoning tokens buy compute, and the answer token is then conditioned on the
> intermediate results rather than having to hold them implicitly.** ⭐ It doesn't help on extraction,
> classification, formatting or translation, ⭐⭐ **where the answer is already in the input and there's
> nothing to reason about — that's the commonest waste, CoT bolted onto a classifier out of habit.**
> ⭐ Two things matter more than whether to use it: ⭐⭐ **the reasoning is not an explanation — it's text
> that precedes the answer, not a trace of why it was produced, so never show it to a user as a
> justification because it looks like an audit trail and isn't.** ⭐ And ⭐⭐ **order is functional: a schema
> with `answer` before `reasoning` produces the answer first and rationalises it, giving you the cost
> of CoT and none of the benefit. Field order in a JSON schema is a functional decision here.**

**3. ⭐⭐ "One prompt or five?"**
> ⭐ Five, and the argument is engineering rather than quality. ⭐⭐ **A mega-prompt does three of five
> things well, you can't tell which step failed, you can't test steps independently, you can't cache
> anything, and improving one step risks regressing the other four.** ⭐ Decomposed, you get five small
> eval sets instead of one impossible one, ⭐⭐ **each step can use a different model so only the hard
> step pays for the expensive one, independent steps parallelise, failures are attributable, and
> intermediate results are cacheable and inspectable — which is what makes it debuggable at 3 a.m.**
> ⭐ It's the same argument as service decomposition, arriving inside a prompt — ⭐⭐ **which is why an
> experienced engineer's first move on a failing prompt is to split it, not to reword it.**

**4. "What chaining patterns do you use?"**
> ⭐ Sequential when there's a real dependency, ⭐⭐ **watching that errors compound — 90% three times is
> 73%, so three steps at 98% beats five at 90%.** ⭐ Parallel-and-aggregate wherever sub-tasks are
> independent, ⭐⭐ **because errors don't compound and latency is the max rather than the sum.**
> ⭐ Map-reduce for long documents, accepting that the reduce step loses cross-chunk detail. ⭐⭐ **Route-then-specialise
> is the best value per unit of complexity, because each specialised prompt is short, cacheable and
> separately testable.** ⭐ And generate-then-validate — ⭐⭐ **but with code, not another model, wherever
> code can do it: a regex, a schema, a database lookup. A second LLM call to check the first is the
> most overused pattern in this field.**

**5. ⭐⭐ "How do you know when to stop prompt engineering?"**
> ⭐ Four signs: ⭐⭐ **eval scores oscillating rather than climbing after revision fifteen; fixing case A
> breaking case B repeatedly, which is the model at its capability limit rather than you wording it
> badly; a 3,000-token prompt of accumulated special cases nobody dares delete a line from; and
> needing more than about eight few-shot examples to hold behaviour.** ⭐ Then, in order: decompose —
> the first answer and usually the right one; try a stronger model, which is cheap and tells you
> whether the task is possible at all; retrieval if the failures are knowledge failures; a tool if
> they're arithmetic or lookup failures; fine-tuning if they're format failures and you have hundreds
> of examples. ⭐⭐ **And the one people won't say: the feature may be a bad fit, and narrowing the scope
> or adding a human step is worth more than three more weeks of prompt revisions.**

**6. "Any Python detail that matters here?"**
> ⭐⭐ **Render prompts with `StrictUndefined`, so a missing template variable raises instead of
> silently rendering an empty string** — that's how a prompt loses half its instructions in production
> with nothing in the logs. ⭐ And keep prompts as versioned artefacts with typed inputs rather than
> f-strings scattered through handlers, ⭐⭐ **because the moment you want to A/B a prompt or attribute a
> quality regression to a change, you need a version identifier on the request.**

## 🎙️ Articulation drill
