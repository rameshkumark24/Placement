# Part 1 — ⭐ Four instrument types, and choosing wrongly

```
⭐⭐ COUNTER — monotonically increasing. ⭐ requests, errors, bytes, retries.
⭐    ⭐⭐ YOU NEVER READ A COUNTER'S VALUE. You read its RATE:
⭐         rate(http_requests_total[5m])
⭐    ⭐ Because it resets to zero on restart, and `rate()` handles that.
⭐    ⭐⭐ A dashboard showing a raw counter is a dashboard showing uptime.
⭐
⭐⭐ GAUGE — goes up and down. ⭐ queue depth, active connections, pool size,
⭐    memory. ⭐⭐ A gauge is a SAMPLE: if it spikes between scrapes, YOU NEVER
⭐    SEE IT. ⭐ That is why "we were never out of connections" is not evidence.
⭐
⭐⭐ HISTOGRAM — pre-defined buckets, counted server-side.
⭐    ⭐ durations, sizes. ⭐⭐ Percentiles are computed at QUERY time from
⭐    buckets, so they are AGGREGATABLE ACROSS INSTANCES. ⭐ This is the one
⭐    you want, and choosing buckets is the whole skill:
⭐         buckets=(.005,.01,.025,.05,.1,.25,.5,1,2.5,5,10)
⭐    ⭐⭐ IF YOUR p99 IS 3 s AND YOUR TOP BUCKET IS 1 s, EVERY PERCENTILE
⭐    ABOVE THAT IS A LIE — it reports the bucket edge, not the value.
⭐
⭐ ⭐ SUMMARY — percentiles computed IN the process.
⭐    ⭐⭐ CANNOT BE AGGREGATED. Three pods' p99s cannot be combined into a
⭐    service p99 — ⭐ see Part 2. ⭐ Prefer histograms almost always.
```

```python
from prometheus_client import Counter, Histogram, Gauge

REQS = Counter("http_requests_total", "", ["method", "route", "status"])
LAT  = Histogram("http_request_duration_seconds", "", ["method", "route"],
                 buckets=(.005,.01,.025,.05,.1,.25,.5,1,2.5,5,10))
INFLIGHT = Gauge("http_requests_in_flight", "")

@app.middleware("http")
async def metrics_mw(request: Request, call_next):
    route = request.scope.get("route")
    label = route.path if route else "unmatched"     # ⭐⭐ THE TEMPLATE, NOT THE URL
    with INFLIGHT.track_inprogress():
        start = time.perf_counter()
        response = await call_next(request)
    REQS.labels(request.method, label, response.status_code).inc()
    LAT.labels(request.method, label).observe(time.perf_counter() - start)
    return response
```

> ⭐⭐ `route.path` is `/orders/{id}`, not `/orders/8412`. ⭐ That single line is Part 3's entire
> lesson, and getting it wrong is the most common way to destroy a Prometheus server.

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ Percentiles, and the two things everyone gets wrong

```
⭐⭐ WRONG #1: YOU CANNOT AVERAGE PERCENTILES.
⭐   ⭐ pod A p99 = 100 ms, pod B p99 = 900 ms.
⭐   ⭐⭐ SERVICE p99 IS NOT 500 ms. It is not derivable from those two numbers
⭐        AT ALL — ⭐ you need the underlying distribution.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THIS IS THE ENTIRE REASON HISTOGRAMS BEAT SUMMARIES: bucket COUNTS
⭐        add across pods, so you sum the buckets and then compute the
⭐        percentile. ⭐ Percentiles do not add; counts do.
⭐        histogram_quantile(0.99, sum(rate(..._bucket[5m])) by (le))
⭐
⭐⭐ WRONG #2: THE MEAN HIDES EVERYTHING THAT MATTERS. (Day 080's point, with
⭐   the number attached.)
⭐   ⭐ 99 requests at 10 ms + 1 request at 5 s ⇒ ⭐ MEAN = 60 ms. Looks fine.
⭐   ⭐⭐ ONE USER WAITED FIVE SECONDS, and at 1,000 rps that is 10 users every
⭐        second having your worst experience — ⭐ permanently, invisibly.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ ALERT ON p95/p99. LOOK AT p50 ONLY TO ASK "did EVERYTHING get
⭐        slower, or only the tail?" ⭐ Those have completely different causes:
⭐        p50 up = systemic (a slow dependency, CPU saturation);
⭐        p99 up with flat p50 = ⭐⭐ contention, GC, a cold cache, one bad
⭐        shard, or a lock — ⭐ i.e. a QUEUEING problem, not a speed problem.
⭐
⭐ ⭐⭐ AND THE ONE THAT SOUNDS LIKE A RIDDLE AND IS ASKED DELIBERATELY:
⭐   ⭐ "if your p99 is 1 s, what fraction of PAGE LOADS are slow?"
⭐   ⭐⭐ A page making 20 API calls is slow if ANY of them is slow:
⭐        1 − 0.99²⁰ ≈ 18%. ⭐ NOT 1%.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ TAIL LATENCY IS AMPLIFIED BY FAN-OUT. That is why p99 matters far
⭐        more than it intuitively should, and why Day 100's fan-out and this
⭐        are the same fact seen twice.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Cardinality: the mistake that takes down monitoring

```
⭐⭐ EVERY UNIQUE COMBINATION OF LABEL VALUES IS A SEPARATE TIME SERIES, EACH
⭐   WITH ITS OWN MEMORY AND INDEX ENTRY — FOREVER.

⭐   ❌ REQS.labels(path="/orders/8412")        ⭐⭐ 1 SERIES PER ORDER ID
⭐   ❌ .labels(user_id=...)                     ⭐⭐ 1 SERIES PER USER
⭐   ❌ .labels(email=...) / .labels(trace_id=…) ⭐⭐ unbounded
⭐   ❌ .labels(error=str(e))                    ⭐ exception text contains ids
⭐
⭐   ⭐⭐ THE ARITHMETIC THAT ENDS THE ARGUMENT:
⭐        10 methods × 200 routes × 12 statuses = 24,000 series. ⭐ Fine.
⭐        ADD user_id WITH 1,000,000 USERS  = ⭐⭐ 24 BILLION SERIES.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ PROMETHEUS OOMs. AND IT DIES DURING THE INCIDENT THAT MADE YOU ADD
⭐        THE LABEL — ⭐ you lose your monitoring exactly when you need it, and
⭐        the outage now has no telemetry at all.
⭐
⭐⭐ THE RULE: A LABEL VALUE MUST COME FROM A SMALL, CLOSED, KNOWN-IN-ADVANCE
⭐   SET. ⭐ method, route TEMPLATE, status class, region, tenant tier, version.
⭐   ⭐⭐ IF YOU CANNOT WRITE DOWN EVERY POSSIBLE VALUE, IT IS NOT A LABEL.
⭐
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ AND THE ANSWER TO "BUT I NEED PER-USER DETAIL": THAT IS WHAT LOGS
⭐        AND TRACES ARE FOR (Days 146, 148). ⭐ High cardinality is precisely
⭐        the axis metrics traded away to be cheap. ⭐⭐ Wanting it back means
⭐        you picked the wrong pillar, not that you need a bigger label set.
```

```
⭐ TWO MORE TRAPS:
⭐   ⭐⭐ `unmatched` FOR UNKNOWN ROUTES. Without it, a 404-scanning bot
⭐     creates a series per URL it invents — ⭐ an attacker can OOM your
⭐     monitoring from outside. ⭐⭐ That is a real, cheap denial of service.
⭐   ⭐ MULTIPROCESS MODE. With N gunicorn workers (Day 111), ⭐⭐ each has its
⭐     own registry, so `/metrics` returns ONE RANDOM WORKER'S numbers and
⭐     your traffic looks like 1/N of reality. ⭐ Fix: `PROMETHEUS_MULTIPROC_DIR`
⭐     + `MultiProcessCollector`, and clean the directory on boot.
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ What to measure: RED and USE

```
⭐⭐ RED — for anything that SERVES REQUESTS. Three metrics, and they are
⭐   sufficient for a first dashboard:
⭐   ⭐ RATE      — requests/sec       ⭐ is traffic normal?
⭐   ⭐ ERRORS    — failures/sec       ⭐⭐ AS A RATIO, not a count: a count
⭐                                        goes up with traffic and tells you
⭐                                        nothing about health
⭐   ⭐ DURATION  — p50/p95/p99
⭐
⭐⭐ USE — for anything that is a RESOURCE (CPU, disk, pool, queue):
⭐   ⭐ UTILISATION — % busy
⭐   ⭐ SATURATION  — ⭐⭐ THE QUEUE. The most predictive signal you have:
⭐        saturation rises BEFORE latency does, because queueing is what
⭐        converts load into latency. ⭐ Connection-pool wait time and queue
⭐        depth are early warnings; latency is the symptom that follows.
⭐   ⭐ ERRORS      — the resource's own failures
⭐
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE THREE PYTHON-SPECIFIC ONES PEOPLE FORGET, EACH TIED TO A DAY:
⭐        ⭐ DB POOL: in-use / waiting / wait-time   (Day 135)
⭐        ⭐ QUEUE DEPTH + OLDEST MESSAGE AGE        (Day 142)
⭐          ⭐⭐ AGE, NOT DEPTH — depth 10 with age 4 hours is a stuck consumer,
⭐             and depth alone cannot tell you that
⭐        ⭐ EVENT-LOOP LAG                          (Day 134)
⭐          ⭐⭐ THE SINGLE BEST DETECTOR OF A BLOCKING CALL IN async def, and
⭐             almost nobody has it. Measure it directly:
```

```python
async def measure_loop_lag(gauge, interval=1.0):
    while True:
        start = time.perf_counter()
        await asyncio.sleep(interval)
        gauge.set((time.perf_counter() - start) - interval)   # ⭐⭐ >50 ms = blocked
```

# Part 5 — ⭐⭐ SLOs, error budgets, and alerts that do not lie

```
⭐⭐ THE VOCABULARY, PRECISELY, BECAUSE INTERVIEWERS TEST THE DISTINCTION:
⭐   ⭐ SLI — a MEASUREMENT. "fraction of requests < 300 ms and non-5xx"
⭐   ⭐ SLO — a TARGET on that SLI. "99.9% over 30 days" ⭐⭐ (internal)
⭐   ⭐ SLA — a CONTRACT with money attached ⭐ (external, always looser)
⭐
⭐⭐ THE ERROR BUDGET IS THE IDEA THAT MAKES SLOs USEFUL:
⭐   ⭐ 99.9% over 30 days ⇒ ⭐⭐ 43 MINUTES of failure you are ALLOWED.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THAT IS A BUDGET TO SPEND, NOT A FAILURE TO AVOID. Budget left ⇒
⭐        ship faster, take risks. Budget gone ⇒ ⭐ feature freeze, fix
⭐        reliability. ⭐⭐ It converts "should we ship or stabilise?" from an
⭐        argument into a NUMBER — that is the whole point, and it is the
⭐        answer to give.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ AND 100% IS THE WRONG TARGET: it is unachievable, and the last
⭐        nine costs more than the feature you did not build.

⭐⭐ ALERTING — THE FOUR RULES, EACH FROM A REAL FAILURE:
⭐  1. ⭐⭐ ALERT ON SYMPTOMS, NOT CAUSES. "error ratio > 1%" — ⭐ NOT "CPU >
⭐       80%". ⭐⭐ High CPU with everyone served fine is a machine doing its
⭐       job; a cause-alert wakes you for a non-problem and MISSES the
⭐       problems whose cause you did not think of.
⭐  2. ⭐⭐ EVERY PAGE MUST BE ACTIONABLE AND URGENT. If the answer is "look at
⭐       it tomorrow", it is a TICKET. ⭐ Non-actionable pages train people to
⭐       ignore pages, ⭐⭐ and then the real one is ignored too.
⭐  3. ⭐ ALERT ON BURN RATE, NOT INSTANTANEOUS VALUES. ⭐⭐ MULTI-WINDOW:
⭐       fast burn (14.4× over 1 h) pages; slow burn (3× over 6 h) tickets.
⭐       ⭐ This is what stops a 30-second blip from waking you while still
⭐       catching a slow bleed that will exhaust the month.
⭐  4. ⭐⭐ ALERT ON ABSENCE. `rate(...[5m]) == 0` — ⭐ a service that stopped
⭐       receiving traffic looks PERFECT on every other metric: no errors, no
⭐       latency. ⭐⭐ THE HEALTHIEST-LOOKING DASHBOARD IS A DEAD SERVICE.
```

```
⭐ AND `/metrics` IS AN INFORMATION LEAK — ⭐⭐ route names, versions, tenant
⭐   labels, traffic volumes. ⭐ Bind it to an internal port or require auth.
⭐   ⭐⭐ It is the endpoint people forget when they list what is public.
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Graphing a raw counter | It resets on restart; you want `rate()`. |
| Using a summary for latency | Percentiles can't be aggregated across pods. Histograms add bucket counts. |
| Averaging percentiles across instances | Mathematically meaningless. Sum buckets, then compute. |
| Alerting on the mean | 99 fast + 1 five-second request averages to "fine". Alert on p95/p99. |
| Histogram buckets that top out below your p99 | Every percentile above the top bucket is a fabricated number. |
| A high-cardinality label (user id, path, trace id) | Series count explodes; Prometheus OOMs during the incident that caused it. |
| Raw URL instead of the route template | A 404-scanning bot can OOM your monitoring from outside. |
| No `unmatched` bucket for unknown routes | Same problem, arriving as unbounded label values. |
| Prometheus client with gunicorn workers, no multiprocess mode | `/metrics` returns one random worker; traffic looks like 1/N. |
| Error **count** instead of error **ratio** | Counts rise with traffic and say nothing about health. |
| Queue depth without oldest-message age | Depth 10 with age 4 hours is a stuck consumer, and depth can't show it. |
| No event-loop lag metric | The best detector of a blocking call in `async def`, and almost nobody has it. |
| Alerting on CPU or memory | Cause-alerts wake you for non-problems and miss unanticipated causes. |
| Pages that aren't urgent | They train people to ignore pages, including the real one. |
| No burn-rate windows | Either a blip wakes you or a slow bleed goes unnoticed. |
| No alert on traffic absence | A dead service has zero errors and perfect latency. |
| `/metrics` exposed publicly | Leaks routes, versions, tenants and volumes. |

## Interview questions

**Q: Why histograms rather than summaries?**

> Because summaries compute percentiles inside the process, and percentiles don't aggregate. If pod
> A reports p99 of 100 ms and pod B reports 900 ms, the service p99 isn't 500 ms — it isn't derivable
> from those two numbers at all. A histogram exports bucket counts, and counts add: sum the buckets
> across pods, then compute the quantile. The trade is that you must choose buckets in advance, and
> the trap there is that if your p99 is three seconds and your top bucket is one second, every
> percentile above it is a fabricated number equal to the bucket edge.

**Q: You add `user_id` as a metric label to debug an incident. What happens?**

> Your monitoring dies. Every unique label combination is a separate time series with its own memory
> and index entry. Method times route times status is maybe twenty-four thousand series, which is
> fine; multiply by a million users and it's twenty-four billion. Prometheus OOMs — and it does so
> during the incident that made you add the label, so you lose telemetry exactly when you need it
> most. The rule is that a label value must come from a small closed set you could write down in
> advance. If you can't enumerate it, it's not a label. And the honest answer to "but I need per-user
> detail" is that high cardinality is precisely what metrics traded away to be cheap — that's what
> logs and traces are for. Wanting it back means you reached for the wrong pillar.

**Q: Your p99 is 1 second and p50 is 20 ms. Is that a problem?**

> Depends on fan-out, and the arithmetic is worse than intuition suggests. If a page makes twenty API
> calls and is slow when any one of them is slow, the probability of a slow page is one minus
> 0.99 to the twentieth — about eighteen percent, not one percent. So a "one percent" tail is
> nearly a fifth of page loads. That's also diagnostic: a flat p50 with a rising p99 isn't a speed
> problem, it's a queueing problem — contention, a lock, GC, a cold cache, one bad shard. If p50 rose
> too, I'd look for something systemic like a slow dependency or CPU saturation.

**Q: What's an error budget and what is it for?**

> If the SLO is 99.9% over thirty days, the error budget is the 0.1% — about forty-three minutes of
> failure you're allowed. The point is that it's a budget to spend, not a failure to avoid. Budget
> remaining means ship faster and take risks; budget exhausted means freeze features and fix
> reliability. It converts "should we ship or stabilise?" from an argument between engineering and
> product into a number both sides already agreed on. And it's why 100% is the wrong target — it's
> unachievable, and the last nine costs more than the feature you didn't build to get it.

**Q: What would you alert on?**

> Symptoms, not causes: error ratio and latency against the SLO, not CPU or memory. High CPU while
> every request is served fine is a machine doing its job, and cause-based alerts both wake you for
> non-problems and miss the causes you didn't anticipate. I'd use multi-window burn rate — a fast
> burn over an hour pages, a slow burn over six hours files a ticket — so a thirty-second blip
> doesn't wake anyone but a slow bleed that will exhaust the month still gets caught. And I'd alert
> on the *absence* of traffic, because a service that stopped receiving requests has zero errors and
> perfect latency: the healthiest-looking dashboard is a dead service.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes: "What would you monitor for this service?"**

Start with RED for the service and USE for its resources, and name saturation as the leading
indicator because queueing is what turns load into latency. Then the three people forget: pool wait
time, oldest message age rather than depth, and event-loop lag as the blocking-call detector. Say
histograms not summaries, with the aggregation reason. Then move up a level to SLIs, an SLO and an
error budget, and explain the budget as a decision rule rather than a scoreboard. Close on alerting:
**symptoms not causes, burn rate not instantaneous values, and an alert for the absence of traffic —
because a dead service looks perfect on every other metric.**
