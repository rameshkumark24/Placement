**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** BUILD

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: an aggregate is not "a group of related tables". It is the set of data
> that must change *atomically together*, which means it is defined by your invariants and by your
> contention, not by your entity-relationship diagram.**
>
> ⭐ Today you turn Day 249's stories into tables. ⭐⭐ Everything from Stage 5 applies at once —
> and the point of doing it here rather than there is that these are *your* tables, and getting one
> wrong today costs you a migration on Day 273.

# Part 1 — ⭐ From stories to entities, with a real test

```
⭐   THE FOLK METHOD: "underline the nouns."
⭐   ⭐⭐ IT OVER-PRODUCES. In Day 249's stories, "availability",
⭐     "window", "clash" and "status" are all nouns and none is a table.

⭐⭐ THE THREE QUESTIONS THAT ACTUALLY DECIDE:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ DOES IT HAVE AN IDENTITY THAT SURVIVES ITS ATTRIBUTES?
⭐      ⭐ If every field changed, would it still be "the same one"?
⭐      ⭐⭐ Yes ⇒ an ENTITY (a job, a technician). No ⇒ a VALUE (a time
⭐        window, a money amount, an address).
⭐   ② ⭐ DOES ANYTHING REFER TO IT INDEPENDENTLY?
⭐      ⭐⭐ No ⇒ it is probably part of something else, not a table.
⭐   ③ ⭐ DOES IT HAVE A LIFECYCLE OF ITS OWN?
⭐      ⭐⭐ A `work_item` is created, edited and completed on its own
⭐        schedule ⇒ entity. An `address` is replaced wholesale ⇒ value.

⭐⭐ AND THE ONE THAT CATCHES PEOPLE: A JOIN TABLE WITH ATTRIBUTES IS AN
⭐   ENTITY (Day 188). `assignment` is not "job_technicians" — it has a
⭐   time window, a state, an assigner and an assigned-at. ⭐ Name it
⭐   from the domain and give it its own surrogate key.
```

```
⭐ `fieldwork` ENTITIES, and what each is for:
⭐   tenant       ⭐⭐ the isolation boundary. Everything else has this.
⭐   user         ⭐ owner | dispatcher | technician
⭐   customer     ⭐ who is billed
⭐   site         ⭐ where the work happens (⭐⭐ separate from customer —
⭐                  one customer, many sites; ⭐ and the address on a
⭐                  JOB is a SNAPSHOT of the site's, Day 188)
⭐   job          ⭐⭐ the aggregate root. Holds the state machine.
⭐   assignment   ⭐⭐ technician × job × time window. The race lives here.
⭐   work_item    ⭐ what was actually done, and its price
⭐   photo        ⭐ evidence, uploaded from the field
⭐   invoice      ⭐⭐ money. Its own root.
⭐   payment      ⭐ what the provider told us, including "we don't know"
⭐   outbox       ⭐ Day 142's table, first-class from the start
⭐   idempotency_key ⭐⭐ also first-class. Day 184 #6.
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ The aggregate boundary: the decision that shapes everything

```
⭐⭐ AN AGGREGATE IS DEFINED BY: ***WHAT MUST BE CONSISTENT AT EVERY
⭐   COMMIT.*** Ask, for each invariant: "could this be violated if two
⭐   people acted at the same time on different rows?"

⭐   INVARIANT                                    ⇒ AGGREGATE
⭐   a job's state transitions are legal          ⇒ ⭐ inside `job`
⭐   work_items belong to exactly one job and     ⇒ ⭐ inside `job`
⭐     can only be added while `in_progress`
⭐   ⭐⭐ a technician has no overlapping           ⇒ ⭐⭐ ***NOT INSIDE
⭐     assignments                                     ANY AGGREGATE***
⭐   an invoice's total equals the sum of its     ⇒ ⭐ inside `invoice`
⭐     lines at issue time
⭐   an invoice cannot be issued for an           ⇒ ⭐⭐ ACROSS TWO
⭐     unfinished job                                  aggregates
```

```
⭐⭐ THE TWO ROWS THAT DO NOT FIT ARE THE INTERESTING ONES, AND EACH HAS
⭐   A DIFFERENT ANSWER:

⭐ ⓐ ⭐⭐ THE OVERLAP INVARIANT SPANS ROWS THAT NO SINGLE AGGREGATE OWNS
⭐    — it is a rule about a technician's *whole schedule*. ⭐ This is
⭐    Day 195's WRITE SKEW, exactly: two transactions read a set, each
⭐    decides, each writes a different row, and the rule breaks with
⭐    both committed and no error.
⭐    ⇒ ⭐⭐ SO IT GOES WHERE A BUG CANNOT REACH IT (Day 184 #2):
⭐        ALTER TABLE assignments ADD CONSTRAINT no_double_booking
⭐          EXCLUDE USING gist (technician_id WITH =, during WITH &&)
⭐          WHERE (status <> 'cancelled');
⭐      ⭐ `btree_gist` for the equality part; ⭐⭐ the partial predicate
⭐        is essential or cancelled assignments block re-booking.
⭐    ⇒ ⭐⭐ FOUR LINES OF DDL REPLACE A LOCK, A RETRY LOOP AND A RACE
⭐      YOU WOULD HAVE LOST.

⭐ ⓑ ⭐ "Cannot invoice an unfinished job" spans two aggregates, so it
⭐    is enforced in the SERVICE, inside one transaction, by reading the
⭐    job `FOR UPDATE` (or by a conditional `UPDATE ... WHERE status =
⭐    'completed'` and checking the rowcount — ⭐⭐ preferred, because it
⭐    is one statement and needs no lock).
```

```
⭐⭐ AND THE SECOND JOB OF THE BOUNDARY, WHICH PEOPLE MISS: ***AN
⭐   AGGREGATE IS A CONTENTION UNIT.*** If you make `tenant` the
⭐   aggregate root "so everything is consistent", every write in the
⭐   company serialises on one row. ⭐ Big aggregates are correct and
⭐   slow; small aggregates are fast and push invariants outward into
⭐   constraints. ⭐⭐ Prefer small, and put the cross-aggregate rules in
⭐   the database.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ The schema, with every Stage 5 rule applied

```
⭐⭐ RUN DAY 188'S CHECKLIST OVER YOUR ERD BEFORE YOU WRITE MIGRATION
⭐   0001. Here it is, applied:

⭐ ① ⭐⭐ EVERY TABLE HAS `tenant_id`, NOT NULL, IN EVERY INDEX'S
⭐    LEADING POSITION. ⭐ Day 190's E-S-R: tenant is always an equality
⭐    predicate, so it is always first.
⭐ ② ⭐⭐ SNAPSHOT vs CURRENT, decided per field and written in a comment:
⭐      job.site_address_snapshot ⇒ ⭐⭐ SNAPSHOT (where we actually went)
⭐      work_item.unit_price_cents ⇒ ⭐⭐ SNAPSHOT (what we charged)
⭐      job.customer_id            ⇒ ⭐ reference (who they are now)
⭐      invoice.customer_name      ⇒ ⭐⭐ SNAPSHOT (who it was issued to)
⭐ ③ ⭐ `timestamptz` everywhere. `date` only for a genuine calendar
⭐    date. ⭐⭐ And `during tstzrange` on assignment — a real range
⭐    type, because the EXCLUDE constraint needs one and because
⭐    "start + end as two columns" makes every overlap query wrong.
⭐ ④ ⭐ Money: `bigint` minor units. One currency (Day 249's out-list).
⭐ ⑤ ⭐⭐ `NOT NULL` BY DEFAULT; each nullable column justified in a
⭐    comment. ⭐ Day 187: every nullable column is an unwritten branch.
⭐ ⑥ ⭐ Status: `text` + a `CHECK` (not a native enum — Day 187's
⭐    "who adds a value" test says developers, rarely, via migration).
⭐ ⑦ ⭐⭐ CHECK CONSTRAINTS FOR REAL INVARIANTS:
⭐      CHECK (total_cents >= 0)
⭐      CHECK (lower(during) < upper(during))
⭐      CHECK (status <> 'invoiced' OR invoiced_at IS NOT NULL)
⭐ ⑧ ⭐⭐ PARTIAL UNIQUE INDEXES for "only one at a time":
⭐      UNIQUE (job_id) WHERE status IN ('active') -- one live assignment
⭐      UNIQUE (tenant_id, lower(email)) WHERE deleted_at IS NULL
⭐ ⑨ ⭐⭐ EVERY FOREIGN KEY IS INDEXED (Day 189 — Postgres does not do
⭐    it for you, and an unindexed FK makes every parent delete a scan).
⭐ ⑩ ⭐ `ON DELETE`: `RESTRICT` everywhere by default. ⭐⭐ Exactly one
⭐    `CASCADE`, and only where the child is meaningless alone
⭐    (`photo` → `work_item`). ⭐ Day 188: cascade on a user is how an
⭐    admin click deletes a million rows.
⭐ ⑪ ⭐⭐ HARD DELETE + AN ARCHIVE TABLE, not `deleted_at` — except
⭐    where a soft delete is a genuine domain state, in which case call
⭐    it `cancelled` and model it as a state. ⭐ Day 188.
⭐ ⑫ ⭐ `created_at`/`updated_at` on everything — ⭐⭐ and do NOT index
⭐    `updated_at` (Day 189: it kills HOT updates for the whole table).
```

```sql
-- ⭐⭐ THE TWO TABLES THAT ARE EASY TO POSTPONE AND MUST NOT BE:
CREATE TABLE outbox (                    -- ⭐ Day 142/166
    id            bigint GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY PRIMARY KEY,
    tenant_id     bigint NOT NULL,
    event_type    text   NOT NULL,
    payload       jsonb  NOT NULL,
    created_at    timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    processed_at  timestamptz,
    attempts      int    NOT NULL DEFAULT 0
);
CREATE INDEX ON outbox (created_at) WHERE processed_at IS NULL;  -- ⭐⭐

CREATE TABLE idempotency_keys (          -- ⭐ Day 184 #6
    tenant_id     bigint NOT NULL,
    key           text   NOT NULL,
    request_hash  text   NOT NULL,       -- ⭐⭐ so a REUSED key with a
    response_code int,                   --    DIFFERENT body is a 422,
    response_body jsonb,                 --    not a wrong replay
    created_at    timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, key)
);
-- ⭐⭐ BOTH IN MIGRATION 0001. Adding them later means retrofitting
--    every write path, which is the same argument as Day 249's
--    "you may not defer a cross-cutting concern".
```

# Part 5 — ⭐ The state machine, as data

```
⭐⭐ DAY 249's TABLE BECOMES A DATA STRUCTURE — NOT A CHAIN OF `if`s
⭐   SCATTERED ACROSS FOUR MODULES.

⭐   TRANSITIONS = {
⭐     ("draft",       "schedule"): "scheduled",
⭐     ("scheduled",   "dispatch"): "dispatched",
⭐     ("dispatched",  "start"):    "in_progress",
⭐     ("in_progress", "complete"): "completed",
⭐     ("completed",   "invoice"):  "invoiced",
⭐     ("invoiced",    "pay"):      "paid",
⭐     **{(s, "cancel"): "cancelled"
⭐        for s in ("draft","scheduled","dispatched")},
⭐   }
⭐   ⭐⭐ NOTE WHAT THE DATA STRUCTURE MAKES OBVIOUS: `in_progress` is
⭐     NOT in the cancel list. ⭐ That was Day 249's `?`, and it is now
⭐     visible in one place instead of implied by the absence of an
⭐     `elif`.
⭐   ⭐ Day 254 gives it behaviour. Today it just has to exist and be
⭐     complete.
⭐   ⭐⭐ AND MIRROR IT IN THE DATABASE: a CHECK on the status values,
⭐     so a bad UPDATE from psql cannot create a state your code has
⭐     never heard of.
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Underlining nouns to find entities | It over-produces. Use identity, independent reference, and lifecycle. |
| Treating a join table as plumbing | With attributes it's an entity. Name it from the domain. |
| Defining aggregates by relationships | They're defined by what must be consistent at every commit. |
| Making the tenant the aggregate root | Every write in the company then serialises on one row. |
| Enforcing the overlap rule in the service | It's write skew — two rows, no conflict to detect. Use `EXCLUDE`. |
| `EXCLUDE` without a partial predicate | Cancelled assignments block re-booking forever. |
| Start and end as two timestamp columns | Every overlap query is subtly wrong. Use a range type. |
| `tenant_id` missing from index leading position | It's always an equality predicate; E-S-R puts it first. |
| Not deciding snapshot vs current per field | Half of all consistency problems are snapshots mistaken for caches. |
| Nullable by default | Every nullable column is an unwritten branch in every query. |
| `ON DELETE CASCADE` by default | One admin click deletes a million rows. |
| Adding `outbox` and `idempotency_keys` later | Retrofitting them means rewriting every write path. |
| An idempotency table with no request hash | A reused key with a different body replays the wrong response. |
| Indexing `updated_at` | It disables heap-only-tuple updates for the whole table. |
| A state machine as scattered `if`s | The missing transition is invisible; as data it's obvious. |
| No invariants table | You can't answer "where is that enforced?" without reading code. |

## Interview questions

**Q: How do you decide what belongs in an aggregate?**

> By the invariants, not the relationships. The question is what must be consistent at every commit —
> so a job's legal state transitions and its work items are inside the job aggregate, because
> violating either requires acting on the job itself. The interesting cases are the ones that don't
> fit. "A technician has no overlapping assignments" is a rule about the technician's whole schedule,
> which no single aggregate owns, and trying to enforce it in a service is exactly write skew: two
> transactions read the schedule, each decides it's free, each writes a different row, and the rule
> breaks with both committed and no error anywhere. So it goes into a Postgres `EXCLUDE` constraint
> over the technician and the time range — four lines of DDL replacing a lock, a retry loop and a race
> I'd have lost. The second thing I'd say is that an aggregate is also a contention unit: making the
> tenant the root would make everything consistent and serialise every write in the company on one row,
> so I prefer small aggregates and push cross-aggregate rules into constraints.

**Q: Walk me through one schema decision you'd defend.**

> Storing the assignment's time window as a `tstzrange` rather than two timestamp columns. The
> immediate reason is that the `EXCLUDE` constraint needs a range type with the overlap operator, so
> it's what makes the no-double-booking guarantee expressible in the database at all. But I'd defend it
> even without that, because two-column ranges make every overlap query subtly wrong — people write
> `a.start < b.end AND a.end > b.start` and then argue about whether the boundaries are inclusive,
> differently, in three places. A range type has explicit bounds semantics and one operator. The cost
> is that it's less familiar and the ORM mapping needs a moment's thought, which I think is a fair
> trade for a correctness property enforced by the database.

**Q: What's the invariants table?**

> One row per business rule, with where it's enforced and how it's proved. It's the artefact I'd point
> at first in a review, because it answers "where does that live?" without anyone reading code, and
> because the act of filling it in moves rules to better places. The rule I apply is that if the
> enforcement column says "the service", I ask once more whether a constraint could do it — and in my
> schema two of eight rows started as service checks and became database constraints as a result: the
> overlap rule and the one-active-assignment rule. The proof column matters just as much, because a
> rule with no test is a comment. For the concurrency ones the proof is a two-hundred-thread test that
> asserts an exact count, which is the only kind of evidence that means anything for a race.

**Q: Why put the outbox and idempotency tables in the first migration?**

> Same reason you can't defer authorisation: they're cross-cutting, so the cost of adding them is
> proportional to how much code already exists. Every write path that talks to a third party needs the
> outbox, and every unsafe endpoint needs an idempotency key — retrofitting means revisiting all of
> them, and in practice it means some get missed. The detail I'd mention on the idempotency table is
> storing a hash of the request body alongside the key, so that a client reusing a key with a
> *different* payload gets a 422 rather than a replay of an unrelated response. That's a subtle failure
> mode and it's much easier to design in than to discover.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Three minutes: walk someone through your ERD.**

Start with the aggregate boundaries rather than the tables, because that's the design and the tables
are the consequence: what must be consistent at every commit, and therefore what is one unit. Then
name the invariant that fits in no aggregate and what you did about it — **the overlap rule is write
skew, so it lives in an `EXCLUDE` constraint rather than in a service, because two transactions
writing different rows give the database nothing to detect.** Then two schema decisions with reasons:
the range type and one snapshot-versus-current call. Then the invariants table, and say the rule out
loud: **if the enforcement column says "the service", ask once more whether a constraint could do it.**
Close on the two tables you put in migration 0001 that most people add in month three, and why the
cost of adding a cross-cutting concern scales with the code that already exists.
