# Part 3 — ⭐ The ten-minute walkthrough

```
⭐⭐ REHEARSE THIS UNTIL IT IS BORING. ⭐ It is the single highest-leverage
⭐   artefact of the whole stage, because it is what an interviewer actually
⭐   experiences.

⭐  ⭐ 0:00 THE PROBLEM (1 min)
⭐       ⭐⭐ One sentence, then the ONE hard thing: "selling a fixed inventory
⭐       to a spike of concurrent buyers, where the payment step takes ten
⭐       seconds against a third party."
⭐
⭐  ⭐ 1:00 THE SHAPE (2 min)
⭐       ⭐ API → services → repositories → Postgres, plus Celery and Redis.
⭐       ⭐⭐ Name the one architectural rule (services import no FastAPI) and
⭐       say what it BOUGHT you — the worker on Day 166, for free.
⭐
⭐  ⭐⭐ 3:00 THE HARD PART (4 min) — ⭐ SPEND YOUR TIME HERE
⭐       ⭐ the hold as a short lock replacing a long one; three oversell
⭐       defences with the constraint as the one that can't be buggy; the
⭐       outbox; `payment_unknown` and reconciliation.
⭐       ⭐⭐ THIS IS THE PART THAT DISTINGUISHES YOU. Most candidates describe
⭐       CRUD. ⭐ You are describing a race, a third party, and a state that
⭐       means "I don't know".
⭐
⭐  ⭐ 7:00 THE EVIDENCE (2 min)
⭐       ⭐⭐ the 200-thread test · the four-line load-test block · the alert
⭐       that pages · the N+1 the ceiling caught in CI.
⭐       ⭐ Numbers, not adjectives.
⭐
⭐  ⭐⭐ 9:00 THE LIMITS (1 min)
⭐       ⭐ "~330 purchases/sec per ticket type because of the row lock; more
⭐       machines don't help; here's what I'd change if we needed 5,000."
⭐       ⭐⭐ ENDING ON A STATED LIMIT IS STRONGER THAN ENDING ON A CLAIM. ⭐ It
⭐       is the clearest possible signal that you measured rather than hoped.
```

```
⭐⭐ FOUR RULES FOR THE TELLING:
⭐   ⭐ NUMBERS, NOT ADJECTIVES. "p99 240 ms at 1,150 rps", never "it's fast".
⭐   ⭐⭐ NAME ONE THING THAT WENT WRONG. A walkthrough with no failure in it
⭐        sounds rehearsed, because it is — ⭐ and the recovery is the story.
⭐   ⭐ SAY "I DON'T KNOW" WHEN YOU DON'T. ⭐⭐ Then say how you'd find out.
⭐        That answer scores better than a confident wrong one, every time.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ NEVER SAY "IT JUST WORKS". ⭐ Everything in this service works for a
⭐        reason you can name, and naming it is the entire exercise.
```

⭐   ⭐⭐ THE SIX FACTS THE STAGE RESTS ON:
⭐   ⭐ **A framework is a set of defaults you should be able to derive** —
⭐        FastAPI is Starlette plus Pydantic plus signature inspection, and
⭐        everything it gives you, you built in Stage 3.
⭐   ⭐ **`def` and `async def` are different execution models**, and the
⭐        dangerous combination is blocking code inside `async def`.
⭐   ⭐⭐ **Two versions of your code are always live** — which is why
⭐        expand/contract answers migrations, cache keys, event schemas and
⭐        API versioning with the same shape.
⭐   ⭐⭐ **At-least-once is the only delivery guarantee that exists**, so
⭐        idempotency is not a feature, it is the price of admission.
⭐   ⭐ **Put the guarantee where a bug cannot reach it** — a constraint, a
⭐        policy, a required argument — rather than where someone must
⭐        remember it.
⭐   ⭐⭐ **A timeout means "I don't know"**, and systems that survive are the
⭐        ones that model the third outcome.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Ticking a box you can't demonstrate | An unchecked box is a ticket. A falsely ticked one is a failed follow-up question. |
| Rating yourself generously | An interviewer finds the gap in two follow-ups. A 3 you admit beats a 5 you can't defend. |
| Re-reading a weak topic instead of rebuilding it | Recognition isn't recall, and interviews test recall. |
| A walkthrough that's all architecture | Spend your time on the one hard thing, not on the folder layout. |
| Adjectives instead of numbers | "It's fast" is unfalsifiable; "p99 240 ms at 1,150 rps" is evidence. |
| A walkthrough with no failure in it | Sounds rehearsed. The recovery *is* the story. |
| Ending on a claim rather than a limit | A stated ceiling proves you measured; a claim proves nothing. |
| Bluffing an answer | "I don't know, here's how I'd find out" scores higher than a confident wrong answer. |
| No "what I'd do differently" document | It's the cheapest, strongest signal of self-evaluation you can produce. |
| Treating this gate as a formality | The forty questions exist to find the days you need to re-read. |

## Interview questions

**Q: Tell me about a project you're proud of.**

> A ticketing API — the interesting part is selling a fixed inventory to a spike of concurrent buyers
> when the payment step takes ten seconds against a third party, which rules out the obvious
> solutions. You can't hold a database lock across the payment call, and you can't decrement only
> after payment or you oversell. So there's a hold: a short-lived reservation that replaces a long
> lock. Three layers stop overselling — a row lock, a conditional update, and a `CHECK` constraint,
> because my lock can have a bug and the constraint cannot. Under a two-hundred-thread test against
> ten tickets, exactly ten sell, every time. And the state I'm most pleased with is
> `payment_unknown`: a timeout doesn't mean the charge failed, so there's a third state and a
> reconciliation job that resolves it, because without that job it's somewhere orders go and never
> leave.

**Q: What's the limit of your design?**

> About three hundred and thirty purchases per second per ticket type, and I can tell you exactly
> why: every purchase serialises on one row under `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE`, and the locked section is
> around three milliseconds. That's a design property, not a tuning problem, so adding machines
> doesn't help — which is the useful thing to know about it. If I needed five thousand, I'd shard the
> counter into buckets and pick one at random, accepting some complexity and some unfairness at the
> tail of a sellout, or move to queue-based allocation. Browsing scales fine horizontally; the cache
> does most of that work anyway.

**Q: What would you do differently?**

> Several things. I'd make `payment_unknown` a first-class state from day one — I added it after a
> timeout bit me, and the retrofit touched five files it shouldn't have, which is the classic cost of
> discovering a state late. I'd shard the inventory counter from the start if this were a real
> ticketing product, because that decision is cheap now and expensive later. And honestly, I
> over-tested the CRUD paths and under-tested the failure paths, so my coverage number was
> flattering — the tests I actually rely on are the concurrency ones and the authorisation ones, and
> those came last when they should have come first.

**Q: What was the hardest bug?**

> Not a bug exactly — a measurement. p99 was terrible under load with the database idle and the app
> idle, which is the confusing combination. Flat CPU with rising latency means blocked rather than
> busy, so I didn't profile; I looked at saturation signals and found pool wait time up a
> hundredfold, and the widest trace span was `db.acquire` rather than the query. Twenty connections
> serving thirty-two threads. What makes it worth telling is that in a trace it's indistinguishable
> from "the database is slow" unless you instrumented acquire time separately — so the fix was
> trivial and the diagnosis was the whole exercise.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Ten minutes: the full walkthrough, recorded.**

Problem, shape, hard part, evidence, limits — with four minutes on the hard part and one on the
limits. No notes. Then listen back with a pen and mark three things: every adjective that should have
been a number, every place you said "it just works", and every moment you sounded like you were
reciting rather than explaining. Re-record. **The second take is always shorter and always better,
and the gap between them is exactly the material you had not actually internalised.**
