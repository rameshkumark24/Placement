## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Leading with features and frameworks | Visible in thirty seconds and almost uninformative. |
| "I used X" with no alternative | A fact, not a method. Name what you considered and what decided it. |
| Collapsing under "isn't that over-engineered?" | Give the cost, what it bought, and when you'd agree. |
| Lecturing in response to a challenge | Also a fail. Answer in three sentences. |
| Two projects of the same shape | They prove one thing. |
| Not knowing which project to lead with | Read the job; the skill is selection, not volume. |
| Never mentioning the audit | For legacy-modernisation roles it's the closest thing to the job. |
| Listing technologies when asked what you believe | The cross-project comparison generalises; a stack list doesn't. |
| Answers over ninety seconds | Reads as rambling rather than depth. |
| Answering "nothing" to what you're unsure about | That question is failed by that answer. |
| Demoing only the success path | The refusal is what shows engineering. |
| A demo that needs preparation | Then it isn't a demo, it's a rehearsal. |
| Self-assessment by feeling | A 3 with evidence beats a 5 without. |
| Treating a low score as a verdict | It's a study plan. |
| A red or untagged `main` | The story becomes maintenance you didn't do. |

## Interview questions

**Q: Tell me about a project.**

> I'd ask what kind of work the role involves first, because I'd pick a different one. There's a
> multi-tenant transactional system where the hard parts were concurrency, money and tenancy — proven
> by fifty concurrent bookings resolving to exactly one, with the guarantee in an `EXCLUDE` constraint
> rather than in application code. There's a rules-and-retrieval system where there is no right
> answer, only a measured one, and the deliverable is a table showing recall@10 going from 0.62 to
> 0.91 with the change that caused each step. And there's an audit of my own older codebase, which is
> the closest of the three to what most jobs actually are.

**Q: What do you believe about building software?**

> Four things, and I'd show them holding across two very different systems. Put the guarantee in the
> layer that can enforce it — a database constraint in one, a rule engine the model cannot reach in
> the other. Never trust an untrusted producer — a request schema in one, a verified quoted span in
> the other. Treat silence as the enemy, because both systems fail quietly: dead-man alerts on one, a
> scheduled evaluation run on the other. And store what you decided rather than recomputing it, which
> is why an order line keeps its price and a decision record keeps its rule-set version.

**Q: Isn't the outbox over-engineered for a project this size?**

> It's a fair challenge, and the honest answer is that it cost me about eighty lines and one
> background process. What it bought is that the state change and the notification can't diverge —
> which was one of my twelve requirements, because the domain involves money. If the system had no
> money path and no external side effects I'd agree it was unnecessary, and I'd have used an
> `after_commit` hook. What I wouldn't accept is doing it without knowing why, which is why it has an
> ADR with the failure it prevents written out as four outcomes.

**Q: What are you still unsure about?**

> Three things specifically. Whether my refusal threshold generalises — it was calibrated on forty
> questions, which doesn't support the precision I'm quoting. Whether my rule-conflict strategy holds
> at ten times the rule count, because most-restrictive-wins is safe but may become too conservative
> to be useful. And I've never operated any of this under sustained real traffic with real users, so
> everything I know about its behaviour comes from load tests and chaos experiments I designed myself,
> which means I mostly found the failures I already suspected.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Five minutes: the full Stage 7 defence.**

Ninety seconds on the flagship: the problem, the three hard parts, and one proof — **fifty concurrent
bookings resolving to exactly one, with the guarantee in a constraint rather than in application
code.** Ninety seconds on `clearline`: why it's deliberately a different shape, because **a bug there
is fluent and confident rather than a 500**, and the deliverable is a table of changes and the numbers
they moved. Sixty seconds on the audit, framed as the thing most jobs actually are: **you can't find
bugs by reading code you wrote, so you audit with specific questions**, you write a failing test that
proves the bug before you fix it, and **you fix the criticals and record the rest with triggers,
because fixing everything is a rewrite in disguise.** Then sixty seconds on what unifies them, which
is the part almost nobody prepares: **put the guarantee where it can be enforced, never trust an
untrusted producer, treat silence as the enemy, and store what you decided rather than recomputing
it** — each one demonstrated in both systems. Close on thirty seconds of honest uncertainty: the
threshold calibrated on too few questions, the conflict strategy untested at scale, and the fact that
you've never run any of it under sustained real traffic — **so you mostly found the failures you
already suspected.** That last thirty seconds is what makes the previous four and a half minutes
credible.
