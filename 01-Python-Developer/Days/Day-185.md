## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Ticking a box you can't demonstrate live | A falsely ticked box is a failed follow-up, and it'll be the second one. |
| Rating yourself generously | The gap shows in two questions. A 3 you admit beats a 5 you can't defend. |
| Re-reading a weak topic | Recognition isn't recall. Rebuild it from memory. |
| A walkthrough that's all architecture | Four of ten minutes on the hard part, one on the limits. |
| Adjectives instead of numbers | "It's fast" is unfalsifiable; "p99 240 ms at 1,150 rps" is evidence. |
| A story with no failure in it | Sounds rehearsed. The recovery is the story. |
| Ending on a claim rather than a limit | A stated ceiling proves you measured. |
| Leading the comparison with differences | The identical 80% is the insight, and most candidates never notice it. |
| "Django is magic" / "FastAPI is fast" | Name mechanisms. Both are unfalsifiable and both sound junior. |
| Bluffing | "I don't know, here's how I'd find out" scores higher than a confident wrong answer. |
| Treating the gate as a formality | The fifty questions exist to name the days you must re-read. |

## Interview questions

**Q: Tell me about your backend experience.**

> I built a ticketing API end to end — designed, secured, deployed, observed and load-tested — and
> then built its hardest endpoint a second time in the other framework to compare them properly. The
> interesting problem is selling fixed inventory to a spike of concurrent buyers when the payment
> step takes ten seconds against a third party, which rules out the obvious solutions: you can't hold
> a lock across the payment call, and you can't decrement only after payment or you oversell. So
> there's a hold — a short reservation replacing a long lock — three layers stopping oversell, and a
> `payment_unknown` state with a reconciliation job, because a timeout doesn't mean the charge
> failed. Two hundred threads against ten tickets sells exactly ten, every time.

**Q: Which framework do you prefer?**

> I've shipped both, and the honest finding is that the endpoint I built in each was about eighty
> percent identical — the race, the idempotency, the transaction boundary and the dual write were
> mine either way. What differed was how much scaffolding surrounded the hard part and which wrong
> thing was the default. Django's defaults are more numerous and more silent: autocommit means my
> service isn't atomic unless I remember, `ModelViewSet` publishes a DELETE I didn't ask for, and
> `has_object_permission` doesn't run on list, so ownership in a permission class returns everyone's
> data. FastAPI's problem is the opposite — fewer wrong defaults and fewer guardrails, so no
> pagination and no auth unless you add them. So I pick from the product: model-shaped CRUD with a
> back-office and a rotating team is Django; operation-shaped, async-heavy, or a typed contract as a
> deliverable is FastAPI.

**Q: What's the limit of your system?**

> About three hundred and thirty purchases per second per ticket type, because every purchase
> serialises on one row under `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE` and the locked section is roughly three
> milliseconds. That's a design property rather than a tuning problem, so adding machines doesn't
> help — which is the useful thing to know about it. If I needed five thousand I'd shard the counter
> into buckets and pick one at random, accepting some complexity and some unfairness at the tail of a
> sellout, or move to queue-based allocation. Browsing scales horizontally and the cache does most of
> that work anyway. Separately, on the framework side, Django's async ORM is a thread-pool façade, so
> if I needed thousands of concurrent long-lived connections I'd move that capability out rather than
> rewrite.

**Q: What would you do differently?**

> Make `payment_unknown` a first-class state from day one — I added it after a timeout bit me, and
> the retrofit touched five files it shouldn't have, which is the classic cost of discovering a state
> late. Shard the inventory counter from the start if this were a real ticketing product, because
> that decision is cheap now and expensive later. Set the hold TTL to three minutes with an explicit
> extend endpoint rather than ten, because ten lets one user park inventory and my rate limit is the
> only thing stopping it. And honestly, I over-tested CRUD and under-tested failure paths, so my
> coverage number was flattering — the tests I actually rely on are the concurrency ones and the
> authorisation ones, and those came last when they should have come first.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Fifteen minutes: both walkthroughs, back to back, recorded, no notes.**

Ten minutes on the service, five on the comparison. Then listen back with a pen and mark three
things: every adjective that should have been a number, every "it just works", and every moment you
recited rather than explained. Re-record. **The gap between the two takes is exactly the material you
had not internalised — and it is the most efficient study list you will ever generate.**
