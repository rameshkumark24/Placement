# Day 340 🧩

**P-340** · 🧩 **Microservices vs monolith** — ⭐⭐ Conway's law and the distributed monolith

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: microservices are an organisational technique with technical consequences,
> not a technical technique with organisational consequences — you adopt them to let teams deploy
> without coordinating, and if your teams still have to coordinate, you have paid the entire bill and
> bought nothing.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ Conway's law, and the reversal

```
⭐⭐ CONWAY (1967): ***"ORGANISATIONS DESIGN SYSTEMS WHICH ARE
⭐   COPIES OF THEIR OWN COMMUNICATION STRUCTURES."***
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Three teams will produce three components, whether or
⭐     not three is the right number.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ A frontend team and a backend team will produce an API
⭐     that mirrors that split, ⭐⭐ EVEN WHEN THE DOMAIN DOESN'T.

⭐⭐ THE ***INVERSE CONWAY MANOEUVRE***: ⭐ deliberately shape
⭐   the org to get the architecture you want.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ WHICH IS ALSO THE WARNING: ***IF YOU IMPOSE A
⭐     SERVICE BOUNDARY THAT DOES NOT MATCH A TEAM BOUNDARY, THE
⭐     ORGANISATION WILL ERODE IT.*** ⭐ Two teams sharing one
⭐     service will make it incoherent; ⭐ one team owning six
⭐     services will merge them in practice by deploying them
⭐     together.

⭐⭐ THE PRACTICAL TEST, AND IT IS THE WHOLE DAY:
⭐   ***CAN A TEAM SHIP A CHANGE TO PRODUCTION WITHOUT ASKING
⭐      ANOTHER TEAM FOR ANYTHING?***
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ IF YES, THE BOUNDARY IS REAL. ⭐⭐ IF NO, IT IS
⭐     DECORATION, AND YOU ARE PAYING NETWORK COSTS FOR IT.
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ What you actually pay

```
⭐⭐ DAY 306 PRICED ONE IN-PROCESS CALL VS ONE NETWORK CALL.
⭐   ⭐ 100 ns vs 500,000 ns ⇒ ⭐⭐ 5,000×.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ That is the smallest of the costs.

⭐⭐ THE REAL BILL — SEVEN LINES, AND SAY THEM OUT LOUD:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ EVERY CALL CAN NOW FAIL PARTIALLY. ⭐ Timeouts,
⭐        retries, circuit breakers, idempotency — Day 339 —
⭐        ⭐⭐ FOR WHAT USED TO BE A FUNCTION CALL THAT EITHER
⭐        RAN OR RAISED.
⭐   ② ⭐⭐ TRANSACTIONS ARE GONE ACROSS THE BOUNDARY. ⭐ Sagas,
⭐        compensation, eventual consistency (Day 342).
⭐   ③ ⭐ REFACTORING ACROSS THE BOUNDARY IS NOW A MULTI-TEAM,
⭐        MULTI-DEPLOY PROJECT. ⭐⭐ MOVING A FIELD BECOMES A
⭐        QUARTER.
⭐   ④ ⭐ DEBUGGING NEEDS DISTRIBUTED TRACING TO BE MERELY
⭐        POSSIBLE (Day 338).
⭐   ⑤ ⭐ TESTING NEEDS CONTRACT TESTS, OR YOU DISCOVER
⭐        BREAKAGE IN PRODUCTION.
⭐   ⑥ ⭐ N REPOS, PIPELINES, DASHBOARDS, ON-CALL ROTAS,
⭐        DEPENDENCY UPGRADES. ⭐⭐ THE PYTHON VERSION: N
⭐        SEPARATE `requirements.txt` FILES THAT DRIFT, AND A
⭐        CVE THAT MUST BE PATCHED IN ALL OF THEM.
⭐   ⑦ ⭐⭐ LOCAL DEVELOPMENT BECOMES A PROJECT OF ITS OWN. ⭐ A
⭐        new engineer who cannot run the system on day one
⭐        will not understand it in month one.

⭐⭐ ⇒ AND THE HONEST SUMMARY: ***MICROSERVICES TRADE
⭐   IN-PROCESS COMPLEXITY, WHICH THE COMPILER AND TYPE CHECKER
⭐   HELP WITH, FOR DISTRIBUTED COMPLEXITY, WHICH NOTHING HELPS
⭐   WITH.***
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ The three honest reasons to split

```
⭐⭐ ① ***INDEPENDENT DEPLOYMENT BY INDEPENDENT TEAMS.***
⭐   ⭐ 40 engineers on one deploy pipeline is a queue, and
⭐     merge conflicts are a coordination tax.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THIS IS THE ***REAL*** REASON, AND IT IS AN
⭐     ORGANISATIONAL ONE. Below roughly 15–20 engineers it
⭐     usually does not apply.

⭐⭐ ② ***GENUINELY DIVERGENT SCALING OR RESOURCE PROFILES.***
⭐   ⭐ Video transcoding needs GPUs and 30-minute jobs; the API
⭐     needs 200 ms responses.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ AND NOTE: THIS IS OFTEN SOLVED BY A SEPARATE
⭐     ***WORKER POOL FROM THE SAME CODEBASE***, WHICH COSTS
⭐     ALMOST NOTHING. ⭐ Ask whether you need a separate
⭐     ***service*** or just a separate ***deployment***.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ In Python this is unusually cheap: same repo, same
⭐     image, different entrypoint. ⭐⭐ MOST "WE NEED A SERVICE"
⭐     IS ANSWERED BY A SECOND `command:` IN COMPOSE.

⭐⭐ ③ ***FAULT ISOLATION FOR A GENUINELY DIFFERENT RISK
⭐      PROFILE.***
⭐   ⭐ Untrusted plugin execution; a third-party integration
⭐     that leaks memory; ⭐ a component with a different
⭐     compliance boundary.

⭐⭐ AND THE REASONS THAT ARE ***NOT*** REASONS:
⭐   ⭐ ✘ "The codebase is big." ⇒ ⭐ modules (Day 306).
⭐   ⭐ ✘ "It's the modern way." ⇒ ⭐⭐ NOT AN ARGUMENT.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ "We want to use a different language." ⇒ ⭐ rarely
⭐     worth the operational cost; ⭐⭐ AND IF IT IS, IT IS
⭐     REASON ② OR ③ IN DISGUISE — say which.
⭐   ⭐ ✘ "So we can scale." ⇒ ⭐⭐ A MONOLITH SCALES
⭐     HORIZONTALLY PERFECTLY WELL. Day 324's X-axis works on
⭐     any stateless process, ⭐ and the database is the
⭐     bottleneck either way.
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ The distributed monolith, at full scale

```
⭐⭐ THE FIVE MARKERS. ⭐ Three or more means you have one:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ SERVICES MUST BE DEPLOYED IN A SPECIFIC ORDER.
⭐   ② ⭐⭐ SERVICES SHARE A DATABASE (or worse, share ***tables
⭐        with writes from both sides***).
⭐   ③ ⭐ A change to one service routinely requires a change to
⭐        another in the same sprint.
⭐   ④ ⭐ You cannot test a service without running five others.
⭐   ⑤ ⭐ One service being down makes the others return errors
⭐        rather than degrade.

⭐⭐ THE SHARED DATABASE IS THE WORST OF THESE, AND THE MOST
⭐   COMMON. ⭐ Because:
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ THE SCHEMA IS NOW A SHARED, UNVERSIONED, UNTESTED
⭐     API WITH NO OWNER. ⭐ Any service can add a column, hold
⭐     a lock, or write a row the others don't expect.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AND IT REMOVES THE ***ONLY*** BENEFIT YOU WERE BUYING:
⭐     you cannot deploy independently, because a migration
⭐     affects everyone.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ THE RULE FROM DAY 306, AT SCALE: ***ONE SERVICE OWNS A
⭐     TABLE. EVERYONE ELSE ASKS IT.*** ⭐ Enforce it with
⭐     database roles and per-service credentials, ⭐⭐ NOT WITH
⭐     A WIKI PAGE — a wiki page has never prevented a `SELECT`.

⭐⭐ AND THE SUBTLE ONE: ⭐⭐ ***SHARED LIBRARIES CREATE THE SAME
⭐   COUPLING, MORE POLITELY.*** ⭐ A `common` package that every
⭐   service imports, containing domain models — ⭐⭐ NOW A MODEL
⭐   CHANGE REQUIRES COORDINATED UPGRADES ACROSS TEN REPOS, AND
⭐   YOU HAVE A MONOLITH WITH EXTRA STEPS. ⭐ Shared libraries
⭐   should contain infrastructure (logging setup, auth
⭐   middleware), ⭐⭐ NEVER DOMAIN TYPES.
```

# Part 5 — ⭐⭐ The modular monolith, and the migration path

```
⭐⭐ THE POSITION THAT IS ALMOST ALWAYS RIGHT:
⭐   ***START WITH A MODULAR MONOLITH. EXTRACT A SERVICE WHEN
⭐      A ***SPECIFIC*** PAIN APPEARS, NAMING WHICH OF THE
⭐      THREE REASONS IT IS.***

⭐⭐ WHAT "MODULAR" MEANS CONCRETELY (Day 306, made enforceable):
⭐   ⭐ One package per bounded context.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ CROSS-MODULE ACCESS ONLY VIA `api.py` — no reaching
⭐     into another module's models or repositories.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ ONE MODULE OWNS ITS TABLES; OTHERS NEVER QUERY THEM.
⭐     ⭐ This is the rule no linter can see, so it needs a
⭐     review convention or a schema-per-module with separate
⭐     roles.
⭐   ⭐ Enforced by import-linter contracts (Day 299) in CI.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ AND THEN EXTRACTION IS MECHANICAL: `api.py` BECOMES
⭐     AN HTTP CLIENT, AND EVERYTHING ELSE IS UNCHANGED.

⭐⭐ THE EXTRACTION SEQUENCE — SIX STEPS:
⭐   ① ⭐ Make the module clean in-process first. ⭐⭐ IF YOU
⭐        CANNOT MAKE IT A CLEAN MODULE, EXTRACTING IT WILL
⭐        NOT MAKE IT CLEAN — IT WILL MAKE IT DISTRIBUTED AND
⭐        DIRTY.
⭐   ② ⭐ Split the tables it owns; break foreign keys to
⭐        others (⭐⭐ THE HARDEST STEP, AND USUALLY THE ONE
⭐        THAT REVEALS THE BOUNDARY WAS WRONG).
⭐   ③ ⭐ Put the `api.py` interface behind a feature flag that
⭐        can route in-process ***or*** over HTTP.
⭐   ④ ⭐ Deploy the new service, ⭐ route a percentage.
⭐   ⑤ ⭐ Move the data. ⭐ Dual-write, backfill, verify (Day 329).
⭐   ⑥ ⭐ Delete the in-process path.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ NOTE STEP ③: THE SAME INTERFACE, TWO TRANSPORTS. ⭐ It
⭐     is the only way to make this reversible, ⭐⭐ AND
⭐     REVERSIBILITY IS WHAT MAKES THE DECISION SAFE TO MAKE.
```

# Part 6 — ⭐ Sizing services, and the two-pizza myth

```
⭐⭐ "TWO-PIZZA TEAM" DESCRIBES A ***TEAM***, NOT A SERVICE.
⭐   ⭐ One team may own several services. ⭐⭐ SEVERAL TEAMS
⭐     MUST NEVER OWN ONE.

⭐⭐ THE SIZING HEURISTICS THAT ACTUALLY HELP:
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ A SERVICE SHOULD OWN A ***BOUNDED CONTEXT*** (Day
⭐     305), NOT AN ENTITY. ⭐ "OrderService" ✔ ·
⭐     ⭐⭐ "UserAddressService" ✘ — an entity-per-service split
⭐     guarantees chatty calls and cross-service transactions.
⭐   ⭐ It should be rewritable by its team in a few weeks.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ IT SHOULD BE POSSIBLE TO STATE ITS PURPOSE IN ONE
⭐     SENTENCE WITHOUT USING "AND".
⭐   ⭐ It should own its data and its failure.

⭐⭐ NANOSERVICES — THE OPPOSITE FAILURE:
⭐   ⭐ 200 services for 30 engineers. ⭐⭐ MORE SERVICES THAN
⭐     ENGINEERS MEANS NOBODY UNDERSTANDS ANY OF THEM, AND
⭐     EVERY FEATURE TOUCHES SIX.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Symptom: a one-field change requiring five pull
⭐     requests and a deployment order.
```

# Part 7 — ⭐ Answering it without sounding dogmatic

```
⭐⭐ THE SHAPE THAT SCORES:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ "HOW MANY ENGINEERS, IN HOW MANY TEAMS?" ⭐ Ask this
⭐      first. It is the actual variable.
⭐   ② ⭐ "What's the deploy frequency, and is coordination
⭐      currently a bottleneck?"
⭐   ③ ⭐ "Are there genuinely divergent resource profiles?"
⭐   ④ ⭐⭐ THEN RECOMMEND, WITH THE COST ATTACHED.

⭐⭐ THE ANSWER, IN ONE BREATH:
⭐   ***"Under about twenty engineers, a modular monolith with
⭐      enforced boundaries — because the benefit of
⭐      microservices is independent deployment by independent
⭐      teams, and with three teams that's not yet a
⭐      bottleneck. I'd enforce module boundaries in CI so
⭐      extraction stays cheap, and I'd extract the first
⭐      service when a specific pain appears — most likely the
⭐      transcoding pipeline, on resource-profile grounds. What
⭐      I would not do is split by entity, or let two services
⭐      share a database, because that's the distributed
⭐      monolith: all of the cost and none of the benefit."***

⭐⭐ AND IF THE INTERVIEWER PUSHES "BUT WE ALREADY HAVE 40
⭐   SERVICES AND IT'S PAINFUL":
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE ANSWER IS NOT "REWRITE AS A MONOLITH". ⭐ It is:
⭐     find the services that always deploy together and merge
⭐     those; ⭐ break shared-database access first, since it is
⭐     the marker that removes every benefit; ⭐ and ⭐⭐ MEASURE
⭐     ***DEPLOYMENT COUPLING*** — how often does a change to
⭐     service A require a change to service B in the same
⭐     week? ⭐ That number, from the git log, is your real
⭐     architecture diagram (Day 298).
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ Splitting for "scale" | A monolith scales horizontally fine. The database is the bottleneck either way |
| ⭐⭐ Two services sharing a database | The schema becomes an unversioned API with no owner, and independent deploy is gone |
| ⭐⭐ Domain models in a shared library | A model change means coordinated upgrades across ten repos. A monolith with extra steps |
| ⭐ One service per entity | Guarantees chatty calls and cross-service transactions. Split by bounded context |
| ⭐⭐ Service boundaries that don't match team boundaries | Conway's law erodes them. Two teams on one service makes it incoherent |
| ⭐ More services than engineers | Nobody understands any of them and every feature touches six |
| ⭐⭐ Extracting a module that isn't clean in-process | You get distributed *and* dirty |
| ⭐ Extraction with no in-process fallback path | Irreversible, so the decision is unsafe to make |
| ⭐⭐ Ignoring local development | An engineer who can't run the system on day one won't understand it in month one |
| ⭐ "It's the modern way" | Not an argument. Name which of the three reasons applies |
| ⭐ Answering a broken microservice estate with "rewrite as a monolith" | Merge what always deploys together and break the shared database first |

## Interview questions

**1. "Microservices or monolith?"**
> ⭐⭐ I'd ask how many engineers in how many teams, because **that's the actual variable** — the
> benefit is independent deployment by independent teams, and under about twenty engineers that isn't
> yet a bottleneck. ⭐ So: modular monolith with boundaries enforced in CI, extract when a specific pain
> appears and name which of the three reasons it is. ⭐ And I'd say what I'd refuse: splitting by
> entity, and letting two services share a database.

**2. "Explain Conway's law and why it matters here."**
> ⭐ Organisations produce systems shaped like their communication structures — three teams produce
> three components whether or not three is right. ⭐⭐ The consequence for this decision is sharp:
> **impose a service boundary that doesn't match a team boundary and the organisation will erode it.**
> Two teams sharing a service make it incoherent; one team owning six services will effectively merge
> them by always deploying them together.

**3. "What does a service split actually cost?"**
> ⭐ The 5,000× latency difference is the smallest item. ⭐⭐ The real bill: **every call can now fail
> partially, so you need timeouts, retries, breakers and idempotency for what was a function call**;
> transactions are gone across the boundary; refactoring across it becomes a multi-team quarter;
> debugging requires distributed tracing to be possible at all; testing requires contract tests; and
> you multiply repos, pipelines, rotas and dependency upgrades. ⭐ Plus local development becomes its
> own project.

**4. "What are the markers of a distributed monolith?"**
> ⭐ Ordered deploys, a shared database, changes that routinely span services in one sprint, being
> unable to test one service without five others, and one service being down causing errors rather than
> degradation. ⭐⭐ The shared database is the worst, because **the schema becomes a shared,
> unversioned, untested API with no owner — and it removes the only benefit you were buying.** ⭐ I'd
> enforce table ownership with per-service database roles rather than a wiki page.

**5. "How do you extract a service?"**
> ⭐ Make the module clean in-process first — ⭐⭐ **if you can't make it a clean module, extracting it
> won't make it clean, it'll make it distributed and dirty.** ⭐ Then split the tables it owns and break
> foreign keys, which is the hardest step and usually reveals the boundary was wrong. ⭐⭐ Then put the
> interface behind a flag that routes **in-process or over HTTP — the same interface, two transports**,
> which is what makes it reversible, and reversibility is what makes the decision safe. ⭐ Then
> percentage rollout, dual-write and backfill the data, delete the in-process path.

**6. "You inherit forty painful services. What do you do?"**
> ⭐ Not a rewrite. ⭐⭐ First I'd measure **deployment coupling from the git log — how often does a
> change to A require a change to B in the same week** — because that number is the real architecture
> diagram. ⭐ Then merge the services that always deploy together, and break shared-database access
> first, since that's the marker removing every benefit. ⭐ Consolidating a few boundaries is usually a
> quarter's work with visible payoff; a rewrite is two years with none.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes.** Frame it correctly first: **microservices are an organisational technique with
technical consequences, not the reverse** — you adopt them so teams can deploy without coordinating,
and if the teams still coordinate you've paid the whole bill and bought nothing. Give Conway's law and
its sharp consequence: **impose a boundary that doesn't match a team boundary and the organisation will
erode it.** Then price it honestly, and go past latency: **every call can fail partially, transactions
are gone, refactoring across the boundary becomes a quarter, debugging needs tracing to be possible at
all** — you're trading in-process complexity, which the type checker helps with, for distributed
complexity, which nothing helps with. Give the three real reasons — independent deployment, genuinely
divergent resource profiles, fault isolation — and note that the second is often just a second
entrypoint from the same image. Name the distributed monolith's markers and single out the worst:
**a shared database turns the schema into an unversioned API with no owner and deletes the only benefit
you were buying**, so enforce table ownership with database roles rather than a wiki page. Close on the
path: **modular monolith, boundaries enforced in CI, and extraction that keeps the same interface over
two transports so it stays reversible** — and on the measurement that beats any opinion: **deployment
coupling from the git log is your real architecture diagram.**
