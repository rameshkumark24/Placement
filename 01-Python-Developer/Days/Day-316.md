# Day 316 🎟️

**P-316** · 🎟️ **BookMyShow** — ⭐⭐ holding a seat across a payment

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** BUILD

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: the entire problem is that a seat must be reserved for someone who has
> not paid yet, across a call to a system you do not control, which can time out — so this is the
> double booking, the hold expiry job and `payment_unknown` in one question, and it is the most
> system-design-flavoured LLD problem there is.**

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ The three-state seat, and why two are not enough

```
⭐⭐ THE NAIVE MODEL: seat is FREE or BOOKED.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THEN WHAT IS IT WHILE THE USER TYPES THEIR CARD
⭐     DETAILS? ⭐ Free means someone else takes it and you sell
⭐     it twice. ⭐ Booked means an abandoned checkout removes a
⭐     seat from sale permanently.

⭐⭐ SO: ***FREE → HELD → BOOKED***, PLUS TIME.
⭐   ⭐ `HELD` carries `held_by` and `hold_expires_at`.
⭐   ⭐⭐ A HOLD IS NOT A LOCK — IT IS A ***DATA*** STATE WITH
⭐     AN EXPIRY, ⭐ which is what makes it survive a process
⭐     restart, work across replicas, and be queryable.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ And that is the single most important sentence in this
⭐     problem: ⭐⭐ CANDIDATES WHO REACH FOR A LOCK CANNOT
⭐     EXPLAIN WHAT HAPPENS WHEN THE HOLDING PROCESS DIES.

⭐⭐ THE STATE MACHINE (Day 309):
⭐   FREE ──hold──► HELD ──confirm──► BOOKED   ⭐⭐ terminal
⭐                   │
⭐                   ├──release/expire──► FREE
⭐                   └──payment_unknown──► ⭐⭐ HELD_PENDING
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THAT FOURTH STATE IS THE ONE ALMOST NOBODY DRAWS,
⭐     AND PART 5 IS ABOUT IT.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ 10–25: the hold, atomically

```sql
-- ⭐⭐ ONE STATEMENT. CHECK AND ACT AS ONE OPERATION (Day 310).
UPDATE seats
   SET status = 'held', held_by = :booking_id,
       hold_expires_at = now() + interval '8 minutes'
 WHERE show_id = :show
   AND seat_no = ANY(:seats)
   AND (status = 'free'
        OR (status = 'held' AND hold_expires_at < now()))  -- ⭐⭐ LAZY EXPIRY
RETURNING seat_no;
```

```
⭐⭐ FOUR THINGS THIS ONE STATEMENT GETS RIGHT:
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ NO APPLICATION LOCK AT ALL. Correct across any
⭐     number of replicas, and it survives a restart.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ `rowcount == len(seats)` OR NOTHING — ⭐ a partial
⭐     hold is worse than a failure, because the user is told
⭐     "3 of your 4 seats". ⭐ Wrap it in a transaction and roll
⭐     back unless every seat was taken.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ THE `hold_expires_at < now()` CLAUSE MEANS EXPIRY
⭐     IS ***LAZY***: an expired hold is reclaimed by whoever
⭐     asks next, so correctness ***never*** depends on the
⭐     sweeper job running. ⭐ The job (Day 264) is for tidiness
⭐     and for freeing seats nobody asks for — ⭐⭐ NOT FOR
⭐     CORRECTNESS, and that distinction is the answer to "what
⭐     if your cron dies?"
⭐   ⭐ `RETURNING` drives the next step (Day 264): the outcome
⭐     comes from what the write actually changed.

⭐⭐ AND THE ALTERNATIVE YOU SHOULD NAME AND REJECT:
⭐   ⭐ `SELECT … FOR UPDATE` then `UPDATE` — ⭐ correct, ⭐⭐
⭐     BUT IT HOLDS A ROW LOCK FOR THE ROUND TRIP, AND ON A
⭐     POPULAR SHOW EVERY REQUEST QUEUES BEHIND IT (Day 196).
⭐   ⭐ Redis distributed lock (Day 207) — ⭐⭐ NEEDLESS: you
⭐     have a transactional database and the invariant belongs
⭐     in it.
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ 25–35: the payment, outside the transaction

```
⭐⭐ THE THREE-TRANSACTION SHAPE (Day 256), AND IT IS THE WHOLE
⭐   ANSWER:
⭐   ⭐ TXN 1 — hold the seats, create a `booking` in `pending`,
⭐        commit. ⭐ Fast, no I/O inside.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ NO TRANSACTION — call the payment provider, with a
⭐        timeout and an idempotency key.
⭐   ⭐ TXN 2 — mark seats `booked`, booking `confirmed`, write
⭐        an outbox row for the ticket email (Day 263), commit.

⭐⭐ WHY NEVER ONE TRANSACTION AROUND ALL THREE:
⭐   ⭐ A payment call takes 200 ms–30 s. ⭐⭐ HOLDING A
⭐     TRANSACTION OPEN THAT LONG PINS A CONNECTION, BLOCKS
⭐     VACUUM (Day 194) AND, ON A POPULAR SHOW, EXHAUSTS THE
⭐     POOL WITHIN SECONDS. ⭐ The show sells out and the site
⭐     goes down, in that order.

⭐⭐ AND THE IDEMPOTENCY KEY IS NOT OPTIONAL:
⭐   ⭐ The user double-clicks; the client retries a timeout.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ WITHOUT A KEY THE PROVIDER CHARGES TWICE, AND YOU
⭐     FIND OUT FROM A CUSTOMER. ⭐ Use the booking id.
```

# Part 5 — ⭐⭐ 35–42: the timeout, which is the real question

```
⭐⭐ THE PROVIDER TIMES OUT. ***YOU DO NOT KNOW WHETHER THE
⭐   CUSTOMER WAS CHARGED.*** (Day 263.)

⭐   ⭐ ❌ TREAT AS FAILED ⇒ release the seats ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE
⭐     CHARGE LANDS, THE SEATS ARE GONE, AND YOU HAVE TAKEN
⭐     MONEY FOR NOTHING.
⭐   ⭐ ❌ TREAT AS SUCCESS ⇒ ⭐ issue tickets you were never
⭐     paid for.
⭐   ⭐ ✅ ⭐⭐ A THIRD STATE: `payment_unknown`. ⭐ Seats stay
⭐     held, ⭐⭐ THE HOLD IS ***EXTENDED*** (a normal expiry
⭐     would release seats that may already be paid for), the
⭐     user is told "we're confirming your payment", and a
⭐     reconciliation job resolves it.

⭐⭐ THE RECONCILIATION JOB (Day 263):
⭐   ⭐ query the provider by ***your*** reference for every
⭐     `payment_unknown` booking older than 2 minutes
⭐   ⭐ charged ⇒ confirm; not charged ⇒ release; still unknown
⭐     after N attempts ⇒ ⭐⭐ ESCALATE TO A HUMAN, WHICH IS A
⭐     LEGITIMATE FINAL STATE
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ AND THE PREREQUISITE THAT MAKES IT POSSIBLE AT ALL:
⭐     ***YOU MUST HAVE SENT YOUR OWN REFERENCE WITH THE
⭐     CHARGE.*** ⭐ Without it, reconciliation cannot be done
⭐     retroactively — you cannot ask "did you charge for
⭐     booking 8f3?" if the provider never knew the booking id.

⭐⭐ AND THE WEBHOOK: ⭐ the provider also calls you.
⭐   ⭐ It may arrive ⭐⭐ BEFORE YOUR HTTP RESPONSE, twice, or
⭐     out of order. ⭐ So the handler is idempotent, verifies
⭐     the signature, and ⭐⭐ ALWAYS RETURNS 2xx EVEN WHEN IT
⭐     DOES NOTHING — because a 500 makes the provider retry
⭐     forever.
```

# Part 6 — ⭐ The rest, and the extensions

```
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ THE EXPIRY JOB (Day 264): idempotent, guarded in the
⭐     `WHERE` clause, one runner via advisory lock, watermarked.
⭐     ⭐ And it is ***not*** load-bearing, thanks to lazy expiry.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ THE SEAT MAP IS A ***READ***: it does not go through
⭐     the booking aggregate (Day 301), it is cached for a few
⭐     seconds, and ⭐⭐ IT IS ALLOWED TO BE STALE — the truth is
⭐     established by the conditional UPDATE, not by what the
⭐     user saw. ⭐ Saying that out loud settles the "but two
⭐     users see the same free seat" objection cleanly.
⭐   ⭐ HIGH DEMAND ⇒ ⭐ a virtual waiting room in front, which
⭐     is a system-design answer (Stage 9), plus ⭐ per-user rate
⭐     limits (Day 314).
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ "TWO SEATS TOGETHER" ⇒ the allocation problem: find
⭐     contiguous free seats in a row. ⭐ A different algorithm,
⭐     the same atomic hold.
⭐   ⭐ CANCELLATION ⇒ ⭐ seats return to `free`, and the refund
⭐     is another outbox-driven external call with the same
⭐     three-outcome problem.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐ CLOSE: three tests (100 threads on one seat, timeout →
⭐   `payment_unknown`, duplicate webhook); one change (contiguous
⭐   seat allocation); ⭐⭐ AT 100×: a blockbuster on-sale is one
⭐   hot show row — partition by show and put the waiting room in
⭐   front.
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| A two-state seat | There's no state for "someone is typing their card details". |
| A lock instead of a hold | You can't say what happens when the holding process dies. |
| `SELECT FOR UPDATE` then `UPDATE` | Correct, but every request on a popular show queues behind it. |
| A distributed lock | Needless when you have a transactional database. |
| Allowing a partial hold | "3 of your 4 seats" is worse than a clean failure. |
| Expiry only via a cron job | Make it lazy in the `WHERE` clause so correctness never depends on the job. |
| The payment call inside the transaction | A pinned connection for up to thirty seconds; the pool dies before the show sells out. |
| No idempotency key | A double-click charges twice, and you learn from a customer. |
| Treating a timeout as failure | The charge lands, the seats are gone, you took money for nothing. |
| Treating a timeout as success | You issue tickets you were never paid for. |
| Letting a `payment_unknown` hold expire normally | You release seats that may already be paid for. |
| Not sending your own reference | Reconciliation becomes impossible after the fact. |
| A webhook handler that isn't idempotent | It can arrive before your response, twice, or out of order. |
| Returning 500 from a webhook | The provider retries forever. |
| Treating a stale seat map as a bug | It's a read; the conditional `UPDATE` establishes truth. |

## Interview questions

**Q: What's the hard part?**

> Holding a seat for someone who hasn't paid, across a call to a system I don't control that can time
> out. That forces a three-state seat — free, held, booked — because with two states there's no state
> for "this person is typing their card details": free means you sell it twice, booked means an
> abandoned checkout removes it from sale forever. And the hold is data with an expiry, not a lock,
> which is what makes it survive a process restart and work across replicas. Candidates who reach for
> a lock can't answer what happens when the holding process dies.

**Q: How do you stop two people getting the same seat?**

> One conditional `UPDATE` that sets the seats to held where they're currently free or their hold has
> expired, with `RETURNING`. The affected-row count is the answer: all of them or none, rolled back
> otherwise, because a partial hold telling someone they got three of four seats is worse than a clean
> failure. No application lock at all, so it's correct across any number of replicas and survives a
> restart. And the expired-hold clause makes expiry lazy — an expired hold is reclaimed by whoever
> asks next, so correctness never depends on the sweeper job running, which is my answer to "what if
> your cron dies?"

**Q: Where does the payment call go?**

> Outside the transaction, in a three-transaction shape: hold the seats and create a pending booking
> and commit; call the provider with a timeout and an idempotency key; then confirm the seats, mark
> the booking, and write an outbox row in a second transaction. Never one transaction around all
> three, because a payment call takes anywhere from two hundred milliseconds to thirty seconds, and
> holding a transaction open that long pins a connection and blocks vacuum — on a popular show the
> pool is exhausted within seconds, so the site goes down before the show sells out.

**Q: The payment times out. What happens?**

> You don't know whether the customer was charged, so neither of the obvious answers is safe: treating
> it as failed releases the seats and the charge still lands, so you've taken money for nothing;
> treating it as success issues tickets you were never paid for. So there's a third state,
> `payment_unknown` — seats stay held, the hold is *extended* rather than expiring normally because
> those seats may already be paid for, the user is told we're confirming, and a reconciliation job
> queries the provider by my own reference and resolves it, escalating to a human after N attempts.
> The prerequisite is that I sent my reference with the charge, or reconciliation is impossible
> retroactively.

**Q: Two users see the same seat as free.**

> That's fine, and it's a design decision rather than a defect. The seat map is a read — cached, a few
> seconds stale, not going through the booking aggregate — and truth is established by the conditional
> update, not by what the user saw. One of them gets `rowcount = 1` and the other gets zero and a
> clear "those seats just went" message. Trying to make the seat map perfectly live would cost far
> more and still wouldn't remove the race, because there's always a gap between rendering and
> clicking.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Three minutes.** Name the hard part first: **a seat must be reserved for someone who hasn't paid,
across a call to a system you don't control that can time out.** That gives three states — free, held,
booked — and the key sentence: **a hold is data with an expiry, not a lock**, so it survives a restart
and works across replicas. Then the mechanism: **one conditional `UPDATE` where the seats are free or
their hold has expired, all-or-nothing, with the rowcount as the answer** — no application lock, and
expiry is lazy so correctness never depends on the cron job. Then the payment: **three transactions,
with the provider call outside all of them**, because a thirty-second call inside a transaction pins a
connection and exhausts the pool before the show sells out — plus an idempotency key, because a
double-click otherwise charges twice. Then the real question: **a timeout means you don't know**, and
both obvious answers are wrong — one takes money for nothing, the other gives away tickets. So
`payment_unknown`, with the hold *extended* rather than expiring, and a reconciliation job that can
only work because you sent your own reference. Close on the objection: **two users seeing the same
free seat is fine** — the seat map is a cached read, and truth is established by the conditional
update.
