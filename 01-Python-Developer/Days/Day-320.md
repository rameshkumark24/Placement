# Day 320 🚕

**P-320** · 🚕 **Food delivery & cab booking** — ⭐⭐ matching supply to demand

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** BUILD

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: matching is an allocation problem where the supply can decline — a driver
> is not a parking spot, because a spot never says no — so the design is an offer with a timeout, not
> an assignment, and everything else follows from that.**

# Part 1 — ⭐ 0–5: scope

```
⭐⭐ FIVE QUESTIONS:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ "CAN A DRIVER DECLINE?" ⇒ ⭐ the whole design. If
⭐        yes, an assignment is an ***offer***, not a fact.
⭐   ② ⭐ "Match on distance, ETA, or something else?" ⇒ ⭐⭐
⭐        DISTANCE IS EASY AND WRONG — straight-line distance
⭐        ignores rivers, one-way streets and traffic.
⭐   ③ ⭐ "One rider per driver, or pooling?"
⭐   ④ ⭐ "How fresh must driver locations be?"
⭐   ⑤ "Surge pricing? Scheduled rides?"

⭐ ASSUMED: drivers can decline, ETA-based matching via a
⭐ routing service, one rider per driver, locations every 4 s,
⭐ surge out of scope.
⭐⭐ OUT: payments, routing implementation, maps, ratings, fraud.
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ The offer, not the assignment

```
⭐⭐ THE NAIVE MODEL: `ride.driver_id = best_driver.id`. DONE.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ The driver is asleep. ⭐ The driver declines. ⭐ The
⭐     driver's app is offline. ⭐⭐ AND YOUR RIDER IS WAITING
⭐     FOR SOMEBODY WHO IS NEVER COMING, WITH NO TIMEOUT AND NO
⭐     RECOVERY.

⭐⭐ THE MODEL THAT WORKS — ***AN OFFER WITH AN EXPIRY***:
⭐   ⭐ `Offer(ride_id, driver_id, expires_at)` — 15 seconds.
⭐   ⭐ ACCEPT ⇒ ride assigned, other offers cancelled.
⭐   ⭐ DECLINE or EXPIRE ⇒ ⭐ next candidate.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THIS IS DAY 316'S SEAT HOLD, WITH A HUMAN WHO CAN
⭐     SAY NO. ⭐ Same shape: a data state with an expiry, lazy
⭐     reclamation, and correctness that does not depend on a
⭐     sweeper job.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ And it is the ***third*** time this shape has appeared
⭐     (seat, ATM authorisation, offer) — ⭐⭐ SAYING THAT OUT
⭐     LOUD IS WHAT MAKES THESE LOOK LIKE ONE SKILL.

⭐⭐ THE INVARIANT: ***A DRIVER HOLDS AT MOST ONE ACTIVE OFFER
⭐   OR RIDE; A RIDE HAS AT MOST ONE ACCEPTED DRIVER.***
```

```sql
-- ⭐⭐ ACCEPTANCE IS ONE CONDITIONAL UPDATE (Day 310)
UPDATE rides SET driver_id = :driver, status = 'assigned'
 WHERE id = :ride AND status = 'searching';   -- ⭐ rowcount decides
```

```
⭐ ⭐ TWO DRIVERS ACCEPT THE SAME RIDE IN THE SAME 200 ms — ⭐
⭐   which happens constantly, because you offered to several.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ ROWCOUNT 1 WINS; THE LOSER GETS "THIS RIDE HAS BEEN
⭐     TAKEN", WHICH IS A ***UI STATE YOU MUST DESIGN***, not an
⭐     error. ⭐ Drivers experience it several times a shift.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Broadcast or sequential?

```
⭐⭐ ⓐ SEQUENTIAL — offer to the best driver, wait 15 s, then
⭐     the next.
⭐   ⭐ + at most one driver is inconvenienced per attempt
⭐   ⭐ − ⭐⭐ 15 s × 4 DECLINES = A MINUTE OF A RIDER STARING
⭐        AT A SPINNER, and they cancel at about 40 seconds.

⭐⭐ ⓑ BROADCAST — offer to the nearest N simultaneously,
⭐     first accept wins.
⭐   ⭐ + fast
⭐   ⭐ − ⭐⭐ N−1 DRIVERS ARE INTERRUPTED FOR NOTHING, AND THEY
⭐        LEARN TO IGNORE OFFERS — which degrades the whole
⭐        marketplace, not just this ride.

⭐⭐ ⓒ ***BATCHED WITH ESCALATION*** — offer to 2, then 4, then
⭐     8, with short windows.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE ANSWER, AND IT IS A ***PRODUCT*** TRADE-OFF
⭐     BETWEEN RIDER WAIT AND DRIVER ANNOYANCE — ⭐ saying that
⭐     explicitly is worth more than the algorithm, because it
⭐     shows you know the constraint is not technical.
⭐   ⭐ And it is tunable: batch size and window are config, so
⭐     you can measure and change them (Day 291's shadow mode).
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ 15–30: finding candidates

```
⭐⭐ THE NAIVE SEARCH: scan all drivers, compute distance, sort.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ 50,000 drivers per city, per request, is not viable.

⭐⭐ SPATIAL INDEXING — NAME THE OPTIONS AND PICK ONE:
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ GEOHASH / H3 CELLS — ⭐ bucket drivers by cell, query
⭐        the rider's cell and its neighbours. ⭐ Simple, works in
⭐        Redis (`GEOADD`/`GEOSEARCH`) or Postgres. ⭐⭐ THE
⭐        DEFAULT.
⭐   ⭐ QUADTREE / R-TREE — better for skewed density, more to
⭐        operate; ⭐ PostGIS gives it to you.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AND THE TRAP: A CELL BOUNDARY. ⭐ The nearest driver
⭐        may be 50 m away in the ***adjacent*** cell, so you
⭐        must always query neighbours — ⭐⭐ AND A NAIVE
⭐        SINGLE-CELL LOOKUP FAILS EXACTLY WHERE PEOPLE STAND:
⭐        AT JUNCTIONS AND BUILDING EDGES.

⭐⭐ AND THE RANKING, WHICH IS WHERE JUDGEMENT SHOWS:
⭐   ⭐ ❌ STRAIGHT-LINE DISTANCE. ⭐⭐ A DRIVER 200 m AWAY
⭐        ACROSS A RIVER IS TWELVE MINUTES AWAY. ⭐ Distance is
⭐        a candidate filter, never the ranking.
⭐   ⭐ ✅ ⭐⭐ ETA FROM A ROUTING SERVICE, computed for the
⭐        shortlist only — ⭐ routing is expensive, so filter by
⭐        cell first (cheap), then rank by ETA (expensive) on
⭐        ten candidates.
⭐   ⭐ ✅ Plus acceptance rate and idle time, ⭐ because a
⭐        marketplace has fairness requirements a pure ETA sort
⭐        does not satisfy.
```

# Part 5 — ⭐⭐ Location updates: the write problem

```
⭐⭐ 50,000 DRIVERS × ONE UPDATE EVERY 4 s = ⭐⭐ 12,500 WRITES
⭐   PER SECOND, FOREVER, PER CITY.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ Writing each to Postgres is ⭐⭐ 12,500 ROW UPDATES/s
⭐     ON ONE TABLE, WHICH IS 12,500 DEAD TUPLES/s (Day 194) —
⭐     the table bloats faster than autovacuum can clean it.

⭐⭐ THE ANSWER, AND IT IS A DATA-STORE DECISION:
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ CURRENT LOCATION LIVES IN REDIS (`GEOADD`), NOT
⭐     POSTGRES. ⭐ It is ephemeral, high-churn, and losing it
⭐     costs one update cycle.
⭐   ⭐ Postgres keeps the ***trip*** history, written once per
⭐     ride, ⭐ or a batched trace.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE PRINCIPLE: ***SEPARATE THE HIGH-CHURN EPHEMERAL
⭐     STATE FROM THE DURABLE FACT.*** ⭐ It is the same
⭐     reasoning as the seat map being a cached read (Day 316).
⭐   ⭐ And on the wire: batch and compress; ⭐ do not send an
⭐     update when stationary; ⭐⭐ AND EXPECT LOCATIONS TO BE
⭐     STALE BY DESIGN — a driver's position is always a few
⭐     seconds old, so matching must tolerate it.
```

# Part 6 — ⭐ Food delivery: what differs

```
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ THREE PARTIES, NOT TWO — customer, restaurant,
⭐     courier — ⭐ so the state machine has a second axis:
⭐     order preparation runs ***concurrently*** with courier
⭐     assignment.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ THE TIMING PROBLEM IS THE WHOLE PRODUCT: assign too
⭐     early and the courier waits at the restaurant; too late
⭐     and the food goes cold. ⭐ So matching is triggered by
⭐     ***predicted*** ready time, not by order placement —
⭐     ⭐ which makes prep-time estimation a first-class input.
⭐   ⭐ BATCHING — one courier, two nearby orders. ⭐⭐ THIS IS
⭐     AN OPTIMISATION PROBLEM (a small vehicle-routing problem)
⭐     AND IT IS SOLVED GREEDILY IN PRACTICE. ⭐ Say that rather
⭐     than pretending it is exact.
⭐   ⭐ CANCELLATION IS ASYMMETRIC: ⭐⭐ AFTER COOKING STARTS,
⭐     SOMEONE PAYS. ⭐ That is a business rule, not a technical
⭐     one, and it belongs in the state machine as an explicit
⭐     transition with a cost.
⭐ ⇒ ⭐ CLOSE: three tests (two drivers accepting simultaneously,
⭐   offer expiry escalation, cell-boundary candidate search); one
⭐   change (acceptance rate in ranking); ⭐⭐ AT 100×: matching
⭐   is per-city and shards naturally — the hard part is a single
⭐   hot cell at a stadium, which needs its own batching.
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Assigning a driver directly | A driver can decline; an assignment is an offer with an expiry. |
| No offer timeout | The rider waits for someone who is never coming. |
| Read-then-write on acceptance | Two drivers accept in the same 200 ms; use a conditional update. |
| Treating the losing accept as an error | It's a UI state drivers hit several times a shift. |
| Pure sequential offers | A minute of spinner; riders cancel at forty seconds. |
| Pure broadcast | N−1 drivers interrupted for nothing, and they learn to ignore offers. |
| Not naming the trade-off | It's rider wait versus driver annoyance — a product decision. |
| Scanning all drivers | Fifty thousand per city, per request. |
| Querying one geohash cell | The nearest driver is often just over a boundary. |
| Ranking by straight-line distance | Two hundred metres across a river is twelve minutes. |
| Routing every candidate | Filter by cell cheaply, rank the shortlist expensively. |
| Writing locations to Postgres | 12,500 dead tuples a second; the table bloats faster than vacuum. |
| Expecting fresh locations | They're seconds stale by design; matching must tolerate it. |
| Modelling delivery as two parties | Three, with preparation running concurrently with assignment. |
| Matching a courier at order time | Assign on predicted ready time or the food goes cold or the courier waits. |
| Claiming optimal batching | It's a small vehicle-routing problem, solved greedily. |

## Interview questions

**Q: How do you match a rider to a driver?**

> Not by assigning — by offering, because a driver can decline, be asleep, or be offline, and a direct
> assignment leaves the rider waiting for someone who's never coming. So it's an offer with a
> fifteen-second expiry: accept assigns the ride and cancels the other offers, decline or expiry moves
> to the next candidate. That's the same shape as holding a seat across a payment and an ATM
> authorisation — a data state with an expiry rather than a lock — and acceptance is a conditional
> update whose rowcount decides, because when you offer to several drivers two will accept within the
> same couple of hundred milliseconds. The loser gets "this ride has been taken", which is a UI state
> you design rather than an error, since drivers hit it several times a shift.

**Q: Offer to one driver or many?**

> Batched with escalation — two, then four, then eight, with short windows — and I'd name why rather
> than just picking. Pure sequential means fifteen seconds per decline, and riders cancel at around
> forty. Pure broadcast interrupts N−1 drivers for nothing, and they learn to ignore offers, which
> degrades the marketplace rather than just this ride. So the real constraint is rider wait versus
> driver annoyance, which is a product trade-off, and the batch size and window are config so they can
> be measured and tuned.

**Q: How do you find nearby drivers?**

> Geohash or H3 cells as the index — Redis `GEOSEARCH` or PostGIS — querying the rider's cell *and its
> neighbours*, because the nearest driver is frequently just over a boundary and a single-cell lookup
> fails exactly where people stand. But cells are a candidate filter, not a ranking: straight-line
> distance is easy and wrong, since a driver two hundred metres away across a river is twelve minutes
> away. So filter cheaply by cell, then rank the shortlist by ETA from a routing service, mixing in
> acceptance rate and idle time because a marketplace has fairness requirements a pure ETA sort
> doesn't meet.

**Q: Fifty thousand drivers sending locations every four seconds.**

> That's twelve and a half thousand writes a second, forever, per city — and in Postgres that's
> twelve and a half thousand dead tuples a second on one table, which bloats faster than autovacuum
> can clean. So current location goes in Redis, where it's ephemeral and losing it costs one update
> cycle, and Postgres keeps the durable trip history written once per ride. The principle is
> separating high-churn ephemeral state from the durable fact — the same reasoning as the seat map
> being a cached read. And locations are stale by design, so matching has to tolerate a position
> that's a few seconds old.

**Q: What's different about food delivery?**

> Three parties instead of two, and preparation runs concurrently with courier assignment — so the
> timing is the whole product. Assign too early and the courier waits at the restaurant; too late and
> the food is cold. That makes matching trigger on *predicted* ready time rather than order placement,
> which promotes prep-time estimation to a first-class input. Batching two nearby orders onto one
> courier is a small vehicle-routing problem solved greedily, and I'd say that rather than claim it's
> optimal. And cancellation is asymmetric: once cooking starts, somebody pays — a business rule that
> belongs in the state machine as an explicit transition with a cost.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes.** Lead with the modelling insight: **a driver is not a parking spot, because a spot
never says no** — so an assignment is an offer with an expiry, and accept, decline and timeout are all
first-class. Note it's the third appearance of hold-act-commit, after the seat and the ATM. Then
acceptance: **a conditional update whose rowcount decides, because when you offer to several drivers
two will accept within the same couple of hundred milliseconds** — and the loser gets a designed UI
state, not an error. Then the strategy question, framed as the trade-off it is: **sequential means a
minute of spinner, broadcast means N−1 drivers learn to ignore offers**, so batched escalation, and
say plainly that the constraint is rider wait versus driver annoyance rather than anything technical.
Then candidate search: **cells as a cheap filter with neighbours included, because the nearest driver
is usually just over a boundary**, and ETA rather than straight-line distance, since two hundred
metres across a river is twelve minutes. Close on the write problem — **twelve and a half thousand
location writes a second is twelve and a half thousand dead tuples a second** — so ephemeral location
in Redis, durable trips in Postgres, and matching that tolerates stale positions by design.
