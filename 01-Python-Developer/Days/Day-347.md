# Day 347 🚥

**P-347** · 🚥 **Design 3 — a distributed rate limiter as a service** — ⭐⭐ local decisions, global truth

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** BUILD

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: a rate limiter is the one component that sits on the hot path of every
> single request, so its own latency and availability budget is stricter than anything it protects —
> which forces the answer: enforce locally from a budget, reconcile globally in the background, and
> decide in advance what happens when the global half is unreachable.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐ 0–8: scope, and the constraint that dominates

```
⭐⭐ FUNCTIONAL:
⭐   ① `check(key, cost) -> allow/deny + retry_after + remaining`
⭐   ② Multiple rules per request (⭐ per-key, per-IP,
⭐        per-endpoint, per-tenant — ⭐⭐ ALL EVALUATED, MOST
⭐        RESTRICTIVE WINS).
⭐   ③ Rule configuration without a deploy.
⭐   ④ ⭐ Usage visibility for customers and support.
⭐   ⭐⭐ OUT: billing, quota purchase, per-user analytics.

⭐⭐ NON-FUNCTIONAL — ⭐⭐ AND THESE ARE UNUSUALLY BRUTAL:
⭐   ⭐ 1 M requests/s across the fleet at peak.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ ADDED LATENCY p99 < 1 ms. ⭐ It is on ***every***
⭐     request; 5 ms here is 5 ms on everything you own.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AVAILABILITY MUST EXCEED THAT OF EVERYTHING IT
⭐     PROTECTS. ⭐ A limiter at 99.9% caps your whole platform
⭐     at 99.9%.
⭐   ⭐ Accuracy: ⭐⭐ "APPROXIMATELY RIGHT" IS USUALLY FINE —
⭐     ⭐ and the exception (hard billing caps, login attempts)
⭐     is worth naming up front, because it changes the design.

⭐⭐ ⇒ THE CONSTRAINT THAT DECIDES EVERYTHING:
⭐   ***A SYNCHRONOUS NETWORK CALL PER REQUEST IS 0.5–1 ms
⭐      MINIMUM (Day 323), WHICH ALREADY SPENDS THE ENTIRE
⭐      LATENCY BUDGET AND ADDS A HARD DEPENDENCY.***
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ SO THE DESIGN CANNOT BE "CALL REDIS EVERY TIME" —
⭐     ⭐ and arriving at that by arithmetic rather than
⭐     assertion is the move that scores.
```

# Part 2 — ⭐⭐ The estimate that rules out the obvious design

```
⭐⭐ CENTRAL-REDIS OPTION:
⭐   ⭐ 1 M req/s ⇒ ⭐⭐ 1 M REDIS OPERATIONS/S, EACH A LUA
⭐     SCRIPT.
⭐   ⭐ A single Redis node does ~100k ops/s of scripted work
⭐     comfortably ⇒ ⭐ ***10+ NODES, SHARDED BY LIMIT KEY.***
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AND THE HOT KEY: ONE LARGE CUSTOMER'S KEY IS ***ONE***
⭐     SHARD. ⭐ If they send 200k req/s, that key alone exceeds
⭐     a node — ⭐⭐ AND SHARDING CANNOT HELP, BECAUSE ONE KEY IS
⭐     ONE SHARD (Day 326, Day 329).
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ SO CENTRAL COUNTING FAILS ON ***BOTH*** LATENCY AND
⭐     THE HOT KEY, AND THE HOT KEY IS THE ONE THAT HAS NO
⭐     WORKAROUND.

⭐⭐ MEMORY: 10 M distinct keys × ~100 B = ***1 GB***. ⭐ Trivial.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ SO THE PROBLEM IS NEVER STORAGE — IT IS ***OPS/S AND
⭐     COORDINATION***. ⭐ Saying that out loud reframes the
⭐     whole design correctly.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ The architecture: local decisions, global truth

```
⭐⭐ THE THREE-LAYER DESIGN:

⭐⭐ ① ***LOCAL ENFORCEMENT*** — in the app process (or the
⭐   sidecar/gateway). ⭐ An in-memory token bucket per key.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE DECISION IS A DICT LOOKUP AND SOME ARITHMETIC:
⭐     ***~1 µs, NOT 1 ms.*** ⭐ Latency problem solved by three
⭐     orders of magnitude.

⭐⭐ ② ***ASYNCHRONOUS RECONCILIATION*** — every ~100 ms, each
⭐   pod reports consumption and requests an allocation.
⭐   ⭐ "I used 340 tokens for key X in the last 100 ms; how
⭐     many may I have for the next window?"
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ N POD-TO-SERVICE CALLS PER 100 ms, ***NOT*** ONE PER
⭐     REQUEST. ⭐ At 100 pods that is 1,000 calls/s instead of
⭐     1,000,000 — ⭐⭐ A 1,000× REDUCTION, AND THAT RATIO IS
⭐     THE WHOLE ARGUMENT.

⭐⭐ ③ ***THE ALLOCATOR*** — sharded by limit key, holding the
⭐   authoritative counter, ⭐ distributing the remaining budget
⭐   across pods in proportion to recent demand.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ A POD THAT USED 30% OF THE LIMIT LAST WINDOW GETS
⭐     ~30% OF THE NEXT ALLOCATION. ⭐ Uneven traffic (Day 336's
⭐     objection to naive local limits) is handled by
⭐     ***measurement*** rather than by assumption.

⭐⭐ AND THE ACCURACY YOU ACTUALLY GET:
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ WORST-CASE OVERSHOOT ≈ ONE RECONCILIATION WINDOW OF
⭐     TRAFFIC. ⭐ At 100 ms and a 1,000/s limit, that is up to
⭐     ~100 extra requests in a burst.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ STATE THIS AS A NUMBER, NOT AS "ROUGHLY ACCURATE" —
⭐     it is the trade you are making, and quantifying it is
⭐     what makes it a decision.
```

```python
# ⭐ Local bucket — the hot path, and it must stay this small
class LocalBucket:
    __slots__ = ("tokens", "rate", "burst", "last")

    def allow(self, cost: float, now: float) -> bool:
        self.tokens = min(self.burst,                    # ⭐ lazy refill
                          self.tokens + (now - self.last) * self.rate)
        self.last = now                                  # ⭐⭐ monotonic!
        if self.tokens < cost:
            return False
        self.tokens -= cost
        return True
# ⭐⭐ NO LOCK: ONE BUCKET PER (KEY, WORKER). ⭐ In an async
# ⭐   service the loop is single-threaded between awaits, so
# ⭐   this is atomic ***as long as there is no `await` inside***
# ⭐   (Day 310). ⭐⭐ THAT IS THE ENTIRE CONCURRENCY ARGUMENT,
# ⭐   AND IT IS WHY THE HOT PATH MAKES NO I/O CALLS.
```

# Part 4 — ⭐⭐ The deep dive: what happens when the allocator is down

```
⭐⭐ THIS IS THE QUESTION THAT SEPARATES ANSWERS, AND THE GOOD
⭐   ANSWER IS ***GRADED***, NOT BINARY.

⭐⭐ THE DEGRADATION LADDER — FOUR STAGES:
⭐   ① ⭐ ALLOCATOR REACHABLE ⇒ accurate global enforcement.
⭐   ② ⭐⭐ BRIEFLY UNREACHABLE (< a few seconds) ⇒ ***KEEP
⭐      ENFORCING THE LAST ALLOCATION.*** ⭐ Behaviour is
⭐      unchanged and nobody notices. ⭐⭐ THIS IS THE PROPERTY
⭐      A CENTRAL-COUNTER DESIGN DOES NOT HAVE AT ALL, AND IT
⭐      IS THE STRONGEST ARGUMENT FOR THIS ARCHITECTURE.
⭐   ③ ⭐ LONGER OUTAGE ⇒ fall back to `limit / expected_pods`
⭐      locally. ⭐ Approximate, conservative, still bounded.
⭐   ④ ⭐ TOTAL OUTAGE, EXTENDED ⇒ ⭐⭐ THE PER-RULE POLICY
⭐      DECIDES: FAIL OPEN OR FAIL CLOSED.

⭐⭐ AND THE POLICY IS ***PER RULE***, DECIDED IN ADVANCE:
⭐   ⭐ ***FAIL OPEN*** — abuse protection on a read endpoint.
⭐     ⭐ Risk: overload. ⭐ Acceptable: yes, usually.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ ***FAIL CLOSED*** — login attempts, password reset,
⭐     OTP, payment retries, anything with a hard billing cap.
⭐     ⭐ Risk: users blocked. ⭐⭐ ACCEPTABLE, BECAUSE THE
⭐     ALTERNATIVE IS UNLIMITED CREDENTIAL STUFFING.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ A LIMITER WITH ***ONE*** GLOBAL FAILURE POLICY IS
⭐     WRONG FOR HALF ITS RULES, AND SAYING SO IS THE POINT.

⭐⭐ THE HARD-LIMIT EXCEPTION, STATED HONESTLY:
⭐   ⭐ Some limits ***cannot*** overshoot — a free-tier cap
⭐     that costs you money, or a legal cap.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐⭐ THOSE RULES OPT OUT OF LOCAL ENFORCEMENT AND TAKE A
⭐     SYNCHRONOUS CALL, ACCEPTING THE MILLISECOND. ⭐ Two
⭐     modes, chosen per rule — ⭐⭐ AND OFFERING BOTH IS BETTER
⭐     THAN DEFENDING ONE.
```

# Part 5 — ⭐ Rule evaluation, configuration and the response

```
⭐⭐ MULTIPLE RULES PER REQUEST:
⭐   ⭐ Evaluate all matching rules; ⭐⭐ DENY IF ***ANY*** DENIES;
⭐     ⭐ report the ***most restrictive*** in the headers.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AND THE ORDERING SUBTLETY: DO NOT CONSUME TOKENS
⭐     FROM RULE B IF RULE A ALREADY DENIED. ⭐ Evaluate
⭐     cheaply first, ⭐ and only commit the deduction once every
⭐     rule has allowed — ⭐⭐ OTHERWISE A DENIED REQUEST STILL
⭐     BURNS THE CUSTOMER'S QUOTA, WHICH IS A SUPPORT TICKET
⭐     THAT IS VERY HARD TO DEBUG.

⭐⭐ CONFIGURATION WITHOUT A DEPLOY:
⭐   ⭐ Rules in a store, pushed to pods, ⭐⭐ CACHED LOCALLY WITH
⭐     A LONG STALE-ON-ERROR WINDOW (Day 339's static
⭐     stability) — the config service being down must not stop
⭐     enforcement.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ VERSION EVERY RULE CHANGE AND MAKE IT AUDITABLE. ⭐ A
⭐     bad rule is an outage, and "who changed this and when" is
⭐     the first question in the incident.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ SHADOW MODE FIRST: EVALUATE AND ***LOG*** WITHOUT
⭐     ENFORCING. ⭐ Ship every new rule this way for a day and
⭐     look at who would have been blocked — ⭐ this single
⭐     practice prevents most limiter incidents.

⭐⭐ THE RESPONSE CONTRACT (Day 336):
⭐   ⭐ `429` + ⭐⭐ `Retry-After` (JITTERED) + `RateLimit-Limit /
⭐     -Remaining / -Reset`.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AND A DISTINCT ERROR CODE PER RULE TYPE — ⭐ "you hit
⭐     the per-endpoint limit" is actionable; "429" is not, and
⭐     the difference shows up as support volume.
```

# Part 6 — ⭐⭐ Where it runs, and the multi-region question

```
⭐⭐ THREE PLACEMENTS, AND YOU WANT MORE THAN ONE:
⭐   ① ⭐ CDN/EDGE — volumetric and per-IP. ⭐⭐ THE ONLY LAYER
⭐      THAT PROTECTS YOU FROM TRAFFIC THAT WOULD OTHERWISE
⭐      REACH YOUR ORIGIN AT ALL.
⭐   ② ⭐ GATEWAY — per-key quotas. ⭐ The main one.
⭐   ③ ⭐ IN-SERVICE — per-tenant, per-feature, cost-based
⭐      (Day 336). ⭐⭐ ONLY THE SERVICE KNOWS THAT THIS GraphQL
⭐      QUERY COSTS 40 UNITS.

⭐⭐ MULTI-REGION — ⭐ and this is a genuinely good follow-up:
⭐   ⭐ ❌ A single global allocator ⇒ ⭐⭐ 150 ms OF
⭐     RECONCILIATION LATENCY, so allocations are stale by more
⭐     than a window.
⭐   ⭐ ✔ ⭐⭐ ***SPLIT THE LIMIT BY REGION*** in proportion to
⭐     observed traffic, and reconcile regionally with a slow
⭐     cross-region rebalance (seconds, not milliseconds).
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ A customer whose traffic moves regions gets slightly
⭐     wrong limits for a few seconds. ⭐⭐ ACCEPTABLE, AND SAY
⭐     THE NUMBER.
⭐   ⇒ ⭐ For hard caps: ⭐⭐ ONE REGION OWNS THE COUNTER AND
⭐     OTHERS CALL IT SYNCHRONOUSLY, ACCEPTING 150 ms — which
⭐     is Day 330's PACELC choice, made explicitly and for one
⭐     rule only.
```

# Part 7 — ⭐ Bottlenecks, monitoring, and the summary

```
⭐⭐ WHAT BREAKS:
⭐   ① ⭐⭐ ALLOCATOR HOT SHARD — one enormous customer.
⭐      ⇒ ⭐ Their key gets a dedicated shard (a directory,
⭐        Day 329), ⭐ or a longer reconciliation window since
⭐        their traffic is smooth at that volume.
⭐   ② ⭐ Rule-config fan-out to thousands of pods ⇒ ⭐ push via
⭐      pub/sub with a poll fallback, ⭐⭐ AND JITTER THE POLL OR
⭐      EVERY POD FETCHES AT THE SAME SECOND.
⭐   ③ ⭐ Memory from unbounded key growth ⇒ ⭐⭐ LRU-EVICT LOCAL
⭐      BUCKETS AND EXPIRE ALLOCATOR ENTRIES, OR AN ATTACKER
⭐      CREATES 100 M DISTINCT KEYS AND YOU OOM. ⭐ That is a
⭐      real attack: the limiter's own state is user-controlled.

⭐⭐ WHAT TO MONITOR — ⭐ four things:
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ DENY RATE BY RULE. ⭐ A rule denying 40% is either
⭐     under attack or misconfigured, and you want to know which
⭐     within minutes.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ OVERSHOOT: actual vs configured, per key. ⭐ This is
⭐     your accuracy SLI, and almost nobody measures it.
⭐   ⭐ Reconciliation lag and failure rate.
⭐   ⭐ Added latency p99 — ⭐⭐ ALERT IF IT EXCEEDS 1 ms.

⭐⭐ THE SUMMARY THAT LANDS:
⭐   ***"A limiter is on the hot path of everything, so its
⭐      latency and availability budget is stricter than what it
⭐      protects. That rules out a call per request: I enforce
⭐      locally from a token budget in about a microsecond, and
⭐      reconcile with a sharded allocator every hundred
⭐      milliseconds — a thousandfold reduction in coordination.
⭐      Worst-case overshoot is one window of traffic, which I'd
⭐      quantify rather than hand-wave. If the allocator is
⭐      unreachable I keep enforcing the last allocation, then
⭐      degrade to limit-over-pods, and only then does the
⭐      per-rule fail-open-or-closed policy apply — fail open
⭐      for abuse protection, fail closed for login. And hard
⭐      caps opt out entirely and take the millisecond."***
```

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ A synchronous Redis call per request | Spends the entire latency budget and makes the limiter a hard dependency of everything |
| ⭐⭐ Ignoring the hot key | One large customer is one shard. Sharding cannot fix it, and it has no workaround |
| ⭐ Dividing the limit by pod count naively | Traffic is never even and N changes. Allocate by *measured* demand |
| ⭐⭐ One global fail-open/fail-closed policy | Wrong for half the rules. Login must fail closed; abuse protection can fail open |
| ⭐⭐ Consuming tokens from later rules after an earlier deny | The denied request burns the customer's quota — a very hard support ticket |
| ⭐ No shadow mode for new rules | A bad rule is an outage. Log-only for a day first |
| ⭐⭐ Unbounded local key state | The limiter's state is user-controlled; an attacker creates 100 M keys and you OOM |
| ⭐ `Retry-After` without jitter | Every blocked client returns in the same millisecond |
| ⭐ A single global allocator across regions | 150 ms reconciliation means allocations are stale by more than a window |
| ⭐⭐ Not measuring overshoot | Accuracy is your product. If you can't state it, you don't know it |
| ⭐ `time.time()` in the bucket | NTP steps backwards and tokens refill wrongly. Monotonic |

## Interview questions

**1. "Why not just use Redis?"**
> ⭐ Arithmetic. A million requests a second is a million scripted Redis operations a second, which is
> ten-plus nodes — ⭐⭐ and worse, **one large customer's key is one shard**, so if they send two hundred
> thousand a second that single key exceeds a node and sharding cannot help. ⭐ Plus the latency: a
> network call is half a millisecond minimum, which spends the entire budget on a component that sits
> on every request. ⭐⭐ So central counting fails on both latency and the hot key, and the hot key has
> no workaround.

**2. "So what's the architecture?"**
> ⭐⭐ **Local decisions, global truth.** ⭐ Each pod enforces from an in-memory token bucket, which is a
> dict lookup and some arithmetic — about a microsecond. ⭐ Every hundred milliseconds it reports
> consumption to a sharded allocator and receives a new budget, allocated in proportion to recent
> demand rather than assumed evenness. ⭐⭐ At a hundred pods that's a thousand coordination calls a
> second instead of a million — **a thousandfold reduction, which is the whole argument.**

**3. "How accurate is it?"**
> ⭐⭐ Worst-case overshoot is about one reconciliation window of traffic — **at a hundred milliseconds
> and a thousand-per-second limit, up to a hundred extra requests in a burst.** ⭐ I'd state that as a
> number rather than saying "approximately accurate", because it's the trade I'm making. ⭐ And I'd
> offer the escape hatch: rules that genuinely cannot overshoot — a billing cap, a legal cap — opt out
> of local enforcement and take the synchronous millisecond.

**4. "The allocator goes down. What happens?"**
> ⭐ A graded degradation, not a binary. ⭐⭐ Briefly unreachable and **pods keep enforcing their last
> allocation, so nothing changes and nobody notices — that's the property a central-counter design
> doesn't have at all.** ⭐ Longer, and they fall back to limit-over-expected-pods, which is
> conservative but bounded. ⭐⭐ Only then does the per-rule policy apply: **fail open for abuse
> protection, fail closed for login and OTP**, because the alternative there is unlimited credential
> stuffing. ⭐ A limiter with one global failure policy is wrong for half its rules.

**5. "How do you roll out a new rule safely?"**
> ⭐⭐ Shadow mode: evaluate and log without enforcing, for a day, then look at exactly who would have
> been blocked. ⭐ That one practice prevents most limiter incidents, because a bad rule is an outage
> and the blast radius is everyone. ⭐ Then version the change and make it auditable, since "who
> changed this and when" is the first question in the incident. ⭐ And the rule config caches locally
> with a long stale-on-error window, so the config service being down never stops enforcement.

**6. "What about multiple regions?"**
> ⭐ A single global allocator gives a hundred and fifty milliseconds of reconciliation latency, which
> makes every allocation stale by more than a window. ⭐⭐ So **split the limit across regions in
> proportion to observed traffic and rebalance slowly** — seconds, not milliseconds. ⭐ A customer whose
> traffic shifts regions gets slightly wrong limits for a few seconds, which I'd accept and quantify.
> ⭐⭐ For a hard cap I'd have one region own the counter and the others call it synchronously — that's
> PACELC's else-branch, chosen explicitly, for one rule.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

**Two minutes.** Lead with the constraint that decides everything: **this component sits on the hot path
of every request, so its latency and availability budget is stricter than anything it protects** — a
limiter at three nines caps your whole platform at three nines. Then rule out the obvious design by
arithmetic, not opinion: **a million requests a second is a million Redis operations a second, and one
large customer's key is one shard, so sharding cannot save you** — that's the failure with no
workaround. Then the architecture in one line: **local decisions, global truth.** Enforce from an
in-memory bucket in about a microsecond; reconcile with a sharded allocator every hundred milliseconds,
with budgets allocated by measured demand rather than assumed evenness — **a thousandfold reduction in
coordination.** Quantify the cost rather than hand-waving it: **worst-case overshoot is one window of
traffic, about a hundred extra requests at a thousand per second.** Then the question that separates
answers: **when the allocator is unreachable, pods keep enforcing their last allocation, which is the
property a central counter simply doesn't have** — then degrade to limit-over-pods, and only then does
the per-rule policy apply, fail open for abuse protection and **fail closed for login, because the
alternative is unlimited credential stuffing.** Close on operations: **shadow mode for every new rule,
because a bad rule is an outage whose blast radius is everyone** — and on measuring overshoot, because
accuracy is the product and most teams have never measured theirs.
