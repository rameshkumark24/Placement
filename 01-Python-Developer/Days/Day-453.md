# Day 453 🛰️

**P-453** · 🛰️ **Serving** — ⭐⭐ requests that last seconds break every default you have

**Time:** 3 hrs · **Mode:** NEW

> ⭐⭐ **The sentence to own: a normal web request is 50 milliseconds and an LLM request is three to
> thirty seconds, and that single fact invalidates your worker model, your autoscaling signal, your load
> balancer timeouts and your deploy procedure — all of which were tuned for a workload you no longer
> have.**

---

# Part 1 — ⭐⭐ The arithmetic that decides your architecture

```
⭐ ***A SYNCHRONOUS WORKER SERVES ONE REQUEST AT A TIME.***
⭐   ⭐ 3-second requests → ⭐⭐ 0.33 REQUESTS PER SECOND PER
⭐     WORKER.
⭐   ⭐ 4 gunicorn workers → ⭐⭐ 1.3 rps. ⭐ On a whole box.
⭐   ⭐ 100 concurrent users → ⭐⭐ A 75-SECOND QUEUE.

⭐ ***BUT THE WORKER IS NOT DOING ANYTHING.*** ⭐ It is blocked on
⭐   a socket, waiting for a provider. ⭐⭐ CPU IS AT 2%.
⭐   ⭐ ⇒ this is ***pure I/O wait***, which is exactly the case
⭐     async was built for.

⭐ ***ASYNC:*** ⭐ one process holds thousands of awaiting
⭐   connections, ⭐⭐ BECAUSE EACH ONE COSTS A COROUTINE AND A
⭐   SOCKET, NOT A THREAD.
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ 50–100× THE CAPACITY ON THE SAME HARDWARE. ⭐ Not a
⭐     tuning gain — a different order of magnitude.

⭐ ⭐⭐ AND THE RULE THAT MAKES IT REAL: ***ONE SYNCHRONOUS CALL
⭐   IN AN ASYNC HANDLER BLOCKS THE ENTIRE EVENT LOOP FOR EVERY
⭐   OTHER REQUEST.***
⭐   ⭐ a `requests.post`, a sync DB driver, a `time.sleep`, a
⭐     tokeniser on a 200k-token document, ⭐⭐ A LOCAL
⭐     SENTENCE-TRANSFORMER FORWARD PASS (DAY 452);
⭐   ⭐ ⇒ `asyncio.to_thread` for CPU-bound work, ⭐ async drivers
⭐     everywhere else, ⭐⭐ AND A LOOP-BLOCK DETECTOR IN
⭐     DEVELOPMENT (`PYTHONASYNCIODEBUG=1`) BECAUSE THIS BUG IS
⭐     INVISIBLE UNTIL LOAD.
```

# Part 2 — Bounding what you send outward

```
⭐ ***UNBOUNDED CONCURRENCY IS NOT CAPACITY, IT IS A THUNDERING
⭐   HERD WITH EXTRA STEPS.***
⭐   ⭐ 2,000 in-flight requests to a provider with a 500-rpm
⭐     limit → ⭐⭐ 429s → RETRIES → MORE 429s. ⭐ You have
⭐     converted a rate limit into an outage (Day 458).

⭐ ⇒ ***A SEMAPHORE ON THE OUTBOUND SIDE, SIZED TO YOUR ACTUAL
⭐   QUOTA***, ⭐ per model and per provider:
⭐     `sem = asyncio.Semaphore(concurrency_for(model))`
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ AND IT MUST BE ***PROCESS-AWARE***: ⭐ ten pods each
⭐     allowing 50 is 500 in flight. ⭐ Divide by replica count,
⭐     or hold the limit in Redis.

⭐ ***THEN THE QUEUE FORMS — AND YOU MUST DECIDE WHAT IT DOES.***
⭐   ⭐ Little's Law: ⭐⭐ L = λW. ⭐ At 10 rps arriving and 3s
⭐     service, you need 30 concurrent slots just to break even.
⭐     ⭐ Below that, ***the queue grows without bound*** and
⭐     latency is unbounded, not "slow".
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ A REQUEST THAT HAS WAITED 60 SECONDS IS SERVING
⭐     SOMEBODY WHO HAS ALREADY LEFT. ⭐ You are spending money
⭐     to generate an answer nobody will read.

⭐ ***THREE CONTROLS, IN ORDER:***
⭐   ⭐ ① ⭐⭐ ADMISSION CONTROL — ⭐ reject at the door with a
⭐     clear 429 and a `Retry-After`. ⭐⭐ AN EARLY, HONEST
⭐     REJECTION BEATS AN ACCEPTED REQUEST THAT TIMES OUT AFTER
⭐     30 SECONDS AND COSTS YOU A GENERATION.
⭐   ⭐ ② ***DEADLINE CHECK BEFORE THE EXPENSIVE CALL*** — ⭐ if
⭐     the client's deadline has already passed while queued,
⭐     ⭐⭐ DROP IT WITHOUT CALLING THE PROVIDER. ⭐ Free money.
⭐   ⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ LIFO UNDER OVERLOAD (DAY 413). ⭐ FIFO under
⭐     sustained overload means ***everyone*** gets a stale
⭐     answer; LIFO means recent arrivals get fast answers and
⭐     the oldest get dropped — ⭐⭐ WHICH IS THE BETTER OUTCOME,
⭐     AND IT FEELS WRONG UNTIL YOU HAVE SEEN IT.
```

# Part 3 — ⭐⭐ Streaming breaks your infrastructure

```
⭐ ① ⭐⭐ ***PROXY BUFFERING.*** ⭐ nginx buffers responses by
⭐   default, ⭐⭐ SO YOUR PERFECTLY STREAMED SSE ARRIVES AS ONE
⭐   BLOB AT THE END ⭐ and the feature silently does not exist.
⭐   ⭐ ⇒ `proxy_buffering off`, `X-Accel-Buffering: no`.
⭐   ⭐⭐ THE SYMPTOM — "STREAMING WORKS LOCALLY, NOT IN PROD" —
⭐     IS ALMOST ALWAYS THIS.

⭐ ② ⭐⭐ ***IDLE TIMEOUTS.*** ⭐ A model that thinks for 25
⭐   seconds before the first token looks ***idle*** to every
⭐   proxy in the path. ⭐ ALB idle timeout, Cloudflare's 100s,
⭐   API Gateway's 30s hard cap.
⭐   ⭐ ⇒ send a heartbeat comment (`: ping\n\n`) every 15s,
⭐     ⭐⭐ AND RAISE THE IDLE TIMEOUT ABOVE YOUR p99 — ⭐ this is
⭐     Day 388's keep-alive race in a new costume.

⭐ ③ ⭐⭐ ***DEPLOYS.*** ⭐ Draining takes as long as your longest
⭐   open stream. ⭐ A 60-second `stopTimeout` cuts off users
⭐   mid-answer (Day 390); ⭐⭐ FARGATE CAPS IT AT 120s, SO A
⭐   5-MINUTE AGENT RUN ***CANNOT*** BE DRAINED — ⭐ which is one
⭐   more argument for making it a job (Part 5).

⭐ ④ ***ERRORS AFTER 200.*** ⭐ You already sent the status line.
⭐   ⭐⭐ A FAILURE MID-STREAM MUST BE AN SSE ***EVENT***
⭐   (`event: error`), AND THE CLIENT MUST HANDLE IT — ⭐ or the
⭐   user sees a truncated answer and believes it.

⭐ ⑤ ***CANCELLATION.*** ⭐ The client disconnects. ⭐⭐ IF YOU DO
⭐   NOT NOTICE, YOU KEEP PAYING FOR TOKENS NOBODY WILL SEE —
⭐   ⭐ and with an agent, keep taking actions (Day 439). ⭐ Check
⭐   `await request.is_disconnected()` between chunks.
```

# Part 4 — Scale on the right signal

```
⭐ ⭐⭐ CPU-BASED AUTOSCALING IS ***WRONG*** FOR THIS WORKLOAD AND
⭐   IT IS THE DEFAULT EVERYWHERE.
⭐   ⭐ Your pods sit at 3% CPU while queueing 400 requests.
⭐     ⭐⭐ THE AUTOSCALER SEES A HEALTHY, IDLE FLEET AND SCALES
⭐     ***DOWN***.

⭐ ***SCALE ON WHAT IS ACTUALLY SCARCE:***
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ IN-FLIGHT REQUESTS PER POD ⭐ (the real capacity unit)
⭐   ⭐ ⭐⭐ QUEUE DEPTH / QUEUE WAIT TIME ⭐ (the leading
⭐     indicator — it moves before latency does)
⭐   ⭐ provider rate-limit headroom
⭐   ⭐ ⇒ a custom metric via KEDA or a Kubernetes external
⭐     metric. ⭐ Not CPU. ⭐ Not memory.

⭐ ***AND REMEMBER WHAT SCALING OUT DOES ***NOT*** FIX*** (Day
⭐   391): ⭐⭐ IF THE BOTTLENECK IS YOUR PROVIDER QUOTA, MORE
⭐   PODS JUST GET 429s FASTER. ⭐ Capacity planning here is
⭐   ***two*** numbers — your concurrency and your quota — and
⭐   the smaller one is your system.

⭐ ***HEALTH CHECKS:*** ⭐ do ***not*** call the model in a
⭐   liveness probe. ⭐⭐ A PROVIDER BLIP WILL RESTART YOUR ENTIRE
⭐   FLEET AT ONCE, ⭐ which converts a degraded dependency into
⭐   a self-inflicted outage. ⭐ Liveness = the process is alive.
⭐   ⭐ Readiness may consider queue depth.
```

# Part 5 — When it should not be a request at all

```
⭐ ***THE THRESHOLD IS ROUGHLY 10 SECONDS.*** ⭐ Above it, a
⭐   synchronous HTTP request is the wrong shape and every
⭐   problem above gets harder at once.

⭐ ***THE JOB PATTERN:***
⭐   ⭐ `POST /runs` → 202 + `run_id` ⭐ (returns in 20ms)
⭐   ⭐ worker consumes from a queue, ⭐ writes progress
⭐   ⭐ client subscribes by SSE ***or*** polls
⭐   ⭐ result persisted with a TTL

⭐ ⭐⭐ AND LOOK WHAT IT SOLVES FOR FREE — ⭐ all five of these
⭐   were separate problems five minutes ago:
⭐   ⭐ ① deploys drain instantly (⭐ no long-held connections);
⭐   ⭐ ② ⭐⭐ RETRY AND RESUME ALREADY EXIST (DAY 439/444);
⭐   ⭐ ③ backpressure is ***queue depth***, which is visible,
⭐     measurable and scalable-on;
⭐   ⭐ ④ ⭐⭐ THE HUMAN-APPROVAL PAUSE (DAY 440) IS JUST A JOB
⭐     STATE;
⭐   ⭐ ⑤ a lost connection does not lose the work.

⭐ ***THE COST IS UX*** — ⭐ and it is smaller than it sounds,
⭐   because you were going to stream progress anyway.
⭐   ⭐⭐ "SUBMIT AND NOTIFY" IS ALSO THE HONEST INTERFACE FOR
⭐     WORK THAT GENUINELY TAKES A MINUTE.
```

# 💬 Interview

**1. "How many concurrent users can one box handle?"**

⭐ It depends entirely on sync versus async, and the gap isn't a tuning difference. ⭐⭐ A synchronous
worker serving three-second requests does a third of a request per second, so four gunicorn workers is
1.3 rps on a whole machine — and the workers aren't doing anything, they're blocked on a socket at two
percent CPU. ⭐ It's pure I/O wait, which is exactly what async is for: one process holds thousands of
awaiting connections because each costs a coroutine and a socket rather than a thread. ⭐⭐ That's fifty
to a hundred times the capacity on the same hardware. ⭐ The rule that makes it real is that a single
synchronous call in an async handler blocks the event loop for everyone — a sync HTTP client, a sync DB
driver, or a local embedding model's forward pass. ⭐⭐ That bug is invisible until load, which is the
worst time to find it.

**2. "What happens when demand exceeds capacity?"**

⭐ You decide, or the system decides badly for you. ⭐⭐ Little's Law says at ten requests a second and
three seconds of service you need thirty concurrent slots to break even — below that the queue grows
without bound, so latency isn't "slow", it's unbounded. ⭐ And a request that's waited sixty seconds is
serving somebody who already left, so you're paying for tokens nobody will read. ⭐⭐ So: admission
control that rejects at the door with a clear 429 and a Retry-After, because an honest early rejection
beats an accepted request that times out after thirty seconds *and* costs a generation. ⭐ A deadline
check before the expensive call, so anything that expired while queued is dropped for free. ⭐⭐ And LIFO
under sustained overload — FIFO means everyone gets a stale answer, LIFO means recent arrivals get fast
ones and the oldest get dropped. ⭐ It feels wrong until you've seen it.

**3. "Streaming works locally and not in production."**

⭐ Almost always proxy buffering. ⭐⭐ nginx buffers responses by default, so a perfectly streamed SSE
arrives as one blob at the end and the feature silently doesn't exist — `proxy_buffering off` and the
`X-Accel-Buffering: no` header. ⭐ The second candidate is idle timeouts: a model that thinks for
twenty-five seconds before the first token looks *idle* to every proxy in the path, so the ALB or
Cloudflare kills it. ⭐⭐ Fix is a heartbeat comment every fifteen seconds and an idle timeout above your
p99. ⭐ It's the same shape as the classic keep-alive race — a timeout ordering problem where the
shortest timeout in the chain decides your behaviour.

**4. "How do you autoscale it?"**

⭐ Not on CPU, which is what everything defaults to. ⭐⭐ Pods sit at three percent CPU while queueing
four hundred requests, so the autoscaler sees a healthy idle fleet and scales **down** exactly when
you're drowning. ⭐ I scale on in-flight requests per pod and on queue wait time, which is the leading
indicator because it moves before latency does — a custom metric through KEDA. ⭐⭐ And I'd be clear
about what scaling doesn't fix: if the bottleneck is the provider quota, more pods just collect 429s
faster. ⭐ Capacity here is two numbers — my concurrency and my quota — and the smaller one is the
system.

**5. "Anything surprising about health checks?"**

⭐ Don't call the model in a liveness probe. ⭐⭐ A provider blip then restarts your entire fleet at once,
which converts a degraded dependency into a self-inflicted outage — and it happens right when you most
need the fleet up to serve cached and fallback responses. ⭐ Liveness means the process is alive.
⭐ Readiness can consider queue depth, so an overloaded pod stops taking new work without dying.

**6. "When would you stop using HTTP request/response?"**

⭐ Around ten seconds. ⭐⭐ Above that I make it a job: POST returns 202 and a run id in twenty
milliseconds, a worker consumes from a queue, and the client subscribes or polls. ⭐ What's striking is
how many separate problems that solves at once — deploys drain instantly because there are no long-held
connections, retry and resume already exist, backpressure becomes queue depth which is visible and
scalable-on, the human approval pause is just a job state, and a lost connection doesn't lose the work.
⭐⭐ On Fargate it's also forced: `stopTimeout` caps at 120 seconds, so a five-minute agent run literally
cannot be drained gracefully. ⭐ The cost is UX, and it's smaller than it sounds because you were going
to stream progress anyway.

## 🎙️ Articulation drill

⭐⭐ **Three minutes, out loud: "how do you serve an LLM feature at scale?"**

Open with the fact everything else follows from. **A normal web request is fifty milliseconds; an LLM
request is three to thirty seconds. That single fact invalidates the worker model, the autoscaling
signal, the load balancer timeouts and the deploy procedure — all of which were tuned for a workload
that no longer exists.**

Then the capacity arithmetic. **A synchronous worker on three-second requests does a third of a request
per second, so four workers is 1.3 rps on a whole box — while sitting at two percent CPU, because
they're blocked on a socket. It's pure I/O wait, so async gives fifty to a hundred times the capacity on
the same hardware. The rule that makes it real is that one synchronous call in an async handler blocks
the loop for everyone: a sync HTTP client, a sync database driver, a local embedding model's forward
pass. That bug is invisible until load.**

Then overload, which is where the design decisions are. **Outbound concurrency is bounded by a semaphore
sized to the actual provider quota and divided by replica count, or unbounded concurrency turns a rate
limit into an outage through retries. Then Little's Law: ten requests a second at three seconds of
service needs thirty slots to break even, and below that the queue grows without bound — latency isn't
slow, it's unbounded. So admission control rejects at the door with a Retry-After, because an honest
early rejection beats an accepted request that times out after thirty seconds and still costs a
generation. Anything whose deadline expired while queued gets dropped before the provider call. And LIFO
under sustained overload, because FIFO means everyone gets a stale answer.**

Close on the infrastructure that quietly breaks. **Streaming: nginx buffers by default, so a perfect SSE
stream arrives as one blob and the feature silently doesn't exist. A twenty-five-second wait for the
first token looks idle to every proxy in the path, so you need heartbeats and a raised idle timeout.
Deploys drain as slowly as your longest open stream, and Fargate caps stopTimeout at two minutes, so a
five-minute agent run can't be drained at all. Autoscaling goes on in-flight requests and queue depth,
never CPU — at three percent CPU with four hundred queued, a CPU autoscaler scales you down. And above
about ten seconds it shouldn't be a request at all: making it a job fixes draining, retry, resume,
backpressure and the human-approval pause in one move.**
